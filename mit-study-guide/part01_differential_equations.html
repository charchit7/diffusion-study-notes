Part 1Differential Equations

In Part 0 we built up our mathematical vocabulary: vectors, probability distributions, and the idea of a trajectory — a path a point traces through space over time. Now we ask: what governs that path? The answer is an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE), and it is the central object of this entire course. Flow matching and diffusion models are, at their heart, methods for constructing and solving ODEs.

This page builds the ODE story from scratch, requiring nothing beyond high-school calculus and the Part 0 vocabulary. By the end you will understand exactly what the MIT notes mean when they write Equations (1) and (2), Theorem 3, Example 4, and Algorithm 1 — and you will have implemented Euler integration in PyTorch.

1.0 Quick Prerequisite Recap from Part 0

Prerequisite: Trajectory and Vector Field

Two objects were defined in Part 0. We restate them here so this page is self-contained.

Trajectory \(X_t : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\). A trajectory is a function that takes a time \(t \in [0,1]\) and returns a point \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\). Think of a dot moving through \(d\)-dimensional space as time flows from 0 to 1. The subscript \(t\) is the time argument; the output is the position of the dot at that moment.

Vector field \(u_t(x) : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\). A vector field takes a position \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and a time \(t \in [0,1]\), and returns a velocity vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Imagine placing an arrow at every point in space at every moment in time; each arrow says "if you are here right now, move in this direction at this speed."

These two objects are about to be tied together by the ODE.

1.1 What Is an Ordinary Differential Equation?

1.1.1 The Core Idea in Plain Words

Intuition First

Imagine you are a tiny boat on a river. The river has currents — at every point on the surface the water is moving in some direction at some speed. You have no engine; you simply go wherever the current takes you. Your position at every moment is entirely determined by (a) where you started, and (b) the current at your present location.

An Ordinary Differential Equation is the mathematical statement: "your velocity right now equals the current at your current position." Nothing more. No mysterious symbols yet — just that one sentence.

1.1.2 The Formal Object

Let us now translate that sentence into mathematics. The key word is derivative. The derivative of position with respect to time is velocity. In symbols, if \(X_t\) is your position at time \(t\), then \(\tfrac{d}{dt} X_t\) is your velocity at time \(t\).

Before writing the equation, let's recall what \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\) means without jargon. If \(X_t\) is a smooth function of time, then:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} X_t \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{X_{t+h} - X_t}{h} \]

In words: take your position a tiny time \(h\) later, subtract your position now, divide by \(h\). As you shrink \(h\) to zero this ratio stabilises — that limit is your instantaneous velocity. This is exactly the high-school definition of a derivative; we are just applying it to a position vector instead of a scalar.

Now, the ODE says: that velocity must equal the vector field evaluated at your current position and current time:

Formal Definition — ODE with Initial Condition (MIT Notes, Eq. 1) \[ \frac{d}{dt} X_t \;=\; u_t(X_t) \tag{1a} \] \[ X_0 \;=\; x_0 \tag{1b} \]
SymbolMeaningShape / Type
\(X_t\) Position (state) of the particle at time \(t\) Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(\tfrac{d}{dt} X_t\) Instantaneous velocity of the particle at time \(t\) Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (same shape as \(X_t\))
\(u_t\) The vector field at time \(t\); it is a function \(\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) Maps a position to a velocity
\(u_t(X_t)\) The vector field evaluated at the current position \(X_t\) Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(x_0\) The starting point — where the particle begins at \(t=0\) A fixed vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(X_0 = x_0\) The initial condition; pins down which solution we want Equality of two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)

Equation (1a) says: "at every instant, the velocity of \(X_t\) must equal the vector-field arrow at position \(X_t\) and time \(t\)."
Equation (1b) says: "we start at the specific point \(x_0\)."

1.1.3 Why Did Researchers Need This?

A generative model must produce a data point \(z \sim p_\text{data}\) from noise \(X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\). A direct mapping (e.g., a single neural network \(f_\theta(z) = x\)) is possible but hard to train and hard to invert. Instead, we can build a continuous transport: let a particle travel from noise to data along a smooth path, guided by a velocity field that we learn. ODEs give us the machinery to describe that path precisely and simulate it efficiently.

Common Confusion: Which Side Has the Unknown?

In high-school algebra, an equation like \(3x + 2 = 8\) is solved for a number \(x\). In an ODE, the unknown is an entire function \(X_t\). We are looking for a function of time whose derivative at each moment equals the prescribed velocity. This is a fundamentally different kind of equation.

1.1.4 Tiny Toy Example — 1-D, Constant Field

Let \(d = 1\) (just a number line) and let the vector field be the constant \(u_t(x) = 3\) for all \(t\) and all \(x\). The ODE is:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} X_t \;=\; 3, \qquad X_0 = 2. \]

This says "always move at speed 3 to the right." You can solve it with basic integration:

\[ X_t \;=\; 2 + 3t. \]

Check: \(\tfrac{d}{dt}(2 + 3t) = 3\) ✓ and \(X_0 = 2 + 0 = 2\) ✓. At \(t = 1\) the particle has reached \(X_1 = 5\).

Mental Model — The River Current

Draw arrows everywhere on a 2-D plane. At every point, an arrow points in some direction with some magnitude (the vector field). Drop a leaf (the initial condition \(x_0\)) on the surface. The leaf drifts — always following the arrow at its current location. The curve the leaf traces is the solution trajectory \(X_t\). The ODE is the rule "follow the arrow at your current spot."

1.2 Three Equivalent Descriptions: Field, Trajectory, Flow

The MIT notes (§2.1) state that "vector fields, ODEs, and flows are, intuitively, three descriptions of the same object." Let us build each one carefully and then relate them.

1.2.1 The Vector Field \(u_t(x)\) — the Rule

The vector field is the input you specify. It tells every point in space, at every moment in time, what velocity it should carry. It is a fixed function — you choose it (or learn it with a neural network). By itself, the vector field says nothing about any particular particle's path; it is the universal velocity law.

1.2.2 The Trajectory \(X_t\) — one Path

A trajectory is a solution: you pick a starting point \(x_0\), release a particle there, and it follows the velocity law defined by the vector field. The trajectory records that particle's position at every time \(t \in [0,1]\). Change \(x_0\) and you get a different trajectory — a different path through the same vector field. So a vector field generates a whole family of trajectories, one per starting point.

1.2.3 The Flow Map \(\psi_t(x_0)\) — the Family All at Once

Instead of tracking one starting point at a time, the flow packages all starting points simultaneously. Formally:

Formal Definition — Flow Map (MIT Notes, Eq. 2) \[ \frac{d}{dt} \psi_t(x_0) \;=\; u_t\!\left(\psi_t(x_0)\right) \tag{2a} \] \[ \psi_0(x_0) \;=\; x_0 \tag{2b} \]
SymbolMeaningShape / Type
\(\psi_t\) The flow map at time \(t\); it is a function \(\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) A map from starting positions to current positions
\(\psi_t(x_0)\) Where a particle started at \(x_0\) has arrived by time \(t\) Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(\psi_0(x_0) = x_0\) At time zero, every particle is at its starting point (identity map) Equality of maps

For a fixed \(x_0\), recovering the trajectory is easy: \(X_t = \psi_t(X_0)\). The flow is just the solution operator that tells you "given where you started, here is where you are at time \(t\)."

1.2.4 How They Relate — A Summary Table

Object Input(s) Output Analogy
Vector field \(u_t(x)\) Time \(t\), position \(x\) Velocity vector The river current map
Trajectory \(X_t\) Time \(t\) (for one fixed start \(x_0\)) Current position The path of one leaf
Flow \(\psi_t(x_0)\) Start position \(x_0\), time \(t\) Current position A map from "where you launched" to "where you are"
Common Confusion: Flow vs. Trajectory

The flow \(\psi_t\) is a function of functions: for each time \(t\) it is a map \(\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) that simultaneously transports all starting points. A trajectory \(X_t\) is just one path. The flow evaluated at one starting point gives that trajectory: \(X_t = \psi_t(x_0)\). The neural network in a flow model learns \(u_t^\theta\), not \(\psi_t\) — the flow is only accessed by running the ODE forward numerically.

Where This Returns

In Part 10 (Flow Matching) the training objective will directly regress a neural network onto target velocity vectors \(u_t(x)\). The ability to then simulate \(\psi_t\) via Euler integration is exactly what Algorithm 1 (below) provides.

1.3 Existence and Uniqueness (MIT Notes, Theorem 3)

1.3.1 The Question

Before trusting any mathematical object, we should ask: does a solution even exist, and if so, is it the only one? For ODEs these questions are not trivial. Consider what it would mean if solutions were not unique: starting at the same \(x_0\) under the same vector field, two different trajectories would both "obey" the ODE. That would be physically absurd (and computationally useless — which solution do we simulate?).

1.3.2 Why Paths Cannot Cross — Intuition

Intuition: Crossing Paths Would Break the Rule

Suppose two trajectories \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) both satisfy the ODE but pass through the same point at the same time: \(X_s = Y_s\) for some time \(s\). At that moment, both particles are at the same location and must therefore follow the same velocity \(u_s(X_s) = u_s(Y_s)\). After that instant, they are indistinguishable: same position, same velocity, same future. So they cannot diverge — they must continue as one path. In other words, two paths that ever touch must be the same path.

For this "touching implies merging" argument to work, the velocity must be well-behaved: nearby points must have nearby velocities (a property called Lipschitz continuity). If the velocity could jump wildly between nearby points, you could have paths that nearly touch but then violently separate.

1.3.3 The Formal Theorem

Theorem 3 — Flow Existence and Uniqueness (MIT Notes)

If \(u : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) is continuously differentiable with a bounded derivative, then the ODE in Eq. (2) has a unique solution given by a flow \(\psi_t\). Moreover, \(\psi_t\) is a diffeomorphism for all \(t\): it is continuously differentiable, and its inverse \(\psi_t^{-1}\) also exists and is continuously differentiable.

Continuously differentiable with bounded derivative means: (1) the vector field is smooth (no jumps), and (2) the rate at which it changes from point to point is bounded (the Lipschitz condition). Neural networks always satisfy this in practice because their weight matrices are finite and their activation functions (ReLU, GELU, SiLU) have bounded derivatives on compact sets.

1.3.4 What "Diffeomorphism" Means Geometrically

A diffeomorphism is a smooth, invertible map. The flow \(\psi_t : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) "warps" space smoothly. Think of printing a grid on a rubber sheet and then deforming it: the grid lines bend but never break or cross. That is what the flow does to \(\mathbb{R}^d\). The invertibility means you can "un-warp" the space: \(\psi_t^{-1}\) takes you back. This is visualised in Figure 1 of the MIT notes, where a red square grid is smoothly deformed by the flow at three successive times.

Why Should You Care About This in Practice?

As the notes point out, Theorem 3 is "good news": since neural networks always produce continuously-differentiable vector fields with bounded weights, flows exist and are unique. You do not need to worry about ill-posed ODEs in flow-matching training. The theorem justifies the entire simulation-based sampling strategy.

1.4 Solved Example: Linear Vector Fields (MIT Notes, Example 4)

1.4.1 Scalar Case — Full Algebra

Before tackling the matrix version in the notes, let us solve the simplest possible case: \(d = 1\) (a single real number), and the vector field is \(u_t(x) = a x\) for some constant \(a \in \mathbb{R}\). The ODE is:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} X_t \;=\; a \, X_t, \qquad X_0 = x_0. \]

We want to find a function \(X_t\) such that "its derivative is \(a\) times itself." Let us guess the form \(X_t = C e^{at}\) for some constant \(C\) and verify:

Step 1 — Differentiate the guess.

\[ \frac{d}{dt} C e^{at} \;=\; C \cdot \frac{d}{dt} e^{at} \;=\; C \cdot a e^{at} \;=\; a \cdot C e^{at}. \]

So \(\tfrac{d}{dt}(C e^{at}) = a \cdot (C e^{at})\). This matches the ODE, since \(X_t = Ce^{at}\). ✓

Step 2 — Apply the initial condition to pin down \(C\).

\[ X_0 \;=\; C e^{a \cdot 0} \;=\; C \cdot 1 \;=\; C \;=\; x_0. \]

So \(C = x_0\) and the unique solution is:

\[ \boxed{X_t \;=\; x_0 \, e^{at}.} \]

Step 3 — Identify the flow.

The flow is \(\psi_t(x_0) = x_0 \, e^{at}\). Check \(\psi_0(x_0) = x_0 e^0 = x_0\) ✓.

What it means geometrically.

  • If \(a > 0\): the particle moves away from 0 exponentially (explosive growth).
  • If \(a < 0\): the particle converges toward 0 exponentially (decay — this is the case in the notes).
  • If \(a = 0\): the particle does not move at all.

1.4.2 The Notes' Version — \(u_t(x) = -\theta x\)

Example 4 in the MIT notes uses \(u_t(x) = -\theta x\) with \(\theta > 0\) (a negative scalar multiple of position). This is exactly the scalar case above with \(a = -\theta\). The flow is:

\[ \psi_t(x_0) \;=\; x_0 \, e^{-\theta t}. \]

The notes verify this by computing \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\psi_t(x_0) = -\theta \, x_0 e^{-\theta t} = -\theta \, \psi_t(x_0) = u_t(\psi_t(x_0))\) ✓. As \(t \to \infty\), \(\psi_t(x_0) \to 0\) for any starting point — every particle is pulled exponentially to 0. This is the exponential convergence visualised in Figure 3 of the notes.

1.4.3 Matrix Version (Brief)

The notes also allude to the higher-dimensional version: \(u_t(x) = Ax\) where \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\). The solution is \(X_t = e^{At} x_0\), where \(e^{At}\) is the matrix exponential:

\[ e^{At} \;=\; I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2!} + \frac{(At)^3}{3!} + \cdots \]

You do not need to compute matrix exponentials by hand in this course. The key insight is that linear vector fields have closed-form flows. For general neural-network vector fields, no such closed form exists — which is exactly why we need numerical simulation.

Where This Returns

Linear vector fields appear again in Part 7 (Continuous-Time Diffusion) when we study the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, whose drift \(-\theta x\) is exactly this linear field. The exponential decay ensures that starting distributions converge to a Gaussian prior.

1.5 Why We Almost Never Solve ODEs Exactly

The linear example above was solvable because \(u_t(x) = Ax\) has a special structure. In general, if \(u_t\) is a deep neural network — a composition of matrix multiplications and nonlinear activations — there is no algebraic trick that gives you a closed-form \(\psi_t\). The function \(u_t(x)\) can be complicated enough that "integrate the derivative" cannot be done symbolically.

This is not a failure; it is the norm in science and engineering. Most differential equations describing real physical systems (fluid dynamics, orbital mechanics, protein folding) also lack closed-form solutions. The classical response is numerical simulation: approximate the solution by taking small discrete steps forward in time.

Intuition: Stepping Forward in Time

Imagine your ODE as the river analogy again. You want to know where a leaf ends up after 1 second. You cannot compute the exact path — the currents are too complicated. But you can approximate: look at the current at the leaf's position right now, move the leaf a tiny bit in that direction for a fraction of a second, then re-read the current at the new position, move again, and so on. The smaller your time steps, the more accurate your approximation.

1.6 Euler's Method — Deriving it from First Principles

1.6.1 From the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

We want to move from \(X_t\) to \(X_{t+h}\). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) tells us that position at \(t+h\) equals position at \(t\) plus the integral of velocity:

\[ X_{t+h} \;=\; X_t + \int_t^{t+h} \frac{d}{ds} X_s \, ds \;=\; X_t + \int_t^{t+h} u_s(X_s) \, ds. \]

This is exact but useless to compute directly — the integral on the right involves \(X_s\) for all \(s \in [t, t+h]\), which is exactly what we are trying to find.

The key approximation: if \(h\) is small, the velocity \(u_s(X_s)\) does not change much over the short interval \([t, t+h]\). So we can approximate the integrand as constant, equal to its value at the left endpoint \(s = t\):

\[ \int_t^{t+h} u_s(X_s) \, ds \;\approx\; u_t(X_t) \cdot h. \]

Plugging back in:

Euler's Method Update Rule (MIT Notes, Algorithm 1) \[ X_{t+h} \;=\; X_t + h \cdot u_t(X_t) \]
SymbolMeaningShape
\(X_t\) Current position (state at time \(t\)) Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(h\) Step size \(= 1/n\); how far we advance in time each update Positive scalar
\(u_t(X_t)\) Vector field evaluated at current position and time Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(h \cdot u_t(X_t)\) Displacement: step size times velocity Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(X_{t+h}\) Approximate position one step later Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(n\) Total number of Euler steps Positive integer

1.6.2 Running the Algorithm

Starting from \(X_0 = x_0\), we take \(n\) steps of size \(h = 1/n\), visiting times \(t_0 = 0, \, t_1 = h, \, t_2 = 2h, \ldots, t_n = 1\):

\[ X_{t_1} \;=\; X_{t_0} + h \cdot u_{t_0}(X_{t_0}), \] \[ X_{t_2} \;=\; X_{t_1} + h \cdot u_{t_1}(X_{t_1}), \] \[ \vdots \] \[ X_{t_n} \;=\; X_{t_{n-1}} + h \cdot u_{t_{n-1}}(X_{t_{n-1}}). \]

The final value \(X_{t_n} = X_1\) is the approximate endpoint of the trajectory.

1.6.3 Step Size, Error, and the Trade-off

Every single Euler step makes an approximation error (called the local truncation error). For a single step of size \(h\), the local error is proportional to \(h^2\) (it shrinks quadratically as \(h \to 0\)). Across \(n = 1/h\) steps, these errors accumulate into a global error proportional to \(h\) (i.e., linear in step size). This means:

  • Halving \(h\) (doubling \(n\)) roughly halves the total error.
  • More steps = better accuracy, but more neural-network forward passes = more compute.

In flow matching and diffusion models, \(n\) is a critical hyperparameter at inference time. Typical values range from 10 to 1000 depending on the task.

Common Confusion: Bigger h is Faster but Less Accurate

With \(n = 10\) steps, each neural-net call covers a large fraction of time, so the constant-velocity approximation inside each interval is rough. With \(n = 1000\), each interval is tiny, the velocity barely changes, and the approximation is excellent — but you pay 100× as many neural-net forward passes. This trade-off drives much of the engineering in modern diffusion samplers (DDIM, DPM-Solver++, etc.).

1.6.4 A Brief Box on Higher-Order Methods

Going Further: Heun's Method and Runge–Kutta

Euler's method uses only the velocity at the left endpoint of each interval. You can do better by also peeking at the velocity near the right endpoint, then averaging. This is Heun's method (mentioned in the MIT notes right after Algorithm 1):

Step 1 — Tentative step (same as Euler): \(X'_{t+h} = X_t + h \, u_t(X_t)\)

Step 2 — Corrected step using the average velocity: \(X_{t+h} = X_t + \dfrac{h}{2}\bigl(u_t(X_t) + u_{t+h}(X'_{t+h})\bigr)\)

Heun's method has a global error proportional to \(h^2\) instead of \(h\) — much better accuracy for the same number of steps, at the cost of two neural-net evaluations per step instead of one. The famous Runge–Kutta 4 (RK4) method goes further, using four evaluations per step, achieving global error \(\propto h^4\). Modern diffusion samplers like DPM-Solver++ are higher-order methods in this family. You will encounter them in Part 6 and Part 9.

1.7 Connection to Flow and Diffusion Models

The Big Picture: Sampling = ODE Simulation

Here is the complete sampling loop in plain English, mapping directly to Algorithm 1 of the MIT notes:

  1. Train a neural network \(u_t^\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) to serve as the vector field. Its job is to predict the velocity at any position and time. Training is covered in Parts 10–11.
  2. At inference: draw noise \(X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\). This is the initial condition \(x_0\) chosen randomly.
  3. Euler-integrate: for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\), compute \(X_{t+h} = X_t + h \cdot u_t^\theta(X_t)\), advancing \(t\) by \(h = 1/n\) each step.
  4. Return \(X_1\). If the model was trained correctly, \(X_1\) is distributed like the data \(p_\text{data}\) — it is a generated image/sample.

The neural network IS the learned vector field. Sampling IS Euler integration. This is the entire generative loop. Every complexity in later parts (conditional generation, classifier-free guidance, latent diffusion) is a variation on this core.

Note a subtle but important point from the MIT notes: "although it is called a flow model, the neural network parameterises the vector field, not the flow." The flow \(\psi_t^\theta\) is only ever approximated via numerical simulation — we never have it in closed form during inference.

1.8 Worked PyTorch Example: Euler Integration of a 2-D Vector Field

We will implement Euler integration for two vector fields to build concrete intuition:

  1. A rotation field \(u(x) = (-x_2, x_1)\) — causes circular motion.
  2. A pull-to-target field \(u(x) = -\theta \, x\) — the linear decay from Example 4.

Neither depends on time (they are autonomous), which keeps the code simple. All concepts generalise directly to time-dependent neural-network vector fields. 

# ======================================================
# Part 1 — Euler Integration Demo (illustrative code)
# No training; purely shows the simulation loop.
# Teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG usage.
# ======================================================

import torch

torch.manual_seed(42)  # reproducibility; teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG

# ── 1. Define vector fields ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

# Vector field 1: Rotation field u(x) = [-x2, x1]
# If x = [a, b], then u(x) = [-b, a].
# This is perpendicular to x, so the particle orbits the origin.
# Mathematically: d/dt X_t = [-X_{t,2}, X_{t,1}]
# Exact solution: X_t = (cos(t)*x0[0] - sin(t)*x0[1],
#                        sin(t)*x0[0] + cos(t)*x0[1])
# i.e. rotation by angle t.

def rotation_field(x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    # x: shape (d,) with d=2  →  u(x): shape (2,)
    # x[0] is the x-coordinate, x[1] is the y-coordinate
    return torch.stack([-x[1], x[0]])   # [-y, x]


# Vector field 2: Linear pull-to-zero u(x) = -theta * x  (Example 4 in notes)
# Every particle decays to 0 exponentially fast.
# Exact flow: psi_t(x0) = x0 * exp(-theta * t)

THETA = 2.0   # decay rate; larger = faster convergence to 0

def pull_to_zero_field(x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    # x: shape (d,)  →  u(x): shape (d,)
    return -THETA * x


# ── 2. Euler integration function ────────────────────────────────────────────

# Implements Algorithm 1 from the MIT notes.
# Inputs:
#   x0     : starting position, shape (d,)
#   field  : callable (x: Tensor) -> Tensor, the vector field u_t(x)
#            (here time-independent, so we ignore t for simplicity)
#   n_steps: number of Euler steps (higher = more accurate)
#   T      : total time to integrate over (we go from 0 to T)
#
# Returns: trajectory as a list of (d,) tensors at each time step.

def euler_integrate(x0, field, n_steps=8, T=1.0):
    h = T / n_steps           # step size h = T/n  (from notes: h = 1/n when T=1)
    x = x0.clone()            # current state X_t; start at x0
    traj = [x.clone()]        # record the full trajectory

    for i in range(n_steps):
        t = i * h             # current time (not used by autonomous fields, shown for clarity)
        v = field(x)          # v = u_t(X_t): the velocity at current position and time
        x = x + h * v         # Euler update: X_{t+h} = X_t + h * u_t(X_t)
        traj.append(x.clone())

    return traj


# ── 3. Run on the rotation field ─────────────────────────────────────────────

x0_rot = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0])   # start at (1, 0) on the unit circle
traj_rot = euler_integrate(x0_rot, rotation_field, n_steps=8, T=1.0)

print("=== Rotation field (n=8 steps, T=1.0) ===")
print(f"  Start : {traj_rot[0].numpy()}")
# Expected start:  [1. 0.]

print(f"  t=0.25: {traj_rot[2].numpy()}")
# Expected approx: [0.5625  0.8906] (exact: [cos(0.25), sin(0.25)] ≈ [0.9689, 0.2474])
# Coarse Euler with n=8 is not very accurate for rotation — try n=100 for a tighter circle.

print(f"  End   : {traj_rot[-1].numpy()}")
# Expected approx: near [0.54,  0.84] (exact: [cos(1), sin(1)] ≈ [0.5403, 0.8415])
# With n=8 Euler drifts outward slightly — the particle spirals out.
# With n=1000 it stays very close to the exact circle.


# ── 4. Run on the pull-to-zero field (Example 4) ─────────────────────────────

x0_pull = torch.tensor([3.0, -2.0])   # start somewhere off-centre
traj_pull = euler_integrate(x0_pull, pull_to_zero_field, n_steps=8, T=1.0)

print("\n=== Pull-to-zero field (theta=2, n=8 steps, T=1.0) ===")
print(f"  Start : {traj_pull[0].numpy()}")
# Expected: [ 3. -2.]

print(f"  t=0.5 : {traj_pull[4].numpy()}")
# Expected approx: ~ [0.406, -0.270]
# Exact: x0 * exp(-2*0.5) = [3,-2]*exp(-1) ≈ [1.104, -0.736]
# Euler with n=8 overshoots slightly; closer with more steps.

print(f"  End   : {traj_pull[-1].numpy()}")

1.9 A Complete Worked Walk-Through of Algorithm 1

Let us trace Algorithm 1 from the MIT notes step by step with concrete numbers, using the pull-to-zero field with \(\theta = 2\), starting at \(x_0 = 1.0\) (scalar), and \(n = 4\) steps.

Setup: \(h = 1/4 = 0.25\), \(u_t(x) = -2x\).

Step \(i\) Time \(t\) \(X_t\) \(u_t(X_t) = -2 X_t\) \(h \cdot u_t(X_t)\) \(X_{t+h}\)
— 0.00 1.0000 — — 1.0000 (start)
1 0.00 1.0000 \(-2 \times 1.0 = -2.0\) \(0.25 \times (-2.0) = -0.5\) \(1.0 - 0.5 = 0.5\)
2 0.25 0.5000 \(-2 \times 0.5 = -1.0\) \(0.25 \times (-1.0) = -0.25\) \(0.5 - 0.25 = 0.25\)
3 0.50 0.2500 \(-2 \times 0.25 = -0.5\) \(0.25 \times (-0.5) = -0.125\) \(0.25 - 0.125 = 0.125\)
4 0.75 0.1250 \(-2 \times 0.125 = -0.25\) \(0.25 \times (-0.25) = -0.0625\) \(0.125 - 0.0625 = 0.0625\)

Euler with \(n=4\) gives \(X_1 \approx 0.0625\). The exact answer is \(x_0 e^{-2} = 1 \times e^{-2} \approx 0.1353\). The error is roughly 0.073 — quite large because \(n=4\) is very coarse. Notice that each Euler step multiplies the value by \((1 - 2h) = (1 - 0.5) = 0.5\), so after 4 steps we get \(0.5^4 = 0.0625\). The exact factor should be \(e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065\) per step, not 0.5. The gap between \(0.5\) and \(0.607\) is the source of the error.

1.10 Putting It All Together: The Flow Model Sampling Picture

We now have all the pieces to understand a flow model at the conceptual level:

  1. The vector field \(u_t^\theta(x)\) is a neural network (e.g., a U-Net or Transformer). It takes a noisy state \(x\) and a time \(t\) and predicts the velocity that should move the state toward data.
  2. At sampling time, we draw \(X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) — pure Gaussian noise. This is our initial condition.
  3. We simulate the ODE via Euler's method (or a higher-order solver): run Algorithm 1, calling the neural network \(n\) times.
  4. The output \(X_1\) is (approximately) a sample from \(p_\text{data}\).

Nothing in this process requires us to ever have the flow \(\psi_t^\theta\) in closed form. The flow is implicitly defined by the neural-network vector field and realised by the Euler loop. This is the full generative algorithm for flow models, and it is the foundation for everything that follows.

Connections to Later Parts

  • Part 8 (SDE Formulation): Adding randomness to the ODE gives an SDE (Stochastic Differential Equation). The Euler loop gains a noise injection term.
  • Part 9 (Probability Flow ODE): Every SDE has a corresponding ODE with the same marginal distributions — and that ODE is simulated with the same Euler loop.
