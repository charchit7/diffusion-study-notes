Part 2Probability & Stochastic Processes

Part 1 gave us deterministic trajectories — one starting point, one ending point, no randomness. Now we add randomness at every step of the trajectory. The result is the stochastic differential equation (SDE), which is the true mathematical home of diffusion models. This part builds every tool from scratch: random vectors, Brownian motion, the SDE itself, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and the Euler–Maruyama simulator. Every concept follows the same recipe: intuition first, then formal definition, then code.

The big picture before we start A diffusion model's forward process takes a clean image and gradually adds random noise until the image is pure Gaussian static. The reverse process removes that noise step by step to generate new images. Both directions are described by an SDE: a differential equation where every small time-step has both a deterministic drift and a random kick. By the end of this part, you will be able to read the core equation \[dX_t = f(X_t, t)\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t\] and know exactly what every symbol means, where it came from, and why it is needed.

2.1 Probability recap: random vectors, joint densities, conditional expectation

This section recaps the probability tools used throughout the MIT notes, especially Appendix A (lines 1703–1776). We cover nothing exotic — just the objects you need to read the formulas confidently.

2.1.1 Random vectors and probability density functions

Recall from Part 0 that an image is a vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\). A random vector \(X \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is a vector whose value is determined by chance — like drawing a random image from a dataset. We describe how likely each value is with a probability density function (PDF):

Definition: Probability Density Function A PDF \(p_X : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) assigns a non-negative number to every point in space. The probability that \(X\) falls in a region \(A\) is: \[P(X \in A) = \int_A p_X(x)\,dx\] The total probability over all space is exactly 1: \[\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p_X(x)\,dx = 1\]

Think of the PDF as a "height map." Where the map is tall, data points are dense; where it is flat, data points are rare. The Gaussian (normal) distribution is the most important PDF in this course:

\[\mathcal{N}(x;\,\mu,\sigma^2 I_d) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{d/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x - \mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]
SymbolMeaningShape
\(x\)the point at which we evaluate the density\((d,)\)
\(\mu\)the mean — where the bump is centered\((d,)\)
\(\sigma^2\)the variance — how spread out the bump isscalar
\(I_d\)the \(d\times d\) identity matrix — "isotropic": equal spread in all directions\((d,d)\)
\(\|x - \mu\|^2\)squared distance from \(x\) to the center \(\mu\)scalar
Intuition: the Gaussian as a bell In 1D, the Gaussian is the classic bell curve centered at \(\mu\). In 2D it is a circular mound (or an elliptical one if the spread differs by direction). In \(d\) dimensions it is a spherical cloud. The exponent \(-\|x-\mu\|^2/(2\sigma^2)\) is just saying "the density falls off as you move away from center; the rate of fall-off is controlled by \(\sigma^2\)." Large \(\sigma\): flat wide cloud. Small \(\sigma\): tall narrow spike.

The MIT notes use the shorthand \(p_t\) to mean the PDF of the random variable \(X_t\) at time \(t\). This compresses notation from \(p_{X_t}(x)\) to just \(p_t(x)\) — a convention you will see everywhere in Appendix A.

2.1.2 Joint and marginal densities

When we have two random variables \(X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^d\), their joint PDF \(p_{X,Y}(x, y)\) describes the probability of simultaneously seeing \(X = x\) and \(Y = y\). Think of it as a density over the combined space \(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\).

The marginal PDFs recover each variable's individual density by integrating out the other:

\[p_X(x) = \int p_{X,Y}(x,\,y)\,dy \qquad p_Y(y) = \int p_{X,Y}(x,\,y)\,dx\]
Marginalisation in plain English Imagine a weather dataset with pairs (temperature \(X\), humidity \(Y\)). The joint density tells you the likelihood of any combination of temperature and humidity. To find the marginal "distribution of temperature regardless of humidity," you sum up the joint density across all possible humidity values. In continuous math, "sum across all" is an integral.

This appears constantly in diffusion theory: the marginal \(p_t(x)\) of the noisy data at time \(t\) is obtained by marginalising out the initial data point \(x_0\):

\[p_t(x) = \int p_{t|0}(x \mid x_0)\,p_{\text{data}}(x_0)\,dx_0\]

where \(p_{t|0}(x \mid x_0)\) is the conditional density of \(X_t\) given we started at \(X_0 = x_0\) — which we define next.

2.1.3 Conditional densities and Bayes' rule

The conditional PDF \(p_{X|Y}(x|y)\) answers: given that \(Y = y\) already happened, what is the probability distribution of \(X\)? Formally (MIT notes Appendix A.2):

\[p_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_Y(y)}, \quad \text{whenever } p_Y(y) > 0\]
Intuition: slicing the joint density Visualise the joint density \(p_{X,Y}\) as a hilly terrain over the \((x,y)\)-plane. The conditional density \(p_{X|Y}(x|y)\) is the cross-section of that terrain along the line \(Y = y\), then re-scaled so the slice integrates to 1. You "zoom into" one row of the joint terrain.

Bayes' rule just rearranges the definition — swap which variable is conditioned on:

\[p_{Y|X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_{X|Y}(x \mid y)\, p_Y(y)}{p_X(x)}\]

This identity will be essential in Part 3 when we derive the score function, and again in Part 8 when we derive the reverse-time SDE.

2.1.4 Expectation and conditional expectation

The expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) is the weighted average of all possible values of \(X\), weighted by their probability:

\[\mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, p_X(x)\,dx\]

More generally, for any function \(f\) of \(X\), the law of the unconscious statistician (MIT notes eq. 98) says:

\[\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int f(x)\, p_X(x)\,dx\]

The conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\) is subtler. The MIT notes (Appendix A.2) distinguish two related but different objects carefully:

Two flavours of conditional expectation 1. \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\): a function from \(\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\). You plug in a specific value \(y\) and get a vector back: \[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] = \int x\, p_{X|Y}(x \mid y)\,dx\] 2. \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\): a random variable. It is the function above, composed with the random variable \(Y\). Its value is uncertain because \(Y\) is uncertain.
Intuition: the best guess \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\) is formally defined as the "best approximating function \(g^\star(Y)\) to \(X\) in the least-squares sense," meaning it minimises \(\mathbb{E}[\|X - g(Y)\|^2]\) over all functions \(g\). Think of it as: given what you know about \(Y\), what is your best guess for \(X\)? This is literally the definition the MIT notes use (eq. 103). In diffusion, this appears as the optimal denoiser: the best prediction of the clean image \(x_0\) given the noisy image \(x_t\).

The tower property (MIT notes eq. 106) is a calculation shortcut that will save us many lines of algebra later:

\[\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\right]\]

In words: to find the unconditional expectation of \(X\), you can first condition on \(Y\), compute the inner expectation (which is a function of \(Y\)), and then average over \(Y\). The MIT notes verify this from the definitions (eq. 107); we will take it as a tool we can use freely.

2.2 Stochastic processes: random trajectories

In Part 1, a trajectory was a deterministic curve \((X_t)_{0 \leq t \leq 1}\) — one fixed path through \(\mathbb{R}^d\) determined entirely by the starting point \(X_0 = x_0\). If you run the same ODE from the same starting point twice, you get the identical path.

A stochastic process is different: it is a family of random variables indexed by time. For every fixed time \(t\), \(X_t\) is a random variable. If you simulate the process twice from the same starting point, you get different paths because randomness is baked into the dynamics.

Key contrast: deterministic vs stochastic trajectory ODE trajectory (Part 1): \(X_t = \Phi_t(x_0)\) where \(\Phi_t\) is the flow map. Given \(x_0\), the future is determined.

SDE trajectory (this part): \(X_t\) is a random variable even if you fix \(X_0 = x_0\). Two runs give two different sample paths. Only the distribution of \(X_t\) is determined; no single path is.

The MIT notes (Section 2.2, opening paragraph) state this precisely: "When we simulate the same stochastic process twice, we might get different outcomes because the dynamics are designed to be random."

Intuitively, imagine watching a pollen grain suspended in water under a microscope. The grain jiggles unpredictably because water molecules are constantly and randomly bumping into it. The grain's position \(X_t\) at time \(t\) is a random variable — different every time you watch it. The entire sequence of positions \((X_t)_{0 \leq t \leq 1}\) is a stochastic process, and it turns out to follow (approximately) Brownian motion.

2.3 Brownian motion (the Wiener process)

2.3.1 Building intuition: the random walk

Before defining Brownian motion rigorously, let's build it from something familiar: a random walk.

Suppose you stand at position 0 on a number line. Every second, you flip a fair coin: heads = step right by \(+1\), tails = step left by \(-1\). After \(n\) steps your position is \(S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} Z_k\) where each \(Z_k \in \{+1, -1\}\) independently with equal probability.

From random walk to Brownian motion Now imagine making the steps tinier and more frequent: instead of big steps every second, take steps of size \(\sqrt{h}\) every \(h\) seconds (where \(h \to 0\)). The \(\sqrt{h}\) scaling (not \(h\)!) is crucial — it keeps the variance finite even as the step size shrinks to zero.

In the limit \(h \to 0\), you get a continuous path that is infinitely jagged. This limiting object is Brownian motion, also called the Wiener process \(W_t\). The MIT notes describe it as "a continuous random walk" (Section 2.2).

Why \(\sqrt{h}\) and not \(h\)? If you take \(n = t/h\) steps of size \(h\), the total variance after time \(t\) is \(n \cdot h^2 = t \cdot h \to 0\) (collapses to zero). But with step size \(\sqrt{h}\), total variance is \(n \cdot h = t \cdot 1 = t\) — it stays equal to \(t\) regardless of step size. Variance growing linearly in time is the correct physical behaviour of diffusion.

2.3.2 Formal definition of Brownian motion

The MIT notes (Section 2.2) define Brownian motion through four properties. Here is each property, its formal statement, and its plain meaning:

Definition: Brownian motion (Wiener process) A Brownian motion \(W = (W_t)_{0 \leq t \leq 1}\) is a stochastic process satisfying:

Property 1 — Starts at zero: \[W_0 = 0\] The process is initialised at the origin. (You can always translate later.)

Property 2 — Gaussian increments: \[W_t - W_s \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, (t-s)\,I_d\right) \quad \text{for all } 0 \leq s < t\] The displacement over any interval \([s, t]\) is a zero-mean Gaussian whose variance is exactly the length of the interval. Longer wait = more spread.

Property 3 — Independent increments: For any \(0 \leq t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n\), the increments \(W_{t_1} - W_{t_0},\; W_{t_2} - W_{t_1},\; \ldots\) are mutually independent random variables. What happened in the last interval tells you nothing about what will happen in the next.

Property 4 — Continuous paths: The sample paths \(t \mapsto W_t\) are continuous functions (you can draw them without lifting your pen — though you would never stop drawing, as the paths have infinite length).
SymbolMeaningShape
\(W_t\)position of the Brownian motion at time \(t\)\((d,)\)
\(W_t - W_s\)displacement (increment) over the interval \([s,t]\)\((d,)\)
\(t - s\)length of the time interval — also the variance of the incrementscalar
\(I_d\)\(d\times d\) identity — the noise is spherically symmetric (isotropic)\((d,d)\)

2.3.3 Why variance grows linearly in time

Recall from Part 0 a key property of Gaussians: variances of independent Gaussians add when you sum the variables. The Brownian motion increment \(W_t - W_0 = (W_t - W_{t/2}) + (W_{t/2} - W_0)\). These two half-interval increments are independent (by Property 3), and each has variance \(t/2\). So the total variance is \(t/2 + t/2 = t\). More generally, if you subdivide \([0, t]\) into \(n\) equal pieces of length \(t/n\), each piece contributes variance \(t/n\), and since there are \(n\) pieces, the total variance is \(n \cdot (t/n) = t\). Variance grows linearly in time: \(\text{Var}(W_t) = t\).

Mental model: uncertainty accumulates Think of time as the number of "mystery ingredients" being stirred into a soup. Each second of time is one more random stir. The total uncertainty in the soup's flavour grows with the number of stirs — linearly. The Brownian motion is simply the formalisation of this accumulated uncertainty.

2.3.4 Simulating Brownian motion

The MIT notes (eq. 5 in Section 2.2) give a direct simulation recipe: set \(W_0 = 0\), then for each step of size \(h > 0\):

\[W_{t+h} = W_t + \sqrt{h} \cdot \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\]

This matches Property 2 exactly: the increment \(W_{t+h} - W_t = \sqrt{h}\,\varepsilon_t\) is Gaussian with variance \(h \cdot I_d\), which equals the interval length \(h\). The square root ensures the variance scales correctly.

2.4 The differential \(dW_t\): a tiny Gaussian kick

Before writing SDEs, we need to understand what \(dW_t\) means heuristically. In ordinary calculus, \(dt\) is an "infinitesimally small" time step. Similarly, \(dW_t\) is an "infinitesimally small" Brownian increment over the interval \([t, t+dt]\):

\[dW_t = W_{t+dt} - W_t \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0,\, dt \cdot I_d)\]

This means \(dW_t\) is a random Gaussian vector with mean zero and variance equal to the (infinitesimal) time step \(dt\).

WARNING — The #1 confusion in stochastic calculus The standard deviation of \(dW_t\) is \(\sqrt{dt}\), NOT \(dt\).

\[\text{std}(dW_t) = \sqrt{dt}, \qquad \text{Var}(dW_t) = dt\]
This matters enormously for simulation. When you take a step of size \(h\), the noise you add must be scaled by \(\sqrt{h}\), not \(h\). Using \(h\) instead of \(\sqrt{h}\) would make the noise collapse to zero as \(h \to 0\), which would give you an ODE, not an SDE. Using \(h^2\) would also be wrong. Only \(\sqrt{h}\) preserves the correct diffusion behaviour. You will see \(\sqrt{h}\) in every SDE solver, including Algorithm 2 in the MIT notes.

The heuristic \(dW_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt)\) is often summarised by saying \((dW_t)^2 \approx dt\). This second-order term is what makes stochastic calculus different from ordinary calculus — and it is the origin of Ito's lemma (Section 2.7 below).

2.5 Stochastic differential equations (SDEs)

2.5.1 From ODE to SDE: adding random kicks

Recall from Part 1 that an ODE trajectory satisfies:

\[X_{t+h} = X_t + h \cdot f(X_t, t) + R_t(h)\]

where \(R_t(h)\) vanishes as \(h \to 0\). In differential notation this is \(dX_t = f(X_t, t)\,dt\). To make this stochastic, the MIT notes (Section 2.2) amend the update to:

\[X_{t+h} = X_t + h \cdot f(X_t, t) + \sigma(t) \cdot \sqrt{h} \cdot \varepsilon_t + R_t(h)\]

where \(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) is a fresh random vector at each step and \(\sigma(t) \geq 0\) is a scalar called the diffusion coefficient. As \(h \to 0\), the remainder term \(R_t(h)\) vanishes. In differential notation, this becomes the SDE:

Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) \[dX_t = \underbrace{f(X_t, t)\,dt}_{\text{drift}} + \underbrace{\sigma(t)\,dW_t}_{\text{diffusion}}\]
where \((W_t)\) is a Brownian motion and the equation is shorthand for the integral form \[X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t f(X_s, s)\,ds + \int_0^t \sigma(s)\,dW_s.\]
SymbolMeaningShape
\(X_t\)the random state at time \(t\)\((d,)\)
\(f(X_t, t)\)the drift: a deterministic vector field that "pulls" the state\((d,)\)
\(dt\)an infinitesimal time stepscalar
\(\sigma(t)\)the diffusion coefficient: controls how much noise is addedscalar
\(dW_t\)an infinitesimal Gaussian kick, \(\sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt\cdot I_d)\)\((d,)\)
Intuition: drift + noise Think of the SDE as a river (the drift \(f\,dt\)) with a storm blowing (the diffusion \(\sigma\,dW_t\)). The drift tells you which direction the current flows and how fast. The diffusion adds an unpredictable gust at every instant. The particle's actual path is a combination of following the river and being blown by the storm. Different runs produce different paths because the gusts are random. However, if you average over many runs, the average path follows the drift (since the gusts average to zero).
Contrast with the ODE from Part 1 An ODE is \(dX_t = f(X_t, t)\,dt\) — no noise term. Setting \(\sigma(t) = 0\) in the SDE recovers the ODE exactly. The MIT notes explicitly state: "every ODE is also an SDE — simply with a vanishing diffusion coefficient \(\sigma_t = 0\)." The SDE is a strict generalisation.

2.5.2 Why diffusion models need SDEs

In Part 1, a flow model used an ODE to transport samples from a simple distribution to the data distribution. A flow already generates diverse samples — the diversity comes from the random draw of \(X_0\). What an SDE with nonzero \(\sigma(t)\) adds is fresh Gaussian noise along the trajectory, injected at every time step. This noise:

  • Gives the model a richer family of dynamics to learn from.
  • Allows the forward process to destroy structure in data (turn images into noise) purely by diffusing.
  • Enables the reverse process to generate new, diverse samples by denoising a fresh noise draw — not by inverting a deterministic map.

2.5.3 Theorem 5: SDE solutions exist and are unique

Just as we needed the ODE existence theorem in Part 1 to trust that the flow exists, we need the analogous result for SDEs. The MIT notes state it as Theorem 5 (Section 2.2):

Theorem 5 — SDE Solution Existence and Uniqueness (MIT notes, Section 2.2) If the drift \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) is continuously differentiable with bounded derivative, and the diffusion coefficient \(\sigma(t)\) is continuous, then the SDE \[dX_t = f(X_t, t)\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t\] has a solution given by a unique stochastic process \((X_t)_{0 \leq t \leq 1}\).

The conditions are mild — essentially "no blowup." The MIT notes note that a rigorous proof would require several lectures of stochastic calculus (constructing Brownian motion from first principles, building the Ito integral). For our purposes, we take this theorem as the guarantee that the processes we write down are well-defined, and focus on using them.

2.6 The Euler–Maruyama method (Algorithm 2)

Theorem 5 guarantees a solution exists. But to actually use an SDE in a computer program, we need to simulate it numerically. The simplest such scheme is the Euler–Maruyama method, which is to SDEs exactly what Euler's method (Part 1) is to ODEs.

2.6.1 Derivation

Recall Euler's method for an ODE: approximate the integral \(\int_t^{t+h} f(X_s, s)\,ds \approx h \cdot f(X_t, t)\). For the SDE, we do the same for the drift, and we approximate the stochastic integral \(\int_t^{t+h} \sigma(s)\,dW_s \approx \sigma(t)\,(W_{t+h} - W_t)\). Since \(W_{t+h} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, h\,I_d)\), we can write it as \(\sqrt{h}\,\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\). Putting it together:

Euler–Maruyama update (MIT notes Algorithm 2, eq. 6) \[X_{t+h} = X_t + h \cdot f(X_t, t) + \sigma(t) \cdot \sqrt{h} \cdot \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\]
where \(h = 1/n\) is the step size and we repeat for \(n\) steps from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 1\).

The MIT notes present this as Algorithm 2 (Sampling from a Diffusion Model). The full algorithm:

  1. Set step size \(h = 1/n\), initialise \(t = 0\).
  2. Draw \(X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}}\) (e.g. a standard Gaussian).
  3. For \(i = 1, \ldots, n\):
    1. Draw \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\).
    2. Set \(X_{t+h} = X_t + h \cdot f^\theta_t(X_t) + \sigma(t)\sqrt{h}\,\varepsilon\).
    3. Advance \(t \leftarrow t + h\).
  4. Return \(X_1\) as the generated sample.
The sqrt(h) is not optional In the Euler–Maruyama update, the noise term is \(\sigma(t)\,\sqrt{h}\,\varepsilon\). This is because the Brownian increment \(W_{t+h} - W_t\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt{h}\), not \(h\). The drift term scales like \(h\) (first order in the step size). The diffusion term scales like \(\sqrt{h}\) (half order). This asymmetry — drift is \(O(h)\), noise is \(O(\sqrt{h})\) — is a fundamental feature of stochastic calculus. The noise always dominates the drift at small scales.

2.7 The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Example 6)

2.7.1 Definition and physical intuition

The most important SDE in diffusion modelling is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process, which the MIT notes introduce as Example 6 (Section 2.2). It uses a constant diffusion coefficient \(\sigma(t) = \sigma \geq 0\) and a linear drift \(f(X_t, t) = -\theta X_t\) for some constant \(\theta > 0\):

Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE (MIT notes Example 6, eq. 8) \[dX_t = -\theta\, X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\]
The drift is \(f(X_t, t) = -\theta X_t\) (linear in \(X_t\), no explicit time dependence). The diffusion is \(\sigma\) (constant).
SymbolMeaningTypical value
\(\theta > 0\)the spring constant — how strongly the drift pulls toward zero0.25 in MIT Figure 3
\(\sigma \geq 0\)the noise amplitude — how vigorously the Brownian kicks perturb the particlevaried in Figure 3
\(-\theta X_t\)a vector pointing from \(X_t\) back toward \(0\) — restoring force\((d,)\)
Intuition: a spring in a thermal bath Imagine a bead attached to a spring. The spring always pulls the bead back toward its resting position (origin). That is the drift \(-\theta X_t\): the further you are from zero, the harder the spring pulls. At the same time, the surrounding fluid bombards the bead with random thermal kicks — that is the Brownian term \(\sigma\,dW_t\).

Two forces compete: the spring pulling toward order (zero), and the random kicks pushing toward disorder (spread). They reach equilibrium when the spread from the kicks equals the compression from the spring. That equilibrium is a Gaussian.

The MIT notes state: "This process converges towards a Gaussian distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma^2/(2\theta))\) if we simulate it for \(t \to \infty\)."

2.7.2 Computing the mean: \(\mathbb{E}[X_t | X_0]\)

Let us find the conditional mean of the OU process. We want \(m(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t \mid X_0 = x_0]\). Taking expectations of the SDE and using \(\mathbb{E}[dW_t] = 0\):

\[\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid X_0] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{dX_t}{dt}\bigg| X_0\right] = -\theta\,\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid X_0] = -\theta\, m(t)\]

This is an ordinary differential equation (an ODE from Part 1!) for \(m(t)\): \(\dot{m} = -\theta\,m\). With initial condition \(m(0) = x_0\), the solution is:

\[\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid X_0 = x_0] = x_0\, e^{-\theta t}\]
Intuition: exponential mean reversion The mean decays exponentially toward zero. After time \(t = 1/\theta\) (one "time constant"), the mean has dropped to \(x_0/e \approx 0.37\, x_0\). After \(t = 5/\theta\), it is essentially zero: \(e^{-5} \approx 0.007\). The parameter \(\theta\) controls how quickly the process "forgets" its starting point. Large \(\theta\) = fast forgetting; small \(\theta\) = slow forgetting.

2.7.3 The stationary distribution

As \(t \to \infty\), the mean \(x_0 e^{-\theta t} \to 0\). The variance converges to a constant value that balances the spring with the noise. The stationary distribution (reached in the long run) is:

\[X_\infty \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\right)\]

This is the equilibrium between noise injection (proportional to \(\sigma^2\)) and mean reversion (proportional to \(\theta\)). Large \(\sigma\) makes the stationary distribution broader; large \(\theta\) makes it narrower.

2.7.4 Why the OU process is central to diffusion models

Connection to diffusion models The OU process is the prototypical forward noising process in diffusion models. Here is the key insight: regardless of where you initialise \(X_0\) (even from a complex image distribution), the OU process will eventually push \(X_t\) toward \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2/(2\theta))\) — a simple, known Gaussian.

This solves a fundamental problem in generative modelling: we need a way to turn any data distribution into a simple distribution that we can sample from easily. The OU forward process does exactly that — it erases the structure in the data and replaces it with Gaussian noise, step by step, in a mathematically tractable way.

The MIT notes note that for \(\sigma = 0\), the OU process reduces to the linear ODE from eq. (3) of Section 2.1, with deterministic convergence to zero. Adding \(\sigma > 0\) turns this into a stochastic process that converges to a Gaussian cloud rather than a single point. Figure 3 in the notes shows this transition beautifully.

2.8 A brief note on Ito's lemma

If you apply a smooth function \(g(X_t)\) to an ODE solution, ordinary calculus (the chain rule) gives \(dg = g'(X_t)\,dX_t\). For an SDE solution, there is an extra term.

Ito's lemma (heuristic version) For a smooth function \(g: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\) and an SDE solution \(X_t\): \[dg(X_t) = g'(X_t)\,dX_t + \tfrac{1}{2} g''(X_t)\,\sigma(t)^2\,dt + \cdots\] The extra second-order term \(\tfrac{1}{2} g''(X_t)\,\sigma^2\,dt\) comes from \((dW_t)^2 \approx dt\). In ordinary calculus, \((dx)^2 = 0\) (second-order terms vanish). In stochastic calculus, \((dW_t)^2 = dt\) does NOT vanish — it is first order in \(dt\).
Why (dW)^2 = dt but (dt)^2 = 0 In ordinary calculus, squaring a tiny number \(dt\) gives an even tinier number \(dt^2\), which is negligible. But \(dW_t\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt{dt}\), so \((dW_t)^2\) has magnitude \(dt\) — the same order as \(dt\) itself. You cannot ignore it. This is the central mathematical surprise of Ito calculus, and it is why computing with SDEs requires a different set of rules than computing with ODEs. You will see Ito's lemma appear when the MIT notes derive the score function and the reverse SDE in Parts 3 and 8.

We will not prove Ito's lemma here — full proofs belong in a graduate stochastic calculus course. The goal is simply that when you encounter the extra term in a later derivation, it does not feel mysterious.

2.9 Summary 7: diffusion models as SDEs

The MIT notes culminate Section 2.2 in Summary 7, which ties everything together into the formal definition of a diffusion model:

Summary 7 — SDE generative model (MIT notes Section 2.2) A diffusion model is parameterised by a neural network \(f^\theta_t\) (the drift) and a fixed diffusion coefficient \(\sigma(t)\): \[X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}}, \qquad dX_t = f^\theta_t(X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t)\,dW_t\] To generate a sample:
  1. Draw \(X_0\) from a simple distribution (e.g. a standard Gaussian).
  2. Simulate the SDE from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 1\) using Euler–Maruyama (Algorithm 2).
  3. Return \(X_1\): this should approximate a sample from \(p_{\text{data}}\).
A diffusion model with \(\sigma(t) = 0\) is a flow model (Part 1 of this guide).

2.10 Connection box: the road ahead

How this connects to the rest of the guide Forward process (Parts 4–7): A convention note: in these notes (as in Summary 7) \(t=0\) is noise \(X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}}\) and \(t=1\) is data \(X_1 \sim p_{\text{data}}\). The noising process is an SDE (often OU-like) that corresponds to running time from 1 down to 0 — i.e. the reverse-time direction. (Song et al. and the DDPM papers use the opposite convention, with data at the start.) By choosing \(\sigma(t)\) and \(\theta\) carefully, the marginals \(p_t(x)\) have closed-form expressions, making training tractable.

Reverse-time SDE (Part 8): There exists a remarkable result (the Anderson reverse-time SDE) that says: running an SDE backwards in time produces a new SDE driven by the score function \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\). This is the theoretical heart of score-based diffusion models — and it is why learning the score function (Part 3) is so important.

Probability Flow ODE (Part 9): Every SDE has an associated deterministic ODE that generates the same marginal distributions \(p_t\). This ODE, called the probability flow ODE, allows faster sampling (no noise needed) and enables exact likelihood computation.

In one sentence: Noising is an SDE that turns data into noise (time running from 1 down to 0 in these notes); generation is the forward \(0 \to 1\) SDE of Summary 7 that turns noise back into data.

2.11 PyTorch example: simulating the OU process

Let us put everything together with runnable code. We simulate many independent trajectories of the 1D OU process using Euler–Maruyama, and watch the empirical distribution converge toward the stationary Gaussian.

import torch
import math

# Teaching code: torch.manual_seed is used for reproducibility, not
# security-sensitive RNG — this is purely a pedagogical simulation.
torch.manual_seed(42)

# OU process parameters
theta = 0.5        # spring constant: rate of mean reversion toward 0
sigma = 1.0        # noise amplitude: standard deviation of the Brownian kicks

# Stationary distribution: N(0, sigma^2 / (2*theta))
# sigma^2 / (2*theta) = 1.0 / 1.0 = 1.0, so stationary is N(0, 1.0)
stationary_var  = sigma**2 / (2 * theta)  # = 1.0
stationary_std  = math.sqrt(stationary_var)  # = 1.0

# Simulation settings
n_trajectories = 10_000   # many trajectories so the empirical distribution is smooth
T              = 5.0      # total time to simulate (most of the way to stationarity)
n_steps        = 500      # number of Euler-Maruyama steps
h              = T / n_steps  # step size h = 5.0 / 500 = 0.01

# Initial condition: start far from the stationary distribution to see convergence
# X_0 = 5.0 for ALL trajectories (a point mass at 5, far from the Gaussian N(0,1))
X = torch.full((n_trajectories,), fill_value=5.0)   # shape: (10000,)

print(f"t=0.00  | mean={X.mean():.3f}  std={X.std():.3f}")
# Expected: t=0.00  | mean=5.000  std=0.000   (all start at 5.0)

# Euler-Maruyama loop
# Update rule: X_{t+h} = X_t + h * (-theta * X_t) + sigma * sqrt(h) * eps
#                                 ^^^drift^^^         ^^^diffusion^^^
# The drift -theta*X_t pulls the mean toward 0.
# The diffusion sigma*sqrt(h)*eps injects Gaussian noise; note sqrt(h) NOT h.

check_times = {50, 100, 200, 500}  # steps at which to print diagnostics

for step in range(1, n_steps + 1):
    t       = (step - 1) * h              # current time
    drift   = -theta * X                  # shape (10000,), deterministic pull toward 0
    eps     = torch.randn(n_trajectories) # fresh N(0,1) kick for each trajectory
    noise   = sigma * math.sqrt(h) * eps  # scale by sqrt(h), NOT h
    X       = X + h * drift + noise       # Euler-Maruyama update

    if step in check_times:
        print(f"t={step*h:.2f}  | mean={X.mean():.3f}  std={X.std():.3f}  "
              f"(target mean=0.000, target std={stationary_std:.3f})")

# Expected output (approximate — exact values depend on RNG seed):
# t=0.50  | mean=3.886  std=0.631  (target mean=0.000, target std=1.000)
# t=1.00  | mean=3.025  std=0.800  (target mean=0.000, target std=1.000)
# t=2.00  | mean=1.831  std=0.936  (target mean=0.000, target std=1.000)
# t=5.00  | mean=0.402  std=0.988  (target mean=0.000, target std=1.000)

# Observations:
# 1. The mean starts at 5.0 and decays toward 0 as x_0 * exp(-theta*t) = 5*exp(-0.5*t).
#    At t=5.0: 5*exp(-2.5) ~ 5*0.082 ~ 0.41, but with noise the empirical mean fluctuates.
# 2. The std starts at 0 (point mass) and grows toward the stationary value of 1.0.
# 3. By t=5.0 the std has essentially reached 1, but the mean is still ~0.41 and
#    still decaying — with theta=0.5 the time constant is 2, so t=5 is only 2.5 time
#    constants; near-full convergence of the mean needs t of roughly 5/theta = 10.
# This is the OU forward process: it erases any initial distribution and replaces
# it with N(0, sigma^2/(2*theta)) = N(0, 1) regardless of the starting point.

# Verify the mean decay formula: E[X_t | X_0=5] = 5 * exp(-theta*t)
for t_check in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0]:
    theoretical_mean = 5.0 * math.exp(-theta * t_check)
    print(f"t={t_check}: theoretical mean = {theoretical_mean:.3f}")
# Expected theoretical means:
# t=0.5: theoretical mean = 3.894
# t=1.0: theoretical mean = 3.033
# t=2.0: theoretical mean = 1.839
# t=5.0: theoretical mean = 0.410
Reading the simulation output Notice that by \(t = 5.0\) the standard deviation is near 1.0, while the empirical mean is about 0.41 (\(5e^{-2.5}\)) and still decaying toward 0 — the process is close to, but has not yet reached, the stationary distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\); near-full convergence of the mean needs \(t \gtrsim 5/\theta = 10\). We started at a point mass at \(x = 5\) — very far from Gaussian. The OU process drove the distribution toward Gaussian regardless of where we started. That is the core property that makes OU useful for diffusion models: it is a universal "noise funnel" that maps any distribution toward a known Gaussian, step by step, in a way we can compute and invert.
