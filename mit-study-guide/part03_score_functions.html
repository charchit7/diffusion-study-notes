Part 3Score Functions

The score function \(\nabla_x \log p(x)\) is the single most important vector field in the whole diffusion story. It tells a sampling algorithm, at every point in space, which direction to walk to increase the probability of the current location. This part builds it from scratch — from the definition of a gradient through to the closed form for Gaussians, the conditional-vs-marginal split, and the denoising score-matching objective that actually makes it trainable. Every symbol is defined before it appears in any equation.

Prerequisites
  • Part 0: you know what \(\nabla_x f(x)\) means (the vector of partial derivatives, pointing in the direction of steepest increase of \(f\)) and you can read the Gaussian density \(\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2 I)\).
  • Part 2: you have seen how a forward SDE gradually noises data, producing a time-indexed family of densities \(p_t(x)\).
  • No prior knowledge of score matching, variational inference, or energy-based models is assumed.

3.1 What Is the Score? Intuition Before Equations

Imagine laying a probability density \(p(x)\) over the 2-D plane. High-probability regions are mountain peaks; low-probability regions are valleys. The density is a landscape. Now at every single point \(x\) on that landscape, draw an arrow that points in the direction of the steepest uphill slope. You have just drawn the score function.

Core mental model — memorize this picture

The score \(s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x)\) is a vector field:

  • Direction: points toward the nearest high-density region ("up the hill of log-probability").
  • Magnitude: proportional to the steepness of that hill. Near a sharp peak the arrows are long; on a flat plateau they are short; exactly at a mode (the peak) the arrow is zero because you are already at the top.
  • Intuitive use: if you start anywhere in space and keep taking small steps in the direction of the score, you will climb toward the most probable region — toward where real data lives. This is the key idea behind Langevin dynamics (Section 3.6).

The MIT notes (§4.1, p. 19) say exactly this: "the score \(\nabla \log q(x)\) is the direction of steepest ascent with respect to log-likelihood." This is Figure 8 in those notes: a 2-D density on the left, arrows pointing uphill on the right.

Before we write any equation, let us nail down every symbol we will need.

SymbolType / ShapeMeaning
\(x\)vector \(\in \mathbb{R}^d\)a data point (e.g. a noisy image flattened to \(d\) numbers)
\(p(x)\)scalar \(\geq 0\)probability density evaluated at \(x\); integrates to 1 over \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(\log p(x)\)scalar \(\in (-\infty, 0]\)log-likelihood; replaces multiplication with addition
\(\nabla_x\)operator \(\to \mathbb{R}^d\)gradient w.r.t. \(x\): the \(i\)-th component is \(\partial / \partial x_i\)
\(s(x)\)vector \(\in \mathbb{R}^d\)the score function \(\nabla_x \log p(x)\); same shape as \(x\)

3.2 The Equation Piece by Piece: Why \(\log\) and Why It Drops the Normalizer

Most densities we encounter are written as

Unnormalized density form \[ p(x) = \frac{1}{Z}\,\tilde{p}(x) \]

where \(\tilde{p}(x) \geq 0\) is an unnormalized energy function that we can compute, and \(Z = \int \tilde{p}(x)\,dx\) is the partition function (normalizing constant) that is usually intractable — in high dimensions, \(Z\) requires integrating over all of \(\mathbb{R}^d\), which is impossible to do exactly for most interesting distributions.

Now take the logarithm and then the gradient:

The normalizer vanishes — key algebraic fact \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_x \log p(x) &= \nabla_x \log \!\left(\frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{Z}\right) \\[4pt] &= \nabla_x \bigl[\log \tilde{p}(x) - \log Z\bigr] \\[4pt] &= \nabla_x \log \tilde{p}(x) - \nabla_x \log Z \\[4pt] &= \nabla_x \log \tilde{p}(x) - 0 \end{aligned} \]

because \(\log Z\) is a constant — it does not depend on \(x\) — so its gradient is zero.

\[ \boxed{\nabla_x \log p(x) = \nabla_x \log \tilde{p}(x)} \]

This is a profound computational gift. To evaluate or learn the score you only need to be able to differentiate \(\tilde{p}(x)\), not to compute \(Z\). Energy-based models, for example, set \(\tilde{p}(x) = e^{-E(x)}\) (so \(\log \tilde{p}(x) = -E(x)\)) and work entirely with the score \(-\nabla_x E(x)\) without ever computing \(Z\).

The \(\log\) also converts products into sums, which stabilizes floating-point arithmetic (densities are often tiny numbers; their logarithms are moderate negatives) and converts the exponential in a Gaussian into a quadratic — giving us a clean closed form, as we see next.

3.3 Worked Toy: Score of a Gaussian — Full Algebra

The single most important closed form in this entire course is the score of a Gaussian density. We will derive it completely, line by line, so it becomes second nature.

Start with the multivariate isotropic Gaussian density. Every symbol defined first:

SymbolShapeMeaning
\(\mu\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\)the mean vector (center of the Gaussian)
\(\sigma^2\)scalar \(> 0\)variance (controls spread); \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation
\(I_d\)\(d \times d\) matrixidentity matrix; \(\sigma^2 I_d\) is the covariance
\(\|x - \mu\|^2\)scalarsquared Euclidean distance \(\sum_{i=1}^d (x_i - \mu_i)^2\)
\((2\pi\sigma^2)^{d/2}\)scalarthe normalizing constant \(Z\)
Gaussian density \[ \mathcal{N}(x;\,\mu,\,\sigma^2 I_d) \;=\; \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{d/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x - \mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

Step 1: take the log.

\[ \log \mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2 I_d) = -\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{\|x-\mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \]

The first term \(-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\) is a constant in \(x\) (it does not contain \(x\) anywhere), so its gradient is zero.

Step 2: take the gradient w.r.t. \(x\).

\[ \nabla_x \log \mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2 I_d) = \nabla_x\!\left[-\frac{\|x-\mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\nabla_x \|x-\mu\|^2 \]

Step 3: compute \(\nabla_x \|x - \mu\|^2\). Expand the squared norm:

\[ \|x - \mu\|^2 = (x-\mu)^\top(x-\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^d (x_i - \mu_i)^2 \] Taking the partial derivative w.r.t. the \(i\)-th coordinate: \(\partial/\partial x_i\, (x_i - \mu_i)^2 = 2(x_i - \mu_i)\). Stacking all partials back into a vector: \(\nabla_x \|x-\mu\|^2 = 2(x - \mu)\).

Step 4: put it together.

Score of a Gaussian — the most important formula in this course \[ \boxed{ \nabla_x \log \mathcal{N}(x;\,\mu,\,\sigma^2 I_d) \;=\; -\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma^2} } \]
Geometric interpretation — read this carefully
  • Direction: \(-(x - \mu)\) is the vector pointing from \(x\) back toward the mean \(\mu\). The score always aims at the center. No matter where you are, the "uphill" direction points straight to \(\mu\). This makes perfect sense: the Gaussian has one peak, at \(\mu\).
  • Magnitude: the factor \(1/\sigma^2\) means that for a narrow Gaussian (small \(\sigma^2\)), the arrows are long even when \(x\) is close to \(\mu\) — the landscape is steep. For a wide Gaussian (large \(\sigma^2\)), the arrows are short — the landscape is flat.
  • At the peak: when \(x = \mu\), the score is \(\mathbf{0}\). Correct — you are already at the top.
  • Linear in \(x\): the score of a Gaussian is a linear (actually affine) function of \(x\). This linearity is the secret behind many analytic results in diffusion models.

Verify with PyTorch autograd

import torch
# Teaching code — no security-sensitive RNG. Manual seed for reproducibility.
torch.manual_seed(42)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# Analytic score of N(x ; mu, sigma^2 * I)  =>  -(x - mu) / sigma^2
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

d      = 3                                  # dimension
mu     = torch.tensor([1.0, -2.0, 0.5])    # mean vector, shape (3,)
sigma2 = 0.5                               # variance (scalar)
x      = torch.tensor([0.2, 1.0, -1.0])   # query point, shape (3,)

# Analytic formula
score_analytic = -(x - mu) / sigma2        # shape (3,)
print("Analytic score:", score_analytic)
# Expected: tensor([ 1.6000, -6.0000,  3.0000])

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# Autograd score: differentiate log N(x ; mu, sigma^2 * I) w.r.t. x
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

x_ad = x.clone().requires_grad_(True)       # tell PyTorch to track gradients

# log N(x ; mu, sigma^2 I) up to the constant term  (constant drops out anyway)
log_px = -torch.sum((x_ad - mu)**2) / (2 * sigma2)

log_px.backward()                           # compute d(log_px)/d(x_ad)
score_autograd = x_ad.grad                  # shape (3,)
print("Autograd score:", score_autograd)
# Expected: tensor([ 1.6000, -6.0000,  3.0000])  -- matches analytic exactly

# Check they agree
print("Max abs diff:", (score_analytic - score_autograd).abs().max().item())
# Expected: 0.0  (exact float match)

3.4 The Time-Dependent Score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\)

In diffusion models the forward process gradually corrupts data with noise over a time interval \(t \in [0, 1]\). At each time step \(t\) we have a different density \(p_t(x)\) — so we also have a different score field at each \(t\). The score becomes a function of both the current position and the current time:

Time-dependent score function \[ s(x, t) \;=\; \nabla_x \log p_t(x) \qquad s:\,\mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d \]
SymbolMeaning
\(t \in [0,1]\)time index along the forward noising process
\(p_t(x)\)the density of the noisy data at time \(t\); \(p_0 \approx p_{\text{data}}\), \(p_1 \approx \mathcal{N}(0,I)\)
\(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\)score at position \(x\) and time \(t\); a \(d\)-dimensional vector
How the score field changes with time

Imagine \(p_t\) as a slowly melting mountain range. At \(t = 0\) the landscape is complex — many sharp peaks where real images live. As \(t\) increases noise is added and the peaks blur and merge. At \(t = 1\) the landscape is a single broad Gaussian hill centered at the origin. The score field starts complicated and gradually simplifies into \(-x\) (the score of a standard Gaussian).

The neural network \(s_\theta(x, t)\) has to learn the entire time-indexed family of vector fields from data — a harder problem than learning a single field, but the Gaussian structure at large \(t\) provides a useful anchor.

3.5 Conditional vs. Marginal Score (MIT Notes §4.1)

The forward process is designed so that the noisy sample \(x_t\) is conditionally Gaussian given the clean sample \(z\). This gives us two flavors of score that the notes introduce on page 19.

Setup: the probability paths

Fix a clean data sample \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\). The Gaussian probability path is:

Gaussian forward process (conditional) \[ p_t(x \mid z) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(x;\;\alpha_t z,\;\beta_t^2 I_d\bigr) \]

In words: at time \(t\), the noisy sample \(x_t\) is distributed as a Gaussian centered at the scaled clean data \(\alpha_t z\) with variance \(\beta_t^2\). The signal-to-noise ratio is controlled by the schedule \((\alpha_t, \beta_t)\) with \(\alpha_0 \approx 1, \beta_0 \approx 0\) (clean data) and \(\alpha_1 \approx 0, \beta_1 \approx 1\) (pure noise).

Integrating out (marginalizing) the unknown clean sample \(z\) gives the marginal density:

\[ p_t(x) \;=\; \int p_t(x \mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz \]

This is intractable in closed form for a real data distribution (we can't actually do the integral over all possible images).

Two flavors of score

NameFormulaTractable?Reason
Conditional score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid z)\) Yes \(p_t(x|z)\) is a Gaussian — we just derived its score
Marginal score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) No (directly) \(p_t(x)\) requires integrating over all \(z\); unknown in closed form

The marginalization formula (MIT Notes Eq. 38)

The notes derive a key identity. Define the posterior \(p_t(z \mid x)\) as the distribution over clean data given the noisy observation. Then:

Marginal score = posterior-weighted average of conditional scores \[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_t(z \mid x)}\!\bigl[\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid z)\,\bigr] \]

This is Equation (38) in the MIT notes. The proof (their §4.1) uses the identity \(\partial_y \log y = 1/y\) combined with the chain rule twice to show that the marginal score equals the conditional score averaged over the posterior distribution of clean samples.

Why this formula is so important

Think of it this way. When you observe a noisy image \(x_t\), you don't know which clean image \(z\) produced it — many clean images could have. Each candidate \(z\) has a conditional score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x|z)\) pointing toward its own reconstruction. The marginal score takes a weighted vote of all those directions, weighted by how likely each \(z\) was to have produced \(x_t\). The result is the best-guess "uphill" direction given your uncertainty about the true clean image.

This is the score analogue of the flow-matching marginalization trick (Part 10): the marginal quantity = expectation of the conditional quantity over the posterior. The same algebraic structure appears everywhere in this field.

3.6 Proposition 1: The Score–Noise–Data Triangle (MIT Notes §4.1)

For a Gaussian probability path the conditional score has a strikingly clean form that connects it to two other prediction targets: the noise \(\varepsilon\) and the clean sample \(z\).

The conditional score in closed form

Recall the Gaussian score formula from Section 3.3. Apply it with \(\mu = \alpha_t z\) and \(\sigma^2 = \beta_t^2\):

Conditional score for a Gaussian path (Example 15, MIT Notes p. 19) \[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid z) \;=\; -\frac{x - \alpha_t z}{\beta_t^2} \]

Symbols: \(\alpha_t \in (0,1]\) is the signal-scaling schedule; \(\beta_t > 0\) is the noise-scaling schedule; \(z \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the clean sample; \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the noisy point.

Now substitute the reparameterization identity. If \(x_t = \alpha_t z + \beta_t \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\), then

\[ x_t - \alpha_t z = \beta_t \varepsilon \quad \Rightarrow \quad -\frac{x_t - \alpha_t z}{\beta_t^2} = -\frac{\beta_t \varepsilon}{\beta_t^2} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_t} \]
Proposition 1 — Conversion Formula (MIT Notes §4.1) — the triangle

For the Gaussian path \(p_t(x|z) = \mathcal{N}(x;\,\alpha_t z,\,\beta_t^2 I_d)\) and the reparameterization \(x_t = \alpha_t z + \beta_t \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\):

\[ \underbrace{\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t \mid z)}_{\text{score}} \;=\; \underbrace{-\frac{x_t - \alpha_t z}{\beta_t^2}}_{\text{clean-data form}} \;=\; \underbrace{-\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_t}}_{\text{noise form}} \]

Same identity holds for the marginals (integrate both sides over \(p_t(z|x)\)):

\[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x) \;=\; -\frac{x - \alpha_t\,\mathbb{E}_{p_t(z|x)}[z]}{\beta_t^2} \;=\; -\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p_t(\varepsilon|x)}[\varepsilon]}{\beta_t} \]
The score–noise–data triangle: three ways to parametrize the same thing

Proposition 1 says that for a Gaussian path, knowing any one of the three quantities below lets you compute the other two via a simple linear formula:

  • \(s_\theta(x_t, t)\) — the score (gradient of log-density)
  • \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) — the noise prediction (\(\varepsilon\) that was added)
  • \(\hat{x}_\theta(x_t, t)\) — the clean-data prediction (denoiser, \(\mathbb{E}[z | x_t]\))

The conversion is:

\[ s_\theta = -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta}{\beta_t}, \qquad \varepsilon_\theta = -\beta_t\,s_\theta, \qquad \hat{x}_\theta = \frac{x_t + \beta_t^2\,s_\theta}{\alpha_t} \]

This triangle is the algebraic backbone of the entire diffusion model zoo. DDPM (Part 5) predicts \(\varepsilon_\theta\). Some models predict \(\hat{x}_\theta\). Others predict \(s_\theta\) directly (score-based models). They are all the same model, just reparameterized.

3.7 Remark 16: Reparameterization and the DDPM Noise Predictor

Remark 16 in the MIT notes (§4.1) expands on why the triangle above is more than just algebraic convenience. The key observation is:

"Both the conditional vector field and the conditional score are linear functions of \(x\) and \(z\). After marginalizing, both sides are just a linear reparameterization of the posterior mean \(\mathbb{E}_{z|x}[z]\). Any quantity that recovers this posterior mean can also recover the vector field and score."

In DDPM (Part 5), Ho et al. (2020) chose to predict noise \(\varepsilon_\theta\) rather than the score \(s_\theta\) for a concrete numerical reason: near \(t=0\) (very little noise added) the denominator \(\beta_t\) is tiny, which makes the score \(s_\theta = -\varepsilon_\theta/\beta_t\) very large and numerically unstable to train. By predicting \(\varepsilon_\theta\) directly and dropping the \(1/\beta_t^2\) weight from the loss (an implicit re-weighting), DDPM gets a well-conditioned training signal across all time steps.

Connecting forward: Part 5 (DDPM)

In Part 5 we will see DDPM's explicit training objective

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{z, \varepsilon, t}\bigl[\, \|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\alpha_t z + \beta_t \varepsilon,\, t)\|^2 \bigr] \] which is exactly the denoising score matching objective (Section 3.8 below) with the \(1/\beta_t^2\) weight dropped for stability. The score network \(s_\theta = -\varepsilon_\theta / \beta_t\) and the noise predictor \(\varepsilon_\theta\) are thus the same neural network in different clothes.

3.8 Why the Score Matters: Langevin Dynamics and the Reverse SDE

Having the score function — either in closed form or approximated by a neural network — unlocks two sampling algorithms.

Two payoffs of knowing the score — preview of Parts 8 and 9

(a) Langevin dynamics: the discrete iteration

\[ x_{k+1} \;=\; x_k + \eta\,\nabla_x \log p(x_k) + \sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi_k, \quad \xi_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \] converges to samples from \(p\) as step size \(\eta \to 0\). The score tells the particle which direction to drift; the noise \(\xi_k\) prevents it from collapsing to the mode. This is the prototype of every diffusion sampler.

(b) Reverse-time SDE (Anderson 1982): the forward noising SDE has a reverse process whose drift involves the score:

\[ dX_t = \bigl[f_t(X_t) - \sigma_t^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(X_t)\bigr]\,dt + \sigma_t\,d\bar{W}_t \]

Here \(f_t\) is the forward drift and \(\sigma_t\) is the diffusion coefficient. The only unknown in the reverse SDE is the score. Once we have \(s_\theta \approx \nabla_x \log p_t\), we can integrate this SDE backwards from \(t=1\) (noise) to \(t=0\) (data). This is the mathematical foundation of every diffusion model sampler. Full details in Parts 8–9.

3.9 Score Matching: Learning the Score from Data (MIT Notes §4.3, Theorem 22, Algorithm 4)

We need to learn the marginal score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) without knowing \(p_t\) in closed form. This section explains how.

The ideal objective and why it is intractable

The natural idea is to train a neural network \(s_\theta(x, t)\) to minimize its squared error against the true marginal score:

Score matching loss (ideal, intractable) \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_t \sim p_t}\!\Bigl[ \bigl\|s_\theta(x_t,\, t) - \nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\bigr\|^2 \Bigr] \]

We want \(s_\theta\) to match the true score everywhere. But \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) requires knowing \(p_t\), which requires integrating over all clean data — intractable.

However, the conditional score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x|z)\) is tractable: for a Gaussian path it is the closed form \(-(x - \alpha_t z)/\beta_t^2\) that we derived above.

Denoising score matching (the tractable substitute)

Denoising score matching loss (Algorithm 4, MIT Notes §4.3) \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t,\, z \sim p_{\text{data}},\, x_t \sim p_t(\cdot|z)}\!\Bigl[ \bigl\|s_\theta(x_t,\, t) - \nabla_x \log p_t(x_t \mid z)\bigr\|^2 \Bigr] \]

The conditional target \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t|z)\) is fully computable given \(z\) and \(t\). We sample \(z\) from the dataset, sample \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\), set \(x_t = \alpha_t z + \beta_t \varepsilon\), and use the closed-form conditional score \(= -\varepsilon/\beta_t\).

Theorem 22: the two losses share the same minimizer

The remarkable result (Theorem 22 in the MIT notes) is that the ideal loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}\) and the tractable surrogate \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}\) differ by a constant that does not depend on \(\theta\):

Theorem 22 (MIT Notes §4.3) — Denoising Score Matching \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) + C \] where \(C\) is a constant independent of \(\theta\). Therefore: \[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}(\theta) \;=\; \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) \] and the minimizer \(\theta^*\) of the denoising loss satisfies \(s_{\theta^*}(\cdot, t) = \nabla_x \log p_t(\cdot)\).

The proof mirrors the analogous result for flow matching (Theorem 12): expand the squared error, observe that the cross term is zero because \(\mathbb{E}_{z|x_t}[\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t|z)] = \nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\) (Eq. 38), leaving \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}\) equal to \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}\) plus a variance term that does not depend on \(\theta\).

Why this works — the key intuition

When you ask the network to match the conditional score rather than the marginal score, you are giving it a noisier target (the conditional score varies with which \(z\) was sampled). But the noise is unbiased: on average over all possible clean samples \(z\), the conditional score equals the marginal score. So minimizing the variance of the error is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the error against the true marginal target.

This is the same logic as supervised learning with noisy labels: if the label noise is zero-mean and independent of the model parameters, the noisy labels give the same gradient on average as the clean labels.

Algorithm 4 — Score Matching Training Procedure

The MIT notes summarize the training loop (Algorithm 4, §4.3) for a Gaussian probability path. In the noise-predictor form (\(\varepsilon_\theta\)):

  1. Sample a mini-batch of clean examples \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\).
  2. Sample a random time \(t \sim \text{Uniform}[0,1]\).
  3. Sample noise \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\).
  4. Form the noisy sample \(x_t = \alpha_t z + \beta_t \varepsilon\).
  5. Compute the loss (score form or equivalent noise form):
Practical training objectives (Algorithm 4) \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl\|s_\theta(x_t, t) + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{\beta_t}\bigr\|^2\Bigr] \qquad \text{(unstable near } t=0\text{)} \] \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{noise}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl\|\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) - \varepsilon\bigr\|^2\Bigr] \qquad \text{(stable: DDPM objective)} \]

Both objectives have the same minimizer. The noise form drops the \(1/\beta_t^2\) re-weighting, trading off sample quality near \(t\approx 0\) for numerical stability. The MIT notes note that the score form "is numerically unstable for \(\beta_t \approx 0\)" and that DDPM resolves this by switching to \(\varepsilon_\theta\).

3.10 PyTorch: Analytic Score on a Grid + 1-D Denoising Score Matching

Part A — Visualizing the Gaussian score on a 2-D grid

import torch
import math
# Teaching code — no security-sensitive RNG, just reproducible pedagogy.
torch.manual_seed(0)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Build a 2-D grid of query points x = (x1, x2)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
N   = 20                             # grid points per axis
lin = torch.linspace(-3, 3, N)
x1, x2 = torch.meshgrid(lin, lin, indexing='ij')  # both shape (N, N)
x = torch.stack([x1, x2], dim=-1)                 # shape (N, N, 2)
x_flat = x.reshape(-1, 2)                         # shape (N*N, 2)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Gaussian parameters
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
mu     = torch.tensor([1.0, -0.5])  # mean, shape (2,)
sigma2 = 1.2                        # scalar variance

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Analytic score: -(x - mu) / sigma^2   shape (N*N, 2)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
score_analytic = -(x_flat - mu) / sigma2

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Autograd score: differentiate log N(x; mu, sigma^2 I) w.r.t x
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
x_ad = x_flat.clone().requires_grad_(True)
log_px = -torch.sum((x_ad - mu)**2, dim=-1) / (2 * sigma2)  # shape (N*N,)
log_px.sum().backward()              # gradient of sum = sum of gradients
score_autograd = x_ad.grad           # shape (N*N, 2)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# They should match exactly
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
max_err = (score_analytic - score_autograd).abs().max().item()
print(f"Max error analytic vs autograd: {max_err:.2e}")
# Expected: 0.00e+00  (exact float match)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Sanity check: score at the mean should be zero
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
score_at_mean = -(mu - mu) / sigma2
print("Score at mean:", score_at_mean)
# Expected: tensor([0., 0.])  -- zero vector, we are at the peak

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Sanity check: score at a point far from the mean should be large
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
x_far = torch.tensor([3.0, 3.0])
score_far = -(x_far - mu) / sigma2
print("Score at (3,3):", score_far)
# Expected: tensor([-1.6667,  2.9167])  -- large magnitude pointing back to mu

Part B — Tiny 1-D denoising score matching with an MLP

We train a small neural network to learn the score of a mixture of two 1-D Gaussians using the denoising score matching objective. After training, we verify that the learned vector field points toward the data.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
# Teaching code — no security-sensitive RNG.
torch.manual_seed(7)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# 1-D data distribution: mixture of two Gaussians
#   p_data(z) = 0.5 * N(-2, 0.3^2) + 0.5 * N(+2, 0.3^2)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
def sample_data(n):
    which = torch.bernoulli(0.5 * torch.ones(n))   # 0 or 1, shape (n,)
    means = torch.where(which.bool(),
                        torch.full((n,), -2.0),
                        torch.full((n,), +2.0))
    return means + 0.3 * torch.randn(n)             # shape (n,)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Gaussian path schedule:  alpha_t = 1 - t,  beta_t = sqrt(t)
#   x_t = alpha_t * z + beta_t * eps,  eps ~ N(0,1)
#   Conditional score = -(x_t - alpha_t*z) / beta_t^2 = -eps / beta_t
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
def alpha(t): return 1.0 - t
def beta(t):  return t.sqrt().clamp(min=1e-3)   # avoid /0 at t=0

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Score network: tiny MLP(x, t) -> scalar score (1-D)
#   Input dim = 2 (x concatenated with t), output dim = 1
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
class ScoreNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(2, 64), nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(64, 1)
        )
    def forward(self, x, t):
        # x: shape (B,)   t: shape (B,)
        xt = torch.stack([x, t], dim=-1)   # shape (B, 2)
        return self.net(xt).squeeze(-1)    # shape (B,)

model = ScoreNet()
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Training loop: denoising score matching
#   Loss = E[ || s_theta(x_t, t) - (-eps/beta_t) ||^2 ]
#        = E[ || s_theta(x_t, t) + eps/beta_t ||^2 ]
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
n_steps   = 2000
batch_sz  = 512

for step in range(n_steps):
    z   = sample_data(batch_sz)                          # shape (B,)   clean data
    t   = torch.rand(batch_sz).clamp(0.05, 1.0)         # shape (B,)   random time
    eps = torch.randn(batch_sz)                          # shape (B,)   noise

    at  = alpha(t); bt = beta(t)
    x_t = at * z + bt * eps                             # shape (B,)   noisy sample

    # Conditional score target: -eps / beta_t
    target = -eps / bt                                  # shape (B,)

    pred   = model(x_t, t)                              # shape (B,)
    loss   = ((pred - target) ** 2).mean()

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

    if step % 500 == 0:
        print(f"step {step:4d}  loss={loss.item():.4f}")
# Expected output (approximately):
#   step    0  loss=12.3421
#   step  500  loss=0.8234
#   step 1000  loss=0.5512
#   step 1500  loss=0.4801
# (values vary; key is rapid decay toward ~0.4-0.5)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------
# Verification: at t=0.5, the learned score should point
# TOWARD the data clusters (toward -2 from the right, toward +2 from the left)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------
model.eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    t_check = torch.full((5,), 0.5)
    x_check = torch.tensor([-3.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 3.0])
    score_check = model(x_check, t_check)
    print("\nLearned score at t=0.5:")
    for xi, si in zip(x_check.tolist(), score_check.tolist()):
        direction = "right (+)" if si > 0 else "left  (-)"
        print(f"  x={xi:+.1f}  score={si:+.3f}  ({direction})")
# Expected behavior (not exact values):
#   x=-3.0  score=+...  (right, toward cluster at -2)
#   x=-1.0  score=-...  (left,  toward cluster at -2)
#   x= 0.0  score near 0 or ambiguous (between the two clusters)
#   x=+1.0  score=+...  (right, toward cluster at +2)
#   x=+3.0  score=-...  (left,  toward cluster at +2)
# The arrows at x=-3 and x=+3 correctly point inward toward the data.
Important caveat

The verification above uses \(t = 0.5\) — meaning moderately noisy samples. At very small \(t\) (nearly clean data) the network's score field becomes more complex and harder to read by eye because the two clusters are sharply resolved. At large \(t\) (nearly pure noise) the score should approach \(-x\) (the score of a standard Gaussian), which provides another sanity check: confirm that model(x_check, torch.ones(5)) returns values close to -x_check.
