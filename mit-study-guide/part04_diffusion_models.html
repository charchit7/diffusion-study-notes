Part 4Diffusion Models — the big picture

Parts 0–3 built the toolkit: vectors, flows, SDEs, and score functions. This part is the bridge — it shows you how those tools snap together into a complete generative algorithm and maps out the four views (DDPM, DDIM, continuous SDE, flow matching) that Parts 5–13 develop in detail. After reading this page you will understand why diffusion models work before you see a single line of the formal derivations.

Prerequisites
  • Part 0 — vectors \(z \in \mathbb{R}^d\), probability densities, the Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\).
  • Part 1 — flows transport a density; an ODE \(\dot{x}_t = u_t(x_t)\) moves points through space.
  • Part 2 — Brownian motion, SDEs of the form \(dx_t = f_t(x_t)\,dt + g_t\,dW_t\), and how they smear a density over time.
  • Part 3 — the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\), what it points toward, and why a neural network can learn it.

4.1 The goal: given a dataset, produce fresh samples

Every generative model in this course traces back to two elementary ideas that the MIT notes state explicitly as their first two Key Ideas (§1.3).

Key Idea 1 (MIT notes §1.3) — Objects are vectors Every kind of object we want to generate — an image, a video frame, a protein backbone — can be flattened into a vector \[ z \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^d. \] For a 512×512 RGB image, \(d = 512 \times 512 \times 3 = 786\,432\). The space of all possible images is simply all of \(\mathbb{R}^d\); "realistic-looking images" are a complicated low-dimensional cloud inside that space.
Key Idea 2 (MIT notes §1.3) — Generation is sampling The diversity of good dog images, good protein folds, or good pieces of music is captured by a probability distribution over \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Call it \(p_{\text{data}}\). "Generate an object" means draw a fresh sample \[ z \;\sim\; p_{\text{data}}. \]

In practice we never know \(p_{\text{data}}\) as an explicit formula. What we do have is a finite dataset:

Key Idea 3 (MIT notes §1.3) — The dataset We are given \(N\) examples \[ z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_N \;\sim\; p_{\text{data}} \] drawn independently from the true distribution. These act as a proxy for \(p_{\text{data}}\). The goal of training is to build an algorithm that, after seeing these examples, can produce new samples \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\) — points that look like they could have come from the same data collection process but were never seen during training.

Let us define every symbol we will use in this part before going further.

SymbolTypeMeaning
\(z \in \mathbb{R}^d\)vectorone data object (image, molecule, …)
\(p_{\text{data}}\)density on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)the true distribution of real data
\(z_1,\ldots,z_N\)vectorstraining dataset, i.i.d. from \(p_{\text{data}}\)
\(p_{\text{init}}\)density on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)the starting noise distribution, \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\)
\(x_t\)random vector, \(t \in [0,1]\)the noisy version of a data point at "time" \(t\)

\(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\)random vectorpure Gaussian noise, same shape as \(z\)
\(s_\theta(x,t)\)neural network \(\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^d\)the learned score approximation \(\approx \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\)

\(T\)integernumber of discrete time steps (DDPM)

4.2 The core trick: forward corruption, learned reversal

The central insight of diffusion models can be stated in a single paragraph.

The big idea Define a forward process that gradually mixes a data point \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\) with more and more Gaussian noise until, at the end of the process, all memory of the original data has been erased and you are left with pure noise \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\). This forward direction is trivial to run: you write down the formula and evaluate it. Now train a neural network to run this process backwards — starting from pure noise and stepping back toward the data distribution. That reverse process is the generative model. At sampling time you draw \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) (easy!) and feed it through the learned reverse process to get a fresh data sample.

A picture in words:

DATA          ──── forward process (add noise, easy) ────►  NOISE
z ~ p_data     x_0 → x_1 → x_2 → … → x_T ≈ N(0,I)     ε ~ N(0,I)

NOISE         ◄─── reverse process (denoise, learned) ───  NOISE
ε ~ N(0,I)     x_T → x_{T-1} → … → x_0 ≈ z ~ p_data   ε ~ N(0,I)

The forward process is a fixed mathematical formula (no learning required). The reverse process uses a single neural network \(s_\theta\) that is trained during the learning phase and then reused at every step of generation.

The forward process in one formula

The simplest continuous-time forward process is a linear interpolation between data \(x_0\) and noise \(\varepsilon\): \[ x_t \;=\; \sqrt{1-t}\;x_0 \;+\; \sqrt{t}\;\varepsilon, \qquad t \in [0,1],\quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I). \] At \(t=0\): \(x_0 = x_0\) (pure data). At \(t=1\): \(x_1 = \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) (pure noise). At intermediate \(t\): a blend of signal and noise. The coefficients \(\sqrt{1-t}\) and \(\sqrt{t}\) are chosen so that if \(\mathrm{Var}[x_0]=1\) the variance of \(x_t\) stays at 1 for all \(t\) — a variance-preserving schedule. (In general \(\mathrm{Var}[x_t] = (1-t)\,\mathrm{Var}[x_0] + t\), which interpolates from \(\mathrm{Var}[x_0]\) to 1.) This is the object the PyTorch illustration in §4.6 demonstrates.

Distribution view Let \(p_t\) denote the marginal distribution of \(x_t\). Then \(p_0 \approx p_{\text{data}}\) and \(p_1 = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\). The forward process is a continuous family of distributions \(\{p_t\}_{t\in[0,1]}\) that interpolates between data and noise. In Part 1 language (flows), a velocity field \(u_t\) transports \(p_0\) to \(p_1\). In Part 2 language (SDEs), adding Brownian noise does the same job stochastically.

4.3 Why gradual? The argument for many small steps

Why not one giant step? Imagine asking a network: "Here is a blob of pure Gaussian noise; turn it directly into a photo-realistic dog." That mapping is astronomically complex — it must simultaneously hallucinate every fur strand, lighting shadow, and background blade of grass from nothing. No amount of capacity makes such a task easy to supervise.

Now imagine a much simpler task: "Here is a nearly-complete dog image with just a tiny sprinkle of noise; remove that sprinkle." That is a well-defined, local regression problem. The network only needs to undo the last little nudge.

Diffusion works by chaining thousands of such tiny denoising steps. Each step is easy to supervise with a mean-squared-error loss. The power of the approach is that the same network, shown the current noise level \(t\) as an extra input, handles every step along the chain.

Formally: at each step the network is asked to predict the score \(\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)\), which (from Part 3) points in the direction of increasing probability — i.e., toward cleaner data. Each denoising step moves \(x_t\) a small distance in that direction. Over many steps the trajectory winds from the noise cloud all the way to the data manifold.

The key takeaway: breaking a hard problem into many easy supervised sub-problems is the fundamental reason diffusion models scale so well and produce such sharp, diverse outputs.

4.4 Three views and one generalization — a roadmap

The rest of this guide develops diffusion from four complementary angles. Each view uses the same trained network but emphasizes different mathematics. Understanding all four is what the MIT notes call "the modern stack."

View Core object Key operation Covered in
(a) DDPM — discrete, probabilistic Markov chain of \(T\) noising steps + learned reverse chain Variational lower bound (ELBO) loss; Gaussian transitions Part 5
(b) DDIM — deterministic sampler Same trained score network; ODE-style update rule Skip steps, deterministic trajectories, same model fewer NFEs Part 6
(c) Continuous SDE / score Forward SDE + reverse-time SDE driven by \(\nabla_x \log p_t\) Take step-size \(\to 0\); Anderson's reverse SDE theorem Parts 7–9
(d) Flow matching — MIT notes' primary framing Velocity field \(u_t(x)\); probability-flow ODE \(\dot{x}_t = u_t(x_t)\) Regress network onto a target velocity; diffusion is a special case Part 10

(a) View 1 — DDPM: a discrete Markov chain (Part 5)

The original Ho et al. (2020) paper defines the forward process as a length-\(T\) Markov chain with Gaussian transitions: \[ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(x_t;\; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\bigr), \] where \(\beta_1 \le \beta_2 \le \cdots \le \beta_T\) is a noise schedule and \(T \approx 1000\). The reverse chain \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) is also Gaussian, with mean predicted by the network. Training minimises a variational bound on \(-\log p_\theta(x_0)\). Part 5 derives this fully.

(b) View 2 — DDIM: the bridge to ODEs (Part 6)

DDIM (Song et al., 2020) notices that the marginals \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\) are fixed, but the transitions between steps do not have to be Markovian or even stochastic. Choosing a deterministic update rule turns the reverse chain into a discretised ODE that can be run with far fewer function evaluations (NFEs) — sometimes as few as 10–50 instead of 1000 — with almost no quality loss. DDIM is the conceptual bridge between the discrete probabilistic view and the continuous ODE view.

(c) View 3 — continuous time: SDEs and the probability-flow ODE (Parts 7–9)

Let the step size go to zero. The discrete Markov chain becomes a stochastic differential equation (SDE): \[ dx_t \;=\; f_t(x_t)\,dt \;+\; g_t\,dW_t, \] where \(f_t\) is a drift, \(g_t\) a diffusion coefficient, and \(W_t\) is Brownian motion (Part 2). The reverse of this forward SDE is also an SDE (Anderson, 1982), and it depends on the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) (Part 3): \[ dx_t \;=\;\bigl[f_t(x_t) - g_t^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\bigr]dt \;+\; g_t\,d\bar{W}_t. \] Alternatively, there is a deterministic ODE with the same marginals — the probability-flow ODE — that uses only the score, no stochasticity. Parts 7–9 derive both in detail.

(d) View 4 — flow matching: the most general framing (Part 10)

Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2022) steps back and asks: what is the most direct way to move a density from noise to data using an ODE? Rather than defining noising schedules and then reversing them, flow matching directly trains a network \(v_\theta(x, t)\) to match a target velocity field \(u_t(x)\) that is analytically tractable at the level of individual data points. DDPM and score-based diffusion turn out to be special cases of flow matching — specific choices of the interpolation \((x_0, x_1) \mapsto x_t\). The MIT notes use flow matching as their primary framework throughout, deriving diffusion as a consequence. Part 10 develops this fully.

How the views relate Think of four lenses on the same camera:
  • DDPM is the original engineering lens — discrete steps, probabilistic loss, most approachable.
  • DDIM is a faster sampler lens — same model weights, cleaner math, fewer steps.
  • The SDE/score lens is the continuous-time mathematical lens — most powerful for theory (guidance, likelihood).
  • Flow matching is the unified design lens — one training objective, generalises everything.
The same neural network \(s_\theta\) trained with any of the last three objectives is approximately the same function; what changes is how you use it at sampling time.

4.5 The role of the neural network

One detail beginners miss: there is only one network, shared across all time steps. It is a function \[ s_\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{R}^d, \qquad (x,\, t) \;\longmapsto\; s_\theta(x, t), \] that takes a noisy sample and the current noise level \(t\) as input. The time conditioning is critical: the same pixel blob at \(t=0.05\) (nearly clean) and \(t=0.95\) (nearly noise) calls for completely different corrections, and the network must handle both by reading \(t\).

This single network is trained to approximate one of three equivalent quantities (which Part 3 showed are related by simple rescaling):

Quantity the network predictsSymbolConnection to score
Score function \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) the thing itself; used directly in SDE/ODE samplers
Noise prediction \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) \(\approx -\sigma_t \nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\); DDPM parameterisation
Velocity field \(u_\theta(x_t, t)\) the "flow matching" parameterisation; derivative of the interpolation

This is the triangle of equivalent parameterisations from Part 3. Once you have any one of them, you can recover the others with simple algebra.

Why one network for all steps? Weight-sharing across time is not just a memory saving. It is what makes diffusion generalize. The network sees thousands of time steps during training and learns a smooth, time-conditioned denoising function. At sampling time, any sequence of \(t\)-values can be used — even ones the network never saw during training — because it has learned the underlying geometry of \(p_t\). This is what enables DDIM's step-skipping trick in Part 6.

4.6 Generation as distribution transport

Let us put this in the language of Parts 1 and 2, which built the mathematical machinery for transporting distributions.

We have two distributions:

  • \(p_{\text{init}} = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\): easy to sample — just call torch.randn.
  • \(p_{\text{data}}\): hard to sample — that is the whole problem.

The generative model is a transport map \(\Phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) such that if \(\varepsilon \sim p_{\text{init}}\), then \(\Phi(\varepsilon) \sim p_{\text{data}}\). In Part 1, this map was an ODE flow \(\Phi = \phi_1\) where \(\dot{\phi}_t = u_t(\phi_t)\) with \(u_t\) learned. In Part 2, the map was stochastic — an SDE trajectory. Both are valid; they differ in how they use the noise injected at each step.

The forward process is the inverse transport: it maps \(p_{\text{data}} \to p_{\text{init}}\) along the same family of distributions \(\{p_t\}_{t\in[0,1]}\). Learning the reverse amounts to learning the inverse map, which is why the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) — the "which direction increases \(p_t\)" signal from Part 3 — is so central.

Connection across Parts 1–3
  • Part 1 (flows): The probability-flow ODE transports \(p_{\text{init}} \to p_{\text{data}}\) deterministically. Every point traces a smooth trajectory; no randomness after the initial draw.
  • Part 2 (SDEs): The reverse SDE adds fresh noise at every step, which allows the trajectory to explore more of the data manifold. In practice this gives slightly more diverse samples.
  • Part 3 (scores): Both the ODE and the SDE sampler are driven by \(\nabla_x \log p_t\). Fitting that score function is the training objective (score matching). Once learned, you plug it into either sampler — two sampling algorithms, one trained model.

4.7 Conditioning and guidance — a teaser

So far we have described unconditional generation: sample any plausible data point. In practice we almost always want conditional generation: "generate a dog image given the text prompt \(y\)." The MIT notes formalise this as sampling from the conditional data distribution.

Key Idea 4 (MIT notes §1.3) — Guided generation Guided generation involves sampling from \[ z \;\sim\; p_{\text{data}}(\cdot \mid y), \] where \(y\) is a conditioning variable (text caption, class label, sketch, …). The guided data distribution \(p_{\text{data}}(\cdot \mid y)\) gives high probability to objects that match the condition \(y\).
Teaser — Part 12 Conditioning turns out to be elegantly handled by modifying the score: \[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid y) \;=\; \nabla_x \log p_t(x) \;+\; \underbrace{\nabla_x \log p_t(y \mid x)}_{\text{guidance term}}. \] Classifier-free guidance, the technique powering Stable Diffusion and every modern text-to-image model, approximates this term without an explicit classifier. Part 12 derives it in detail. For now, just note that conditioning is not a new architecture — it is a modification of the score function the existing network learns.

4.8 PyTorch illustration — watching data dissolve into noise

The following snippet demonstrates the forward process \(x_t = \sqrt{1-t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{t}\,\varepsilon\) on a small 2D dataset. It prints the mean and standard deviation of the cloud at \(t=0\) (pure data), \(t=0.5\) (half-way), and \(t=1.0\) (pure noise), confirming that the data signal vanishes and only Gaussian noise remains. The expected outputs are shown inline.

import torch

# ----------------------------------------------------------------
# Teaching code — not security-sensitive RNG; reproducibility only.
# ----------------------------------------------------------------
torch.manual_seed(42)

# ── 1. Create a toy 2-D data cloud ──────────────────────────────
#    Imagine two clusters: one near (+2, +2) and one near (-2, -2).
#    Each cluster has 500 points drawn from a tight Gaussian.
N = 1000                                   # total data points
cluster_A = torch.randn(N // 2, 2) * 0.4 + torch.tensor([2.0, 2.0])
cluster_B = torch.randn(N // 2, 2) * 0.4 + torch.tensor([-2.0, -2.0])
x0 = torch.cat([cluster_A, cluster_B], dim=0)   # shape (1000, 2); x0 ~ p_data

# ── 2. Define the forward process ───────────────────────────────
#    x_t = sqrt(1-t) * x0 + sqrt(t) * eps
#    This is the variance-preserving interpolation used in many
#    modern diffusion models (e.g., the MIT notes' primary example).
#    At t=0  : x_t = x0        (original data, no noise)
#    At t=0.5: x_t = ~0.71*x0 + ~0.71*eps (equal signal and noise)
#    At t=1  : x_t = eps        (pure Gaussian noise, signal gone)

def forward_process(x0, t):
    """
    x0 : (N, d) tensor — batch of data points
    t  : float in [0, 1] — noise level
    returns x_t : (N, d) tensor — noisy version at time t
    """
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)             # eps ~ N(0, I), same shape as x0
    signal_coeff = (1.0 - t) ** 0.5        # sqrt(1 - t): shrinks toward 0
    noise_coeff  = t ** 0.5                # sqrt(t):     grows toward 1
    return signal_coeff * x0 + noise_coeff * eps

# ── 3. Run the forward process at three time points ─────────────
for t in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0]:
    xt = forward_process(x0, t)
    print(f"t={t:.1f} | mean={xt.mean().item():.4f}  std={xt.std().item():.4f}")

# Expected output:
#   t=0.0 | mean=-0.0069  std=2.0499   ← two clusters; std reflects ±2 offsets
#   t=0.5 | mean= 0.0130  std=1.6039   ← signal half-attenuated; clusters blurring
#   t=1.0 | mean=-0.0066  std=0.9852   ← pure noise; mean≈0, std≈1 (standard Gaussian)
#
#   The std drops from ~2 toward 1, and the mean collapses toward 0.
#   At t=1 the cloud is indistinguishable from N(0, I) — the two
#   clusters have completely dissolved.  This is what a diffusion
#   model learns to reverse.

# ── 4. Verify marginal statistics match theory ───────────────────
#    For any x0, E[x_t] = sqrt(1-t) * x0  and  Var[x_t] = t * I
#    So if x0 has std sigma_0, then x_t has std sqrt((1-t)*sigma_0^2 + t).
#    At t=1: std = sqrt(0 + 1) = 1.  Check:
sigma0 = x0.std().item()                   # ≈ 2.050
for t in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0]:
    expected_std = ((1 - t) * sigma0 ** 2 + t) ** 0.5
    print(f"t={t:.1f} | theoretical std = {expected_std:.4f}")

# Expected output:
#   t=0.0 | theoretical std = 2.0499
#   t=0.5 | theoretical std = 1.6128
#   t=1.0 | theoretical std = 1.0000
What the code shows The forward process is just arithmetic — a weighted sum of a data vector and a noise vector. No network, no training, no gradient. The entire difficulty of diffusion models lies in the reverse: learning to undo this arithmetic step by step, guided only by the score of \(p_t\). The code makes concrete what "data dissolving into noise" means; Parts 5–9 show how to reverse the journey.

4.9 Teaser: the three equivalent network outputs

When you read diffusion papers you will encounter three different things the network is said to predict. They are all the same information, just rescaled. This is the "triangle" of Part 3 applied to the variance-preserving forward process \(x_t = \sqrt{1-t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{t}\,\varepsilon\).

NameSymbolWhat it representsPapers that use it
Score prediction \(s_\theta(x_t, t) \approx \nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)\) direction of rising density at \(x_t\) at time \(t\) Song et al. (2020 NeurIPS)
Noise prediction \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \approx \varepsilon\) the noise component added in the forward step Ho et al. (2020) DDPM
Velocity prediction \(v_\theta(x_t, t) \approx \dot{x}_t\) time-derivative of the interpolation trajectory Salimans & Ho (2022); FLUX, SD3

All three losses train the same function up to rescaling by \(\sqrt{t}\) and \(\sqrt{1-t}\). The choice affects numerical stability at extreme values of \(t\) but not the learned model. Part 5 proves the equivalence explicitly.
