Part 5DDPM — Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

Parts 0–4 built the full toolkit: vectors, flows, SDEs, score functions, and the big-picture forward/reverse idea. This part zooms in on DDPM (Ho et al., 2020), the first landmark discrete-time diffusion model. By the end you will have derived every equation from scratch, understood why the simple noise-prediction loss works, and seen a minimal PyTorch implementation of both training and sampling.

Prerequisites
  • Part 0 — Gaussian distributions, the "variances add" property, reparameterization \(x = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\).
  • Part 2 — Brownian motion, the idea that noise accumulates over steps.
  • Part 3 — the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) and the score ↔ \(\varepsilon\) ↔ \(x_0\) triangle.
  • Part 4 — the two-phase (forward / reverse) picture at a high level.

5.1 Big picture: what DDPM does and why it is special

The MIT notes present diffusion models from the continuous-time SDE viewpoint (probability paths, flow matching). DDPM was historically the discrete-time entry point that made diffusion models practical. The two formulations describe the same mathematics; DDPM just makes a concrete set of choices:

  • Use exactly \(T\) discrete time steps (\(T = 1000\) in the original paper).
  • At each step, add a small controlled amount of Gaussian noise (a Markov chain).
  • Train a neural network to reverse one step of that chain.
  • Derive a simple mean-squared loss by a variational lower bound argument.
Intuition — why discrete steps? Imagine you are teaching someone to restore a shredded document. If you hand them the entire shredded pile and say "reconstruct the original", it is nearly impossible. But if you hand them the document one tear at a time and ask "what did this look like before this single tear?", each sub-task is easy. DDPM makes the same trade: turn one hard generative problem into \(T\) tractable denoising problems, each requiring only one small step of noise to be removed.

5.2 The forward (noising) Markov chain

5.2.1 One-step noising kernel

Let \(x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) be a clean data point (e.g. a flattened image). DDPM defines a forward process by repeatedly applying a fixed Gaussian kernel to produce a sequence \(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_T\):

\[ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(x_t;\;\underbrace{\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,x_{t-1}}_{\text{signal}},\; \underbrace{\beta_t\, I}_{\text{noise}}\bigr), \qquad t = 1, \ldots, T. \]
Symbol table — forward chain
SymbolMeaningShape / range
\(d\)data dimensionalityscalar, e.g. \(d = 784\) for MNIST
\(T\)total number of noising stepsscalar, typically 1000
\(t\)discrete time index\(t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}\)
\(x_0\)clean data point\(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(x_t\)noisy data at step \(t\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\)
\(\beta_t\)noise variance at step \(t\) (noise schedule)\((0, 1)\), e.g. linearly from \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\)
\(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\)signal attenuation factorscalar \(\in (0,1)\)
\(I\)identity matrix\(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\)
Intuition — what one step does Each step does two things simultaneously:
  1. Shrinks the signal by multiplying by \(\sqrt{1-\beta_t} < 1\). Over many steps the original signal gets driven toward zero.
  2. Injects fresh Gaussian noise with variance \(\beta_t\). Over many steps the distribution approaches \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\).
The two effects are carefully balanced so that the total variance stays near 1 throughout (you can verify: if \(\operatorname{Var}[x_{t-1}] \approx 1\) then \(\operatorname{Var}[x_t] = (1-\beta_t)\cdot 1 + \beta_t = 1\)). This keeps the numerics stable across all 1000 steps.

5.2.2 Toy example — one step in 1-D

Let \(d=1\), \(x_0 = 2.0\), \(\beta_1 = 0.1\). Then \(q(x_1 \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_1;\, \sqrt{0.9}\cdot 2.0,\; 0.1)\). The mean is \(\approx 1.897\) and the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.1} \approx 0.316\). A sample might be \(x_1 \approx 1.897 + 0.316 \cdot \varepsilon\) for \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). After 1000 such steps with slowly growing \(\beta_t\), the resulting \(x_{1000}\) will be indistinguishable from \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

5.3 The closed-form jump — the key algebraic payoff

Simulating the full 1000-step chain for every training update would be catastrophically slow. DDPM gets around this with a beautiful algebraic trick: you can jump from \(x_0\) to any \(x_t\) in a single shot.

5.3.1 Defining \(\alpha_t\) and \(\bar{\alpha}_t\)

Introduce two shorthand quantities:

\[ \alpha_t \;:=\; 1 - \beta_t, \qquad \bar{\alpha}_t \;:=\; \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s \;=\; \alpha_1 \cdot \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t. \]
Symbol table — cumulative products
SymbolMeaningValue range
\(\alpha_t\)"keep fraction" at step \(t\)\((0,1)\), close to 1 for small \(\beta_t\)
\(\bar{\alpha}_t\)cumulative product up to step \(t\)decreasing from \(\approx 1\) (at \(t=0\)) to \(\approx 0\) (at \(t=T\))
\(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t\)cumulative noise varianceincreasing from \(\approx 0\) to \(\approx 1\)
Intuition — why these products appear Each step multiplies the signal by \(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\). After \(t\) steps the signal has been multiplied by \(\sqrt{\alpha_1}\cdot\sqrt{\alpha_2}\cdots\sqrt{\alpha_t} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\). The noises from each step also accumulate — but independently, so their variances add. The formula below makes this precise.

5.3.2 Deriving the closed-form for two steps, then the general case

Setup. We use the reparameterization trick from Part 0: if \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)\) then \(x = \mu + \sigma\,\varepsilon\) where \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\). We will also use Property B (variances add for independent Gaussians) from Part 0: if \(\varepsilon_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_1^2 I)\) and \(\varepsilon_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_2^2 I)\) are independent, then \(\sigma_1 \varepsilon_1 + \sigma_2 \varepsilon_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)I)\).

Step 1: derive \(q(x_1 \mid x_0)\) in reparameterized form.

\[ x_1 = \sqrt{\alpha_1}\,x_0 + \sqrt{\beta_1}\,\varepsilon_1, \qquad \varepsilon_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \]

This is just rewriting \(q(x_1 \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\alpha_1}\,x_0, \beta_1 I)\) in reparameterized form (using \(\beta_1 = 1 - \alpha_1\)).

Step 2: substitute into \(q(x_2 \mid x_1)\).

\[ x_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2}\,x_1 + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\varepsilon_2, \qquad \varepsilon_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),\;\varepsilon_2 \perp \varepsilon_1. \]

Plug in the expression for \(x_1\):

\[ x_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2}\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_1}\,x_0 + \sqrt{\beta_1}\,\varepsilon_1\bigr) + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\varepsilon_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2 \alpha_1}\,x_0 + \underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_2 \beta_1}\,\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\varepsilon_2}_{\text{two independent Gaussian vectors}}. \]

Apply Property B to the noise term. The two vectors are independent with variances \(\alpha_2 \beta_1\) and \(\beta_2 = 1 - \alpha_2\) respectively, so their sum has variance:

\[ \alpha_2 \beta_1 + \beta_2 = \alpha_2(1 - \alpha_1) + (1 - \alpha_2) = \alpha_2 - \alpha_2\alpha_1 + 1 - \alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1\alpha_2 = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_2. \]

Therefore we can write the combined noise as \(\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_2}\,\bar{\varepsilon}\) where \(\bar{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), giving:

\[ \boxed{ x_2 = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_2}\,\bar{\varepsilon}, \quad \bar{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). } \]

This is exactly \(q(x_2 \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2}\,x_0,\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_2)I)\).

The general closed-form jump (induction)

The same "plug-in + variances-add" argument works for any number of steps. By induction, for all \(t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}\):

\[ q(x_t \mid x_0) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(x_t;\;\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0,\;(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\,I\bigr), \] or equivalently in reparameterized form: \[ x_t \;=\; \underbrace{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0}_{\text{attenuated signal}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon}_{\text{accumulated noise}}, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \]

Inductive step (sketch): suppose \(x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon'\). Apply one more kernel: \(x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon''\). Substitute, combine the two independent noise terms by Property B, and verify the combined variance is \(\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) + \beta_t = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_t\). QED.

Why this matters — training becomes trivially fast

Without this formula, producing a training example \((x_t, x_0)\) would require simulating the entire chain from step 1 to step \(t\) — up to 1000 forward passes. With the formula you need exactly one matrix-free operation: sample \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\), compute \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon\). The whole training loop lives at \(O(1)\) cost per step, regardless of \(T\).

5.3.3 Mini PyTorch example — verifying the closed form

# Verify the closed-form jump q(x_t | x_0) = N(sqrt(abar_t)*x0, (1-abar_t)*I)
# Teaching code — uses torch.manual_seed for reproducibility, not security.
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)  # reproducible teaching example

T      = 1000
d      = 4        # tiny dimension for fast inspection
N      = 100_000  # number of Monte Carlo samples

# --- Build the linear beta schedule (Ho et al. 2020 defaults) ---
beta   = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)   # shape: (T,)
alpha  = 1.0 - beta                      # shape: (T,)   alpha_t = 1 - beta_t
abar   = torch.cumprod(alpha, dim=0)     # shape: (T,)   abar_t = prod_{s=1}^t alpha_s

# --- Pick a specific time step to test ---
t_idx  = 499        # 0-indexed, so this is t = 500 in 1-indexed notation
ab_t   = abar[t_idx]
print(f"abar at t=500:   {ab_t:.4f}")   # expected: ~0.079 for linear schedule
print(f"1-abar at t=500: {1-ab_t:.4f}") # expected: ~0.92

# --- Fixed clean data point (same for all samples) ---
x0 = torch.ones(d) * 3.0   # x0 = [3, 3, 3, 3], shape: (d,)

# --- Method 1: simulate the full chain step-by-step (slow ground truth) ---
x_chain = x0.unsqueeze(0).expand(N, -1).clone()  # shape: (N, d)
for s in range(t_idx + 1):
    noise    = torch.randn_like(x_chain)
    x_chain  = alpha[s].sqrt() * x_chain + beta[s].sqrt() * noise
# x_chain[:,0] should be N(sqrt(abar_t)*3, 1-abar_t)
# Expected mean: sqrt(0.0786)*3 ~ 0.841,  expected var: ~0.921

# --- Method 2: closed-form jump (instant) ---
eps      = torch.randn(N, d)                            # shape: (N, d)
x_jump   = ab_t.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab_t).sqrt() * eps  # shape: (N, d)

# --- Compare statistics on dimension 0 ---
print(f"\nMethod 1 (full chain) — mean: {x_chain[:,0].mean():.3f}, "
      f"var: {x_chain[:,0].var():.3f}")
print(f"Method 2 (closed form) — mean: {x_jump[:,0].mean():.3f}, "
      f"var: {x_jump[:,0].var():.3f}")
print(f"Theory               — mean: {(ab_t.sqrt()*3.0):.3f}, "
      f"var: {(1-ab_t):.3f}")

# Expected output (approximate, varies with N and seed):
# abar at t=500:   0.0786
# 1-abar at t=500: 0.9214
# Method 1 (full chain) — mean: 0.847,  var: 0.923
# Method 2 (closed form) — mean: 0.844, var: 0.916
# Theory               — mean: 0.841,  var: 0.921
# The two methods give near-identical statistics.

5.4 The reverse chain — learning to undo one step

5.4.1 What the reverse chain looks like

The forward chain takes data and turns it into noise. To generate new data we need to run it in reverse: starting from pure noise \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), iteratively sample \(x_{T-1}, x_{T-2}, \ldots, x_0\). This requires knowing the reverse conditional:

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(x_{t-1};\;\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\;\Sigma_t\bigr). \]

The subscript \(\theta\) signals that the mean \(\mu_\theta\) is output by a learned neural network; the variance \(\Sigma_t\) is typically set to a fixed schedule (more on this below). The network sees \(x_t\) and the time index \(t\), and predicts the center of the Gaussian from which \(x_{t-1}\) should be sampled.

Intuition — why Gaussian reverse steps? When the forward-step variance \(\beta_t\) is small, Anderson (1982) and Feller (1949) show that the true reverse \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\) is also approximately Gaussian. DDPM exploits this: for small enough \(\beta_t\), parameterizing the reverse as a Gaussian is nearly lossless. This is why the schedule uses small \(\beta_t\) values (\(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\)).

5.4.2 The true reverse posterior \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\)

During training we have access to the clean \(x_0\), so we can compute the exact posterior via Bayes' rule:

\[ q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \;\propto\; q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\;q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0). \]

Both factors on the right are Gaussians (from the forward chain). The product of two Gaussians in \(x_{t-1}\) is also Gaussian — this is Gaussian conjugacy. After completing the square in \(x_{t-1}\) one obtains:

\[ q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(x_{t-1};\;\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0),\;\tilde{\beta}_t\,I\bigr), \]

with posterior mean and variance:

\[ \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 \;+\; \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_t, \] \[ \tilde{\beta}_t \;=\; \frac{(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}. \]
Equation breakdown — posterior mean \(\tilde{\mu}_t\)

The posterior mean is a weighted interpolation between two anchor points:

  • \(\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0\): a term that "reaches back" toward the clean data \(x_0\), weighted by how much noise has accumulated (\(\beta_t\)) and how far \(x_{t-1}\) would be from \(x_0\) on average (\(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\)).
  • \(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_t\): a term that "holds on" to the current noisy sample \(x_t\), weighted by how much of the variance at step \(t\) was already present at step \(t-1\) (\(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\)).

At early steps (large \(t\), almost pure noise) the \(x_t\) term dominates because \(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\) is large. At late steps (small \(t\), almost clean) the \(x_0\) term dominates because \(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1} \approx 1\) and \(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1} \approx 0\).

Equation breakdown — posterior variance \(\tilde{\beta}_t\)

\(\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\). This is always smaller than \(\beta_t\): conditioning on \(x_0\) reduces uncertainty. At early steps it is close to \(\beta_t\) (not much extra info from \(x_0\) when the data is already very noisy). At late steps it shrinks to near zero (knowing \(x_0\) pins down \(x_{t-1}\) almost exactly).

Why is this useful? This posterior is the target for the learned reverse step. If the network can reproduce \(\tilde{\mu}_t\), then the learned reverse \(p_\theta\) matches the true reverse, and sampling will produce real data.

5.5 The training objective — from ELBO to noise prediction

5.5.1 The variational bound (ELBO)

We want to maximize the log-likelihood \(\log p_\theta(x_0)\). Since the latent chain \(x_1, \ldots, x_T\) is unobserved, we lower-bound it by the ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund), the same trick as in VAEs:

\[ \log p_\theta(x_0) \;\geq\; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\right]. \]

After expanding \(p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) and \(q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\), and using standard KL-divergence algebra, the ELBO decomposes into three additive terms:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}} \;=\; -\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(x_T\mid x_0)\;\|\;p(x_T)\bigr)}_{L_T,\;\text{prior matching}} \;-\; \sum_{t=2}^{T} \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\;\|\;p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\bigr)}_{L_{t-1},\;\text{denoising terms}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)\right]}_{L_0,\;\text{reconstruction}}. \]
ELBO decomposition — term by term
  • \(L_T\): how well does the final noisy distribution \(q(x_T \mid x_0)\) match the prior \(p(x_T) = \mathcal{N}(0,I)\)? Since the schedule is fixed (no parameters), this term is a constant and can be ignored during training.
  • \(L_{t-1}\) for \(t = 2, \ldots, T\): KL divergence between the (intractable) true reverse \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\) and the learned reverse \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\). Both are Gaussians, so the KL has a closed form. This is where all the learning happens.
  • \(L_0\): reconstruction of \(x_0\) from \(x_1\); in practice often handled separately or folded into the main loss.

5.5.2 From KL between Gaussians to matching means

The KL divergence between two Gaussians with the same covariance is proportional to the squared difference of their means. Both \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\) and \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) are Gaussian; DDPM fixes \(\Sigma_t = \tilde{\beta}_t I\) (the posterior variance). Then:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\;\|\;p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\bigr) \;\propto\; \frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}_t} \bigl\|\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\bigr\|^2. \]

So minimizing \(L_{t-1}\) is equivalent to making the network's predicted mean \(\mu_\theta(x_t, t)\) match the true posterior mean \(\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0)\).

5.5.3 Reparameterize: predict noise instead of mean

Recall that \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon\). Solving for \(x_0\):

\[ x_0 \;=\; \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}. \]

Substitute this into the posterior mean formula \(\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0)\). After algebra (which carefully collects terms in \(x_t\) and \(\varepsilon\)):

\[ \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \!\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\varepsilon\right). \]

This is the key rewrite. Instead of directly predicting \(\tilde{\mu}_t\), the network can equivalently predict the noise \(\varepsilon\) and compute the mean implicitly. Define the noise-predicting network:

\[ \mu_\theta(x_t, t) \;:=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \!\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right). \]

Then the per-step loss \(L_{t-1}\) becomes (up to a constant prefactor):

\[ L_{t-1} \;\propto\; \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,\varepsilon}\! \left[ \left\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\!\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon,\;t\right)\right\|^2 \right]. \]

5.5.4 The simple loss — \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}}\)

Ho et al. (2020) found empirically that dropping the time-dependent weighting \(\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\tilde{\beta}_t \alpha_t (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\) and training with equal weight across all \(t\) gives better sample quality. The final training objective is:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}\{1,T\},\;\; x_0 \sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\;\; \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)} \!\left[ \left\| \varepsilon \;-\; \varepsilon_\theta\!\bigl( \underbrace{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon}_{= x_t}, \;t \bigr) \right\|^2 \right]. \]
Every symbol in \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}}\)
SymbolMeaning
\(t \sim \mathcal{U}\{1, T\}\)time step sampled uniformly from \(\{1, 2, \ldots, T\}\)
\(x_0 \sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\)clean training example drawn from the dataset
\(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\)fresh standard Gaussian noise, same shape as \(x_0\)
\(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon\)the noisy sample \(x_t\), computed in one shot via the closed-form jump
\(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\)network's predicted noise; input is the noisy sample and the time index, output has the same shape as \(\varepsilon\)
\(\|\cdot\|^2\)sum of squared differences over all dimensions of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (MSE up to constant \(1/d\))

Training in words: (1) pick a random clean example; (2) pick a random time step; (3) sample noise; (4) corrupt the example with that noise; (5) ask the network to guess what noise was added; (6) update toward making the guess more accurate.

Warning — why dropping the weighting works (and when it might not)

The ELBO derivation gives each \(L_{t-1}\) a weight \(\propto \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\tilde{\beta}_t\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\). This weight is huge at small \(t\) (since \(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1} \to 0\)). Ho et al. drop it, setting all weights to 1 — which down-weights the low-\(t\) (nearly-clean) terms the ELBO weights enormously, and relatively up-weights the high-noise (large-\(t\)) steps. This loss re-balancing shifts gradient signal toward the harder denoising tasks at large \(t\). In practice it improves perceptual quality (FID score), because those high-noise steps are where the perceptually hard work happens. However, if you care about exact likelihood estimation (bits-per-dimension), use the full ELBO weighting — the simple loss is not a valid likelihood lower bound.

5.6 The score ↔ ε ↔ x₀ triangle revisited

In Part 3 we drew a triangle of three equivalent prediction targets. DDPM completes that triangle in the discrete-time setting.

The score function of the conditional \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\) satisfies (Gaussian conditional score, as derived in Part 3):

\[ \nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) \;=\; -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\varepsilon, \quad\text{where } x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon, \]

and by the Tweedie formula the score of the marginal \(q_t(x_t)\) is the posterior expectation of this: \(\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t) = -\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]/\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\), which is exactly what \(\varepsilon_\theta\) approximates.

Therefore the noise-predicting network \(\varepsilon_\theta\) is related to the score by:

\[ \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \;\approx\; -\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\;\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t). \]
Connection — MIT notes notation

The MIT notes (§4.3, Example 23) use the continuous-time Gaussian probability path \(p_t(x \mid z) = \mathcal{N}(\alpha_t z,\, \beta_t^2 I)\) with the noise-predictor re-parameterization \(\varepsilon_\theta^t \approx -\beta_t\,s_\theta^t\), where \(s_\theta^t = \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) is the score network. In DDPM notation: \(\alpha_t^{\mathrm{notes}} \leftrightarrow \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}^{\mathrm{DDPM}}\) and \(\beta_t^{\mathrm{notes}} \leftrightarrow \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}^{\mathrm{DDPM}}\). The identity \(\varepsilon_\theta = -\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)\) is the discrete analogue of Equation (55) in the notes. In both cases: learning to predict noise is the same as learning the score.

The score ↔ ε ↔ x₀ triangle (DDPM version)

All three prediction targets are algebraically equivalent given \(x_t\) and \(t\):

  • Predict \(\varepsilon\): \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t) \approx \varepsilon\). DDPM's default. Numerically stable; used in \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}}\).
  • Predict \(x_0\): \(x_0\text{-network} \approx \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\). Recovers clean data directly; used in some later works (DDIM, \(v\)-prediction).
  • Predict the score: \(s_\theta(x_t,t) \approx \nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t) = -\varepsilon / \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\). Connects to the SDE / continuous-time formulation.

Any one of the three can be converted to the other two via the closed-form jump formula. The choice affects only numerical conditioning, not expressiveness.

5.7 The sampling algorithm

5.7.1 Deriving the reverse-step update

Given a trained \(\varepsilon_\theta\), to generate data we run the reverse chain. At each step \(t\) we compute the posterior mean estimate using the network's predicted noise, then add a small amount of fresh noise to maintain stochasticity:

\[ x_{t-1} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \!\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right)}_{\text{estimated mean }\mu_\theta(x_t,t)} \;+\; \underbrace{\sqrt{\beta_t}\,z}_{\text{added noise}}, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),\;\;z = 0 \text{ when } t = 1. \]
Equation breakdown — sampling step
TermWhat it does
\(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\)undoes the signal scaling from the forward step \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\)
\(-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\)subtracts the estimated noise, scaled so the result is in the right units
\(\sqrt{\beta_t}\,z\)fresh noise injection; its variance matches \(\tilde{\beta}_t \approx \beta_t\) at large \(t\), ensuring the distribution remains calibrated
\(z = 0\) at \(t=1\)at the very last step we are generating \(x_0\) so we do not add noise — taking the mean produces a deterministic clean output

Intuition for the mean term: the network predicts \(\varepsilon\), so we subtract the "estimated noise content" from \(x_t\) and scale back to the appropriate magnitude for \(x_{t-1}\). This is analogous to one step of gradient descent on the (negative log) data density — the score viewpoint.

Intuition for the added noise: the true reverse posterior \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\) has variance \(\tilde{\beta}_t > 0\). Adding \(\sqrt{\beta_t}\,z\) approximates sampling from a Gaussian centered at \(\mu_\theta\), which is necessary for \(p_\theta\) to match the true reverse. Without it (i.e. always taking the mean), the model would be deterministic and could not model multi-modal distributions at large \(t\).

5.7.2 Full sampling algorithm

Algorithm — DDPM Sampling
  1. Sample \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\).
  2. For \(t = T, T-1, \ldots, 2\):
    1. Sample \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\).
    2. Compute \(\hat{\varepsilon} = \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\).
    3. Update: \(x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\hat{\varepsilon}\right) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,z\).
  3. For \(t = 1\): compute \(x_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_1}}\!\left(x_1 - \frac{\beta_1}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_1}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_1, 1)\right)\) (no noise added).
  4. Return \(x_0\).

Each forward pass of \(\varepsilon_\theta\) requires one network evaluation. Total cost: \(T = 1000\) network calls per generated sample (DDIM in Part 6 reduces this dramatically).

5.8 Practical notes — schedules, time embeddings, and architecture

Practical notes

Noise schedules. Ho et al. (2020) used a linear schedule: \(\beta_t\) grows linearly from \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\) over \(T=1000\) steps. Nichol & Dhariwal (2021) found that at low resolutions (64×64, 32×32) the linear schedule destroys information too quickly near \(t=T\), wasting compute on steps that are already pure noise. They introduced the cosine schedule:

\[ \bar{\alpha}_t \;=\; \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \quad f(t) = \cos\!\left(\frac{t/T + s}{1 + s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2}\right)^2, \quad s = 0.008. \]

This gives a smoother, more gradual decay of signal-to-noise ratio and typically improves log-likelihood and sample quality.

Number of steps \(T\). \(T = 1000\) is the DDPM default. Larger \(T\) gives more accurate approximation of the continuous SDE limit but is slower to sample. DDIM (Part 6) shows that sampling quality is acceptable with far fewer steps post-training.

Time embedding. The network \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) must accept the scalar time index \(t\) as a second input. In practice, \(t\) is converted to a high-dimensional sinusoidal embedding (analogous to positional encodings in transformers): \[ \mathrm{emb}(t) = [\sin(\omega_1 t), \cos(\omega_1 t), \ldots, \sin(\omega_k t), \cos(\omega_k t)] \in \mathbb{R}^{2k}, \] then projected through a small MLP and added to each residual block of the UNet. This lets the same set of weights serve as the denoiser at every step.

Why one network for all \(t\)? The task at step \(t=999\) (recover signal from near-pure noise) is structurally different from the task at \(t=1\) (remove a tiny hair of noise from an almost-clean image). Yet both tasks share the same weights. This works because the time embedding provides sufficient context — the network learns a family of conditional denoisers indexed by \(t\), analogous to a conditional neural field.

UNet architecture. The canonical backbone is a UNet with residual blocks, group normalization, and self-attention layers at lower resolutions. The UNet's skip connections help the network use both coarse and fine features simultaneously, which is important because different noise levels require denoising at different spatial scales.

5.9 PyTorch implementation — loss and verification

5.9.1 Building the schedule and verifying statistics

The code below builds \(\alpha\), \(\bar{\alpha}\) from a linear \(\beta\) schedule and confirms the closed-form jump matches a full-chain simulation (same experiment as Section 5.3.3, reproduced here as a stand-alone snippet for reference). 

# Part A: Build the noise schedule and verify q(x_t | x_0) statistics.
# Teaching code — RNG seeded for reproducibility, not security.
import torch

def make_schedule(T: int = 1000,
                  beta_start: float = 1e-4,
                  beta_end: float = 0.02) -> dict:
    """
    Returns a dict of pre-computed schedule tensors.
    All tensors have shape (T,) and are 0-indexed:
      index 0  corresponds to t=1 in the paper,
      index T-1 corresponds to t=T.
    """
    beta  = torch.linspace(beta_start, beta_end, T)    # (T,)  beta_t
    alpha = 1.0 - beta                                  # (T,)  alpha_t = 1 - beta_t
    abar  = torch.cumprod(alpha, dim=0)                 # (T,)  abar_t = prod_{s=1}^t alpha_s
    return {"beta": beta, "alpha": alpha, "abar": abar}

sched = make_schedule()
print("beta  range:", sched["beta"].min().item(), "to", sched["beta"].max().item())
# expected: 0.0001 to 0.02
print("abar  range:", sched["abar"].min().item(), "to", sched["abar"].max().item())
# expected: ~0.0 to ~0.9999  (abar_T close to 0, abar_1 close to 1)
print("abar at t=1  :", sched["abar"][0].item())    # ~0.9999
print("abar at t=500:", sched["abar"][499].item())  # ~0.079
print("abar at t=999:", sched["abar"][998].item())  # ~0.00004  (almost 0)

5.9.2 The training loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}}\)

# Part B: Minimal training loss implementing L_simple.
# Teaching code — RNG seeded for reproducibility, not security.
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(42)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# Stub model: a tiny linear network that accepts (x_t, t_emb)
# concatenated and outputs noise of the same shape as x_t.
# In a real DDPM this would be a time-conditioned UNet.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
class StubEpsNet(nn.Module):
    """
    Minimal noise-predicting network for illustration.
    Input:  x_t  of shape (B, d)
            t    of shape (B,) containing integer time indices in [0, T-1]
    Output: predicted eps of shape (B, d)
    """
    def __init__(self, d: int, T: int, emb_dim: int = 16):
        super().__init__()
        self.T       = T
        self.emb_dim = emb_dim
        # Learnable time embedding table: one vector per time step
        self.time_emb = nn.Embedding(T, emb_dim)       # shape: (T, emb_dim)
        # Simple MLP: [d + emb_dim] -> [128] -> [d]
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d + emb_dim, 128),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, d),
        )

    def forward(self, x_t: torch.Tensor, t: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # x_t: (B, d),  t: (B,) int64
        emb  = self.time_emb(t)                        # (B, emb_dim)
        inp  = torch.cat([x_t, emb], dim=-1)           # (B, d + emb_dim)
        return self.net(inp)                            # (B, d)


def compute_loss(model: nn.Module,
                 x0: torch.Tensor,
                 sched: dict) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Compute L_simple for a batch of clean examples x0.

    Args:
        model : noise-predicting network eps_theta(x_t, t)
        x0    : clean data batch, shape (B, d)
        sched : schedule dict from make_schedule()

    Returns:
        scalar loss tensor (MSE averaged over batch and dimensions)
    """
    B, d = x0.shape
    T    = sched["abar"].shape[0]

    # --- Step 1: sample a random time step for each example in the batch ---
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (B,), dtype=torch.long)    # (B,)  values in {0, ..., T-1}

    # --- Step 2: sample noise ---
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)                          # (B, d)  eps ~ N(0, I)

    # --- Step 3: form the noisy sample via the closed-form jump ---
    abar_t  = sched["abar"][t]                          # (B,)   scalar abar per example
    # Reshape to (B, 1) so broadcasting works with (B, d) tensors
    sqrt_ab  = abar_t.sqrt().unsqueeze(1)               # (B, 1)
    sqrt_1ab = (1.0 - abar_t).sqrt().unsqueeze(1)       # (B, 1)
    x_t = sqrt_ab * x0 + sqrt_1ab * eps                 # (B, d)  noisy sample

    # --- Step 4: predict the noise ---
    eps_pred = model(x_t, t)                            # (B, d)  eps_theta(x_t, t)

    # --- Step 5: MSE between true noise and predicted noise ---
    loss = ((eps - eps_pred) ** 2).mean()               # scalar
    return loss


# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# Quick sanity check — untrained model, random data
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
d     = 8          # data dimension (e.g. 8 features)
B     = 32         # batch size
T     = 1000
sched = make_schedule(T=T)

model = StubEpsNet(d=d, T=T)
x0    = torch.randn(B, d)                               # (32, 8)  fake clean data

print(f"loss (untrained): {loss.item():.4f}")
# Expected: ~1.0 (for an untrained network predicting zero, MSE ≈ E[eps^2] = 1.0)

5.9.3 Sampling loop (inference)

# Sampling loop — runs the reverse chain to generate x0 from x_T ~ N(0,I).
# Teaching code — RNG seeded for reproducibility, not security.
import torch

@torch.no_grad()
def ddpm_sample(model: nn.Module,
                sched: dict,
                shape: tuple,
                device: str = "cpu") -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    Generate a batch of samples using the DDPM reverse chain.

    Args:
        model  : trained eps_theta network
        sched  : schedule dict from make_schedule()
        shape  : (B, d) tuple — batch size and data dimension
        device : 'cpu' or 'cuda'

    Returns:
        x0_gen : generated samples, shape (B, d)
    """
    T     = sched["beta"].shape[0]
    beta  = sched["beta"].to(device)
    alpha = sched["alpha"].to(device)
    abar  = sched["abar"].to(device)

    # --- Start from pure noise ---
    x = torch.randn(*shape, device=device)              # (B, d)  x_T ~ N(0, I)

    for t_idx in reversed(range(T)):                    # t = T-1, T-2, ..., 0
        t_batch = torch.full((shape[0],), t_idx,
                             dtype=torch.long, device=device)  # (B,)

        # --- Predict noise ---
        eps_hat = model(x, t_batch)                     # (B, d)

        # --- Compute the mean of p_theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) ---
        coeff   = beta[t_idx] / (1.0 - abar[t_idx]).sqrt()    # scalar: beta_t / sqrt(1-abar_t)
        mean    = (x - coeff * eps_hat) / alpha[t_idx].sqrt() # (B, d)

        # --- Add stochastic noise (skip at t=0, the final step) ---
        if t_idx > 0:
            z    = torch.randn_like(x)                  # (B, d)  fresh noise
            x    = mean + beta[t_idx].sqrt() * z        # (B, d)  x_{t-1}
        else:
            x    = mean                                 # final step: no noise added

    return x                                            # (B, d)  generated x_0


# --- Quick sanity check with stub (untrained) model ---
torch.manual_seed(7)
d, B   = 8, 4
sched  = make_schedule(T=1000)
model  = StubEpsNet(d=d, T=1000)
x_gen  = ddpm_sample(model, sched, shape=(B, d))
print(f"Generated shape: {x_gen.shape}")               # expected: torch.Size([4, 8])
print(f"Mean: {x_gen.mean():.3f},  Std: {x_gen.std():.3f}")
# With an untrained model the output will NOT look like data — it BLOWS UP.
# If eps_hat ~ 0, each step computes x_{t-1} = x_t / sqrt(alpha_t) + sqrt(beta_t)*z,
# and the 1/sqrt(alpha_t) factors compound to 1/sqrt(abar_T) ~ 157 over the chain,
# so the std is amplified to ~10^2-10^3. Only a trained eps_theta cancels this
# amplification; after training, x_gen's mean/std should match the data distribution.
# Expected for untrained: Mean ~ O(10), Std ~ O(100-1000) (huge — this is normal!)

5.10 End-to-end connection to the MIT notes

Connection — DDPM in the MIT notes framework

The MIT notes (Appendix E / Summary 45) describe DDPM as a special case of the continuous-time SDE framework with the following correspondences:

  • Forward process: the Markov chain \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\) is a discretization of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SDE \(dX_t = -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t) X_t\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t\), where the continuous variance schedule \(\beta(t)\) interpolates the discrete values.
  • Probability path: the notes' Gaussian path \(p_t(x \mid z) = \mathcal{N}(\alpha_t z, \beta_t^2 I)\) matches \(q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)I)\) under the identification \(\alpha_t^{\text{notes}} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t^{\text{DDPM}}}\), \(\beta_t^{\text{notes}} = \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t^{\text{DDPM}}}\).
  • Training loss: \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}}\) is the denoising score matching loss (Theorem 22 in the notes, §4.3) with the weighting dropped. The notes state explicitly that dropping \(1/\beta_t^2\) (the numerical instability factor near \(\beta_t \to 0\)) was the key practical contribution of Ho et al.
  • Sampling: the DDPM reverse step is one Euler–Maruyama step of the reverse SDE from Theorem 17 in the notes, with diffusion coefficient \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{\beta_t}\).
  • Inverted time convention: the notes use \(t=0\) for clean data and \(t=1\) for noise; DDPM uses \(t=0\) for clean and \(t=T\) for noise. When reading papers, always check which convention is in use.
