Part 6DDIM — Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models

DDPM (Part 5) taught us the fundamental loop: corrupt data with noise, train a network to predict that noise, then reverse the process step by step. The catch: DDPM needs ~1000 sequential neural-network evaluations to produce one sample, and every run is stochastic — the same noise seed gives a different image. DDIM fixes both problems without retraining anything. It is also the bridge to the continuous probability-flow ODE you will meet in Part 9. Read this part after completing Part 5.

Prerequisites
  • Part 5 — DDPM in full: the forward process \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\), the closed-form reparameterization \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\), the \(\varepsilon\)-prediction loss, and DDPM ancestral sampling.
  • Part 1 — the intuition that an ODE integrator moves a point along a deterministic trajectory (we will need this for §6.8).

6.1 The Problem: DDPM is Slow and Stochastic

Recall the DDPM ancestral sampler. To generate one image it runs a loop:

x_T  ~  N(0, I)                        # start from pure noise
for t in [T, T-1, T-2, ..., 1]:
    eps_pred = eps_theta(x_t, t)       # one neural-network forward pass
    z ~ N(0, I)  if t > 1 else z = 0  # fresh stochastic noise
    x_{t-1} = (compute reverse step)   # one denoising update
return x_0                             # generated image

With \(T = 1000\) that is 1000 network evaluations — roughly 3–30 seconds per image on a typical GPU, depending on model size. There are two separate annoyances packed into this loop:

  1. Speed. 1000 sequential steps cannot be parallelised; each step must finish before the next starts.
  2. Stochasticity. Each call to \(\varepsilon_\theta\) is followed by injecting fresh noise \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\), so different runs from the same \(x_T\) produce different \(x_0\). You cannot deterministically reproduce or interpolate between outputs.
Intuition — the dream sampler Imagine you could tell your DDPM model: "Take only 50 steps instead of 1000, jump over the ones in between, and make the whole thing deterministic — same noise in, same image out, every time." That is exactly what DDIM does. The punchline: the model weights never change. You only swap the sampling algorithm that uses those weights.

6.2 The Key Idea: Many Forward Processes, Same Marginals

This is the conceptual core of Song et al. (2020). Take a moment to absorb it before we look at equations.

The DDPM training loss (the \(\varepsilon\)-prediction MSE) only ever uses the marginal distributions \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\) — the conditional distribution of the noisy sample \(x_t\) given the clean image \(x_0\). Recall that those marginals have the closed form:

\[ q(x_t \mid x_0) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\;(1-\bar\alpha_t)I\bigr), \qquad \bar\alpha_t \;=\; \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s). \]

Crucially, this is the marginal at time \(t\) given \(x_0\). The joint distribution \(q(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T \mid x_0)\) — the trajectory — is not constrained by the loss at all. DDPM uses a particular joint (a Markov chain), but there are infinitely many other joints that share the exact same marginals.

Core observation (Song et al. 2020) Because the \(\varepsilon\)-prediction loss only depends on the marginals \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\), any forward process that shares those marginals produces the same trained \(\varepsilon_\theta\). DDIM picks a non-Markovian forward process with the same marginals whose reverse step can be made deterministic (or partially so via a scalar \(\eta\)).

Slogan: same training, new sampler.

We do not need to re-derive the forward process in full — the key consequence is the DDIM update rule, which we build up next.

6.4 The \(\hat x_0\) Prediction: Seeing the Clean Image at Every Step

This is the heart of DDIM. We will derive it line by line.

Starting point: the DDPM closed form

From Part 5 you know that, given a clean image \(x_0\) and a random noise vector \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\), you can jump directly to any noisy \(x_t\) via:

\[ x_t \;=\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\;x_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon. \tag{closed form} \]

This is the reparameterization trick applied to \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\). It holds exactly for every \(t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}\) and involves only two scalars \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) and \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\).

Deriving \(\hat x_0\)

During sampling we do not have \(x_0\) — that is what we are trying to generate. We only have \(x_t\) and the trained predictor \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\). Treat the closed-form equation as an algebraic equation in \(x_0\) and solve:

\[ x_t \;=\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\;x_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t) \] \[ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\;x_0 \;=\; x_t \;-\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t) \] \[ \boxed{ \hat x_0 \;=\; \frac{x_t \;-\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} } \tag{x̂₀ formula} \]
Equation breakdown — the \(\hat x_0\) formula
  • \(x_t\) — the noisy latent we currently hold (shape \(\mathbb{R}^d\)).
  • \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) — the network's prediction of the noise that was mixed into \(x_0\) to produce \(x_t\) (shape \(\mathbb{R}^d\)).
  • \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) — the predicted noise component; subtract it from \(x_t\) to isolate the signal.
  • Dividing by \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) undoes the scaling that the forward process applied to \(x_0\).
  • Result: \(\hat x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\), an estimate of the clean image. It improves at each step as \(x_t\) gets cleaner (smaller \(t\)).
Intuition — a moving estimate Think of \(\hat x_0\) as a blurry photograph that gets progressively sharper as sampling proceeds. At \(t = T\) (pure noise), \(\hat x_0\) is just a rough smear. At \(t = 1\) (nearly clean), \(\hat x_0\) is essentially the final image. DDIM uses this estimate at every intermediate step, even the coarse ones, to compute its update direction.

A minimal PyTorch illustration of the \(\hat x_0\) formula:

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)  # teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG
d = 4  # toy dimension (real images: d = H*W*C)

# Suppose we are at timestep t with these (pretend) scalars
alpha_bar_t = torch.tensor(0.36)   # sqrt(alpha_bar_t)^2 = 0.36 → sqrt = 0.6

# A fake x_t and a fake noise prediction (same shape as data)
x_t      = torch.randn(d)          # shape (4,)  — the noisy sample
eps_pred = torch.randn(d)          # shape (4,)  — eps_theta(x_t, t)

# The x̂_0 formula:
sqrt_ab   = alpha_bar_t.sqrt()           # √ᾱ_t,  scalar
sqrt_1mab = (1 - alpha_bar_t).sqrt()     # √(1-ᾱ_t), scalar

x0_hat = (x_t - sqrt_1mab * eps_pred) / sqrt_ab
print("x_t    :", x_t)
print("eps    :", eps_pred)
print("x̂_0   :", x0_hat)
# Expected (values depend on seed=0):
# x_t    : tensor([ 1.5410, -0.2934, -2.1788,  0.5684])
# eps    : tensor([-1.0845, -1.3986,  0.4033,  0.8380])
# x̂_0   : tensor([ 4.0144,  1.3757, -4.1691, -0.1700])

6.5 The DDIM Update Equation

Now we can write the full DDIM sampling step, which is Equation 12 in Song et al. (2020). This is how we go from \(x_t\) to \(x_{t-1}\) (or more generally, from one timestep in the schedule \(\tau\) to the previous).

\[ \boxed{ x_{t-1} \;=\; \underbrace{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\hat x_0}_{\text{(1) clean target at level }t-1} \;+\; \underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}_{\text{(2) direction toward }x_t} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma_t\;z}_{\text{(3) fresh noise}}, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I). } \tag{DDIM update} \]
Equation breakdown — each of the three terms

Term (1): \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\hat x_0\)
Take the model's current estimate of the clean image, \(\hat x_0\), and re-scale it to the signal level expected at time \(t-1\). This is exactly what the forward process does: at timestep \(t-1\), the signal part of \(x_{t-1}\) has amplitude \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\). Shape: \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

Term (2): \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\)
This is the "direction pointing toward \(x_t\)." At timestep \(t-1\), the total variance of the noise component should be \(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}\). We will put \(\sigma_t^2\) of that variance into fresh noise (term 3), leaving \(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2\) for the deterministic noise direction. The direction itself is \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) — the same prediction we already computed for term (1). Shape: \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

Term (3): \(\sigma_t\,z\), \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\)
Optionally inject \(\sigma_t\) standard deviations of fresh Gaussian noise. When \(\sigma_t = 0\) this term vanishes entirely and the update is deterministic. Shape: \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

The three terms are simply added. The total expected variance of the noise component of \(x_{t-1}\) is \((1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2) + \sigma_t^2 = 1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}\), which matches the forward-process marginal \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)\). The marginals are preserved exactly.

Intuition — three steps in words
  1. Predict where the clean image probably is right now (\(\hat x_0\)).
  2. Rescale that prediction to sit at the right noise level for time \(t-1\) — the deterministic backbone of the update.
  3. Optionally sprinkle a little fresh noise on top (controlled by \(\eta\)).
Compare DDPM ancestral sampling: step 2 is there, but step 3 is always non-zero in DDPM and its magnitude is fixed by the Markovian structure. DDIM gives you a knob.

6.6 The \(\eta\) Knob: Deterministic to Stochastic

The noise scale \(\sigma_t\) is parameterized by a single scalar \(\eta \in [0,1]\) as follows (Song et al., Eq. 16):

\[ \sigma_t \;=\; \eta \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}} \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}. \tag{sigma formula} \]

Let us unpack the two square-root factors:

FactorWhat it captures
\(\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\) Ratio of noise levels at \(t-1\) vs \(t\). When steps are small, this is close to 1. Ensures the noise scale stays commensurate with the overall noise budget.
\(\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}\) How much new noise was added going from \(t-1\) to \(t\) in the forward process. This is the per-step "differential" noise. Small when steps are small; vanishes when \(\bar\alpha_t = \bar\alpha_{t-1}\).

The two limits

\(\eta = 1\): recover DDPM When \(\eta = 1\), the formula gives exactly the DDPM posterior standard deviation: \(\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t\), i.e. \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\) (using \(1 - \bar\alpha_t/\bar\alpha_{t-1} = \beta_t\)). The DDIM update becomes exactly DDPM ancestral sampling. Term (3) is non-zero; each run from the same \(x_T\) gives a different \(x_0\).
\(\eta = 0\): fully deterministic DDIM When \(\eta = 0\), \(\sigma_t = 0\) for all \(t\). Term (3) vanishes. The update collapses to: \[ x_{t-1} \;=\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\hat x_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t). \] This is a deterministic map: the same \(x_T\) always produces the same \(x_0\). No randomness is introduced after the initial sample \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\).
Warning — \(\eta=0\) on weak models Fully deterministic sampling (\(\eta=0\)) compounds every per-step prediction error from \(\varepsilon_\theta\) without the stochastic self-correction that noise injection provides. On an undertrained or small model, \(\eta=0\) can produce slightly blurrier or more artifact-prone images than \(\eta=1\) (which reproduces DDPM quality). On a well-trained large model the two are visually comparable, and \(\eta=0\) is often preferred because of its reproducibility and fast convergence with fewer steps. Practical advice: start with \(\eta=0\) for speed; if quality suffers, try \(\eta \in (0, 1]\).

6.7 Accelerated Sampling: The Strided Schedule

DDIM's big practical payoff: you do not have to visit all 1000 timesteps. The DDIM update at time \(t\) only references \(\bar\alpha_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_{t-1}\) — not any intermediate values. So you can freely choose a subset \(\tau \subset \{1, 2, \ldots, T\}\) and run the update jumping from one element of \(\tau\) to the previous.

Intuition — why skipping works Think of the full 1000-step trajectory as a film reel with 1000 frames. DDPM ancestral sampling watches every frame sequentially. DDIM with 50 steps watches only every 20th frame — but instead of just displaying the frame, it re-computes from scratch where the story is at the skipped position using \(\hat x_0\) as a guide. The model was trained to predict noise at any single timestep independently, so it can still evaluate at the strided positions without any retraining.

The most common choice is a uniform stride:

\[ \tau \;=\; \Bigl\{T,\; T - \lfloor T/S \rfloor,\; T - 2\lfloor T/S \rfloor,\;\ldots,\;\lfloor T/S \rfloor\Bigr\}, \quad |\tau| = S. \]

For \(T=1000\), \(S=50\): stride \(= 20\), so \(\tau = \{1000, 980, 960, \ldots, 20, 0\}\) — the schedule must terminate at \(t = 0\) (where \(\bar\alpha_0 = 1\)), otherwise the returned sample still carries the residual noise of level \(t=20\). That is 50 update steps instead of 1000.

The DDIM update formula is unchanged; you simply replace the pair \((t, t-1)\) with the pair \((\tau_i, \tau_{i-1})\) for consecutive elements in your chosen schedule. \(\bar\alpha\) values at the chosen timesteps are looked up from the precomputed array.

import torch

T = 1000
S = 50
stride = T // S  # = 20

# Build the strided schedule: from T down to stride, then a final step to 0
tau = list(range(T, 0, -stride)) + [0]  # [1000, 980, 960, ..., 20, 0]
print(f"First 5 steps: {tau[:5]}")  # [1000, 980, 960, 940, 920]
print(f"Last 5 steps : {tau[-5:]}")  # [80, 60, 40, 20, 0]
print(f"Total levels : {len(tau)}")  # 51 levels → 50 update steps

# At sampling time, iterate:
# for i in range(len(tau) - 1):
#     t      = tau[i]       # current noisy level
#     t_prev = tau[i+1]     # target level (less noisy)
#     x_t_prev = ddim_step(x_t, t, t_prev, eps_pred, alpha_bar, eta)

6.8 Determinism Unlocks Geometry: Interpolation and Inversion

When \(\eta = 0\), the DDIM sampler is a deterministic function \(f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\), mapping noise vectors to images:

\[ x_0 \;=\; f_\theta(x_T), \quad x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I). \]

Because \(f_\theta\) is deterministic, we can do things that are impossible with stochastic samplers:

Three consequences of determinism
  1. Reproducibility. Fix \(x_T\); always get the same image. You can store a single noise vector and regenerate the same output.
  2. Semantic interpolation. Take two noise vectors \(x_T^{(A)}\) and \(x_T^{(B)}\) that map to images A and B. Linearly interpolate in noise space: \(x_T^{(\lambda)} = (1-\lambda)\,x_T^{(A)} + \lambda\,x_T^{(B)}\) and decode. The resulting image smoothly blends the semantic content of A and B. This works because \(f_\theta\) acts like a smooth coordinate system over the image manifold.
  3. (Approximate) inversion. Given a real image \(x_0\), run DDIM forward (i.e., solve the update equation for \(x_t\) given \(x_{t-1}\)) to find the noise vector \(x_T\) that the deterministic decoder would map back to (approximately) \(x_0\). This "DDIM inversion" is used heavily in image editing pipelines (e.g. prompt-to-prompt, null-text inversion). By contrast, SDEdit does not invert: it stochastically adds forward noise up to a partial level and re-denoises.

6.9 The Bridge to ODEs (Connection to Part 9)

Connection — DDIM is a discrete ODE integrator

Look at the \(\eta=0\) DDIM update:

\[ x_{t-1} \;=\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\hat x_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t), \qquad \hat x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}. \]

Substituting \(\hat x_0\) and re-arranging, one can show that this update is exactly an Euler step of the probability-flow ODE derived by Song et al. (2020):

\[ dx \;=\; \Bigl[ f_t(x) \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}g_t^2\,\nabla_{x}\log p_t(x) \Bigr]\,dt, \]

where \(f_t\) and \(g_t\) come from the VP-SDE (variance-preserving SDE) that corresponds to the DDPM noise schedule, and the score \(\nabla_{x}\log p_t(x)\) is approximated by \(-\varepsilon_\theta / \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\). To be precise, the Euler step is taken after the change of variables \(\tilde x = x / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) with the noise clock \(\bar\sigma_t = \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\): in those coordinates the linear drift vanishes and the ODE becomes simply \(d\tilde x / d\bar\sigma = \hat\varepsilon\), and DDIM is its Euler step — not a plain \(t\)-Euler step of the equation above.

What this means in plain language:

  • DDIM with \(\eta=0\) is the discrete shadow of a continuous ODE.
  • Using more DDIM steps = using a finer Euler grid = more accurate ODE integration.
  • Using fewer steps = coarser Euler steps = faster but less accurate.
  • You can swap the Euler integrator for higher-order ODE solvers (Heun, DPM-Solver, PNDM) and get even better quality with fewer function evaluations — all reusing the same trained \(\varepsilon_\theta\).

Part 9 will derive the probability-flow ODE from first principles and show this connection rigorously. Come back to this box after reading Part 9 — it will make everything click.

6.10 Runnable PyTorch: The Complete DDIM Step

Below is a self-contained implementation of a single DDIM step, heavily commented. We demonstrate both \(\eta=0\) and \(\eta=1\) and confirm they differ only in the noise term.

import torch

torch.manual_seed(42)  # teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────
# Helper: build a simple cosine alpha_bar schedule
# (mimics the DDPM cosine schedule; any schedule works)
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────
def cosine_alpha_bar(T: int) -> torch.Tensor:
    """Returns alpha_bar[0..T], shape (T+1,).
    alpha_bar[t] = cos^2(pi/2 * t/T * (1 + s)/(1 + s))  (simplified).
    """
    t = torch.arange(T + 1, dtype=torch.float32)
    f = torch.cos((t / T + 0.008) / 1.008 * 3.14159265 / 2) ** 2
    ab = f / f[0]          # normalize so alpha_bar[0] = 1
    return ab.clamp(min=1e-5)

T = 1000
alpha_bar = cosine_alpha_bar(T)
# alpha_bar[t] for t in 0..1000; shape (1001,)
# alpha_bar[0]    ≈ 1.0  (pure data)
# alpha_bar[1000] ≈ 0.0  (pure noise)

print(f"alpha_bar[0]    = {alpha_bar[0].item():.4f}")   # 1.0000
print(f"alpha_bar[500]  = {alpha_bar[500].item():.4f}")  # ~0.4938 (depends on schedule)
print(f"alpha_bar[1000] = {alpha_bar[1000].item():.4f}") # ~0.0001

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────
# Core function: one DDIM step
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────
def ddim_step(
    x_t:       torch.Tensor,   # current noisy sample,   shape (d,) or (B, d, ...)
    t:         int,            # current timestep index, integer in {1, ..., T}
    t_prev:    int,            # target  timestep index, integer in {0, ..., t-1}
    eps_pred:  torch.Tensor,   # epsilon_theta(x_t, t),  same shape as x_t
    alpha_bar: torch.Tensor,   # precomputed schedule,   shape (T+1,)
    eta:       float = 0.0,    # stochasticity: 0=deterministic, 1=DDPM
) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    One DDIM update: x_t  ->  x_{t_prev}.

    Returns x_{t_prev}, same shape as x_t.
    """
    ab_t    = alpha_bar[t]       # ᾱ_t,     scalar
    ab_prev = alpha_bar[t_prev]  # ᾱ_{t-1}, scalar

    sqrt_ab_t    = ab_t.sqrt()                 # √ᾱ_t
    sqrt_1mab_t  = (1.0 - ab_t).sqrt()        # √(1-ᾱ_t)
    sqrt_ab_prev = ab_prev.sqrt()              # √ᾱ_{t-1}
    sqrt_1mab_prev = (1.0 - ab_prev).sqrt()   # √(1-ᾱ_{t-1})

    # ── Step A: predict clean image x̂_0 ────────────
    #   x̂_0 = (x_t - √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε_θ) / √ᾱ_t
    x0_hat = (x_t - sqrt_1mab_t * eps_pred) / sqrt_ab_t
    # shape: same as x_t

    # ── Step B: compute sigma_t ─────────────────────
    #   σ_t = η · √((1-ᾱ_{t-1})/(1-ᾱ_t)) · √(1 - ᾱ_t/ᾱ_{t-1})
    ratio       = (1.0 - ab_prev) / (1.0 - ab_t)      # scalar
    diff_ratio  = (1.0 - ab_t / ab_prev).clamp(min=0)  # scalar; clamp for numerical safety
    sigma_t     = eta * ratio.sqrt() * diff_ratio.sqrt()  # scalar

    # ── Step C: deterministic noise coefficient ────
    #   √(1 - ᾱ_{t-1} - σ_t²) · ε_θ
    det_coeff = (1.0 - ab_prev - sigma_t ** 2).clamp(min=0).sqrt()
    # shape: scalar

    # ── Step D: fresh noise (zero when eta=0) ──────
    z = torch.randn_like(x_t) if eta > 0 else torch.zeros_like(x_t)
    # shape: same as x_t

    # ── Assemble the three terms ───────────────────
    # Term 1: √ᾱ_{t-1} · x̂_0            (clean image rescaled)
    # Term 2: det_coeff · ε_θ            (deterministic noise direction)
    # Term 3: σ_t · z                    (fresh stochastic noise)
    x_prev = (
        sqrt_ab_prev    * x0_hat      # term 1
        + det_coeff     * eps_pred    # term 2
        + sigma_t       * z           # term 3
    )
    return x_prev

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────
# Demonstration: one step with a stub ε_θ
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────
d = 8  # toy vector dimension

x_t      = torch.randn(d)   # fake noisy latent,       shape (8,)
eps_stub = torch.randn(d)   # fake noise prediction,   shape (8,)
t      = 500
t_prev = 480  # one stride-20 step back

x_det = ddim_step(x_t, t, t_prev, eps_stub, alpha_bar, eta=0.0)
x_sto = ddim_step(x_t, t, t_prev, eps_stub, alpha_bar, eta=1.0)

print("\n── Single DDIM step ──")
print(f"x_t   shape : {x_t.shape}")     # torch.Size([8])
print(f"x_det shape : {x_det.shape}")   # torch.Size([8])
print(f"x_sto shape : {x_sto.shape}")   # torch.Size([8])

print("\nx_det (eta=0):", x_det.round(decimals=4))
print("x_sto (eta=1):", x_sto.round(decimals=4))

# x_det (eta=0): tensor([ 0.3267,  0.1210,  0.2180,  0.2016, -1.2070, -0.1172,  2.3207, -0.7003])

6.11 Visual Summary

Here is the full DDIM sampling algorithm in structured pseudocode — readable alongside the math:

# DDIM SAMPLING PSEUDOCODE
# Inputs: trained eps_theta, noise schedule alpha_bar[0..T]
#         number of steps S, stochasticity eta in [0, 1]

# 1. Build strided schedule (must end at t = 0, where alpha_bar = 1)
tau = [T, T-T//S, T-2*(T//S), ..., T//S, 0]   # S+1 levels, decreasing, ends at 0

# 2. Sample initial noise
x = sample_from_N(0, I)                     # shape (d,)  — the only randomness (if eta=0)

# 3. Reverse loop (len(tau)-1 = S transitions, the last one landing on t = 0)
for i in range(len(tau) - 1):
    t      = tau[i]                          # current coarse timestep
    t_prev = tau[i + 1]                      # previous coarse timestep

    eps    = eps_theta(x, t)                 # one neural-network call
    x0_hat = (x - sqrt(1-ab[t]) * eps) / sqrt(ab[t])   # predict clean

    sigma  = eta * sqrt((1-ab[t_prev])/(1-ab[t])) * sqrt(1-ab[t]/ab[t_prev])
    det_c  = sqrt(1 - ab[t_prev] - sigma**2)

    z      = randn_like(x) if eta > 0 else 0
    x      = sqrt(ab[t_prev]) * x0_hat  +  det_c * eps  +  sigma * z

# 4. Return final clean sample
return x                                     # x0, the generated image

Key observations from the pseudocode:

  • There is exactly one neural-network call per outer loop iteration.
  • \(\hat x_0\) and \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) are computed once per step and reused for both term (1) and term (2).
  • When \(\eta=0\), the only randomness is the initial \(x_T\). Afterward everything is deterministic: fix the seed, fix the image.
  • When \(\eta=1\), every step injects fresh noise — this is DDPM.

6.12 How DDIM Relates to the MIT Notes

MIT Notes context (Appendix E / Summary 45)

The MIT lecture notes (§Appendix E, Summary 45) discuss DDIM in the context of "Alternative Diffusion Formulations." They observe that the first wave of diffusion papers (DDPM, DDIM) worked in discrete time and used Markov chains, while later work (Song et al. 2021; flow matching) moved to continuous time and SDEs/ODEs. Key remarks from the notes:

  • "Discrete-time: Approximations of SDEs via discrete-time Markov chains are often used." DDIM is exactly a discrete-time ODE integrator, confirming that it sits between the original DDPM chain and the continuous probability-flow ODE.
  • "The probability flow ODE is often better" — by "better" the notes mean it enables tighter bounds and more flexible samplers. DDIM (\(\eta=0\)) is the first step in that direction.
  • The notes use an inverted time convention compared to the DDPM papers: their \(t=0\) corresponds to pure noise and \(t=1\) to clean data. DDPM and DDIM use the opposite convention (\(t=0\) is clean, \(t=T\) is noise). Keep this in mind when reading the notes alongside this guide.
