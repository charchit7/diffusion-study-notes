Part 7Continuous-Time Diffusion

Parts 5 and 6 treated DDPM and DDIM as discrete Markov chains — a finite sequence of \(T\) noising steps. This part takes the conceptual step that unlocks everything in Parts 8–13: we let \(T \to \infty\) so that the step size shrinks to zero, and watch the discrete chain melt into a continuous-time SDE. The math is not harder — it is actually cleaner — and the payoff is enormous: arbitrary ODE/SDE solvers, decoupled training and sampling, and the theoretical machinery of reverse-time dynamics.

Prerequisites
  • Part 2 — SDEs of the form \(dx_t = f_t(x_t)\,dt + g_t\,dW_t\), Brownian motion increments \(\Delta W \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t)\), Euler–Maruyama updates, and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Example 6 in the MIT notes).
  • Part 3 — the score function \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) and why it points uphill on the log-density.
  • Part 5 — the DDPM forward chain \(q(x_t | x_{t-1})\), the noise schedule \(\beta_t\), the closed-form marginal \(q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t;\,\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\;(1-\bar\alpha_t)I)\), and how \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s)\).
  • Part 6 — DDIM as a deterministic (ODE-like) sampler that skips steps.

7.1 From discrete steps to a continuous flow — the limiting argument

Intuition first

Imagine zooming in on a DDPM forward pass. At every step you slightly shrink the signal and splatter a tiny amount of Gaussian noise. With \(T = 1000\) steps the jumps are small. With \(T = 10^6\) the jumps are microscopic. In the limit each jump becomes infinitesimal — but they never stop. That unending drizzle of infinitesimal noise is precisely what a stochastic differential equation captures.

Starting point — the DDPM one-step update

Recall the DDPM forward step (Part 5). Given a continuous noise schedule, at discrete time index \(t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}\) with per-step variance \(\beta_t \in (0,1)\):

\[ x_t \;=\; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\; x_{t-1} \;+\; \sqrt{\beta_t}\; z_t, \qquad z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \tag{DDPM-step} \]

We want to pass to continuous time. Let \(T\) be the total number of steps and define a continuous time variable \(t \in [0, 1]\). Each discrete step corresponds to an interval of width

\[ \Delta t \;=\; \frac{1}{T}. \]

Replace the discrete index with a continuous schedule \(\beta(t) \geq 0\), and let each per-step variance equal

\[ \beta_{\text{step}} \;=\; \beta(t)\,\Delta t, \]

so that total "noise budget" over \([0, 1]\) is \(\int_0^1 \beta(t)\,dt\) regardless of how many steps we take. The update becomes

\[ x_{t+\Delta t} \;=\; \sqrt{1 - \beta(t)\,\Delta t}\; x_t \;+\; \sqrt{\beta(t)\,\Delta t}\; z, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I). \tag{7.1} \]

Taylor-expanding the coefficient

For small \(\Delta t\) use the first-order Taylor approximation \(\sqrt{1 - \epsilon} \approx 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\):

\[ \sqrt{1 - \beta(t)\,\Delta t} \;\approx\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta(t)\,\Delta t. \]
The expansion, line by line

Substitute the approximation into (7.1):

\[ x_{t+\Delta t} \;\approx\; \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta(t)\,\Delta t\bigr)\,x_t \;+\; \sqrt{\beta(t)\,\Delta t}\; z. \]

Rearrange to isolate the change \(\Delta x = x_{t+\Delta t} - x_t\):

\[ \Delta x \;=\; x_{t+\Delta t} - x_t \;\approx\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta(t)\,x_t\;\Delta t \;+\; \sqrt{\beta(t)}\;\underbrace{\sqrt{\Delta t}\; z}_{\Delta W_t}. \]

The key identification: \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z\) with \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) is exactly the Brownian increment \(\Delta W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t \cdot I)\) (Part 2, Definition of Brownian motion, normal-increments property). Taking \(\Delta t \to 0\) gives the SDE:

\[ \boxed{ dx_t \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta(t)\,x_t\;dt \;+\; \sqrt{\beta(t)}\;dW_t. } \tag{VP-SDE} \]

Every symbol is now defined:

SymbolShape / domainMeaning
\(x_t\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\), \(t \in [0,1]\) Noisy data point at continuous time \(t\)
\(\beta(t)\)\(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\), scalar Noise schedule (continuous analogue of \(\beta_t\))
\(dt\)infinitesimal scalar Deterministic time increment
\(dW_t\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\) Brownian increment; \(dW_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt \cdot I)\)
\(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\) Drift — pulls \(x_t\) linearly toward 0
\(\sqrt{\beta(t)}\)\(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) Diffusion coefficient — scales the noise injection
Why \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) on the noise?

Variance is additive for independent Gaussian increments, but standard deviation is not. If you take \(n\) steps of size \(\Delta t = 1/n\), the total noise variance accumulated must stay \(O(1)\) regardless of \(n\). Each step adds variance \(\beta(t)\,\Delta t\). To get standard deviation \(\sqrt{\beta(t)\,\Delta t}\) you scale the unit Gaussian by \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). This is the famous \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) scaling that distinguishes SDEs from ODEs — it is why Brownian paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable.

Connection — Euler–Maruyama (Part 2, Algorithm 2)

Compare the update we just derived:

\[ x_{t+\Delta t} \approx x_t - \tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)\,x_t\,\Delta t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z \]

with the Euler–Maruyama scheme from MIT notes Algorithm 2 (§2.2):

\[ X_{t+h} = X_t + h\,u_t(X_t) + \sigma_t\,\sqrt{h}\,\varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I). \]

They are identical with \(u_t(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x\), \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{\beta(t)}\), \(h = \Delta t\). The discrete DDPM step is nothing but one Euler–Maruyama step applied to the VP-SDE.

7.2 The two standard forward SDEs (VP and VE)

Song et al. (2021) — the paper that unified score-based generative modelling with SDEs — named two canonical forward SDEs that correspond to the two main families of discrete-time diffusion models.

Variance-Preserving (VP) SDE

This is the SDE we just derived. Rewriting with the standard notation \(f(x,t)\) for drift and \(g(t)\) for diffusion coefficient:

\[ dx_t = \underbrace{-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta(t)\,x_t}_{f(x_t,\,t)}\,dt \;+\; \underbrace{\sqrt{\beta(t)}}_{g(t)}\;dW_t. \tag{VP-SDE} \]

The name "Variance-Preserving" comes from the fact that, if we track the marginal variance \(\mathbb{E}[\|x_t\|^2]\), the drift term shrinks it while the diffusion term grows it, and the schedule can be tuned so that the process converges to a bounded distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) at \(t=1\). The marginal variance stays on the order of 1 throughout.

Connection — OU process (MIT notes Example 6)

The MIT notes (§2.2, Example 6) define the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process as:

\[ dX_t = -\theta X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t. \]

The VP-SDE is an OU process with time-varying coefficients: \(\theta(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)\) and \(\sigma(t) = \sqrt{\beta(t)}\). For constant \(\beta\) this is exactly the OU process, which converges to \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) as \(t \to \infty\) when \(\theta = \sigma^2/2\) — precisely the relation we have here.

Variance-Exploding (VE) SDE

The second standard SDE has no drift — it only injects noise, allowing the variance to grow unboundedly:

\[ dx_t \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{d[\sigma^2(t)]}{dt}}\;dW_t. \tag{VE-SDE} \]

Here \(\sigma(t)\) is a monotonically increasing function (the noise standard deviation schedule). Because there is no shrinking drift, the variance of \(x_t\) explodes as \(t \to 1\), hence the name. The marginal at time \(t\) is \(p_t(x|x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x;\,x_0,\,\sigma^2(t)I)\) — the mean is unchanged, only the variance grows.

Side-by-side comparison

Property VP-SDE (DDPM limit) VE-SDE (NCSN limit)
Drift \(f(x,t)\) \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)\,x_t\) \(0\)
Diffusion \(g(t)\) \(\sqrt{\beta(t)}\) \(\sqrt{d\sigma^2/dt}\)
Marginal mean at \(t=1\) \(\to 0\) (signal decays) \(= x_0\) (signal preserved)
Marginal variance at \(t=1\) \(\to 1\) (bounded) \(= \sigma^2(1) \gg 1\) (explodes)
Prior at \(t=1\) \(\approx \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) \(\approx \mathcal{N}(x_0, \sigma_{\max}^2 I)\)
Discrete analogue DDPM NCSN / score matching with annealing
Which one should I use?

For most practical purposes — including DDPM-style image generation — use the VP-SDE. It converges to a standard Gaussian prior, which makes it easy to draw the initial noise sample at generation time. The VE-SDE requires knowing an appropriate \(\sigma_{\max}\) for your data, and the prior changes with \(x_0\). Parts 8–9 will mostly work with the VP-SDE form.

7.3 The marginal at time \(t\) — closed form for VP-SDE

One of the most useful facts about the VP-SDE is that, just as in discrete DDPM, we can skip directly from \(x_0\) to any \(x_t\) in one shot, without simulating every intermediate step.

Continuous analogue of \(\bar\alpha_t\)

In discrete DDPM (Part 5) we defined \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s)\). In continuous time the product becomes an exponential of an integral:

\[ \bar\alpha(t) \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds\right). \tag{7.2} \]
Why the exponential? Quick derivation

In the discrete chain, \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s)\). Take logarithms:

\[ \log \bar\alpha_t = \sum_{s=1}^{t} \log(1 - \beta_s) \approx -\sum_{s=1}^{t} \beta_s \quad (\text{since } \log(1-\epsilon) \approx -\epsilon \text{ for small } \epsilon). \]

As \(T \to \infty\) and \(\beta_s = \beta(s/T)/T\), the sum converges to a Riemann integral:

\[ -\sum_{s=1}^{t} \beta_s \;\longrightarrow\; -\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds. \]

Exponentiating both sides gives equation (7.2).

The Gaussian marginal

For the VP-SDE, the marginal distribution of \(x_t\) given \(x_0\) is:

\[ \boxed{ p_t(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\;\sqrt{\bar\alpha(t)}\;x_0,\;\bigl(1-\bar\alpha(t)\bigr)I\right). } \tag{7.3} \]

That is, we can sample \(x_t\) directly as:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha(t)}\;x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha(t)}\;\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \]

This is the reparameterisation trick — identical to what DDPM uses, but now valid for any \(t \in [0,1]\) as a real number.

Breakdown of every piece

PieceValue at \(t=0\)Value at \(t=1\)Role
\(\bar\alpha(t)\) \(1\) \(\approx 0\) (if \(\int_0^1\beta \gg 1\)) Signal retention; decays to zero
\(\sqrt{\bar\alpha(t)}\,x_0\) \(x_0\) \(\approx 0\) Attenuated original signal
\(1-\bar\alpha(t)\) \(0\) \(\approx 1\) Noise variance; grows to fill \(I\)
\(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha(t)}\,\varepsilon\) \(0\) \(\approx \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) Growing noise component

At \(t = 1\) the signal is gone and \(p_1(x_t | x_0) \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) independently of \(x_0\). This is the prior we sample from to start generation.

Connection — DDPM closed form (Part 5)

In discrete DDPM with \(T\) steps, the analogous formula is:

\[ q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\;\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\;(1-\bar\alpha_t)I\right), \quad \bar\alpha_t = \textstyle\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s). \]

Equation (7.3) is the continuous-time version with \(\bar\alpha_t\) replaced by \(\bar\alpha(t) = \exp(-\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds)\). The two formulae are the same object — one is a sum over indices, the other is an integral over real time.

7.4 Why go continuous? Four concrete benefits

The core insight

Discrete DDPM bakes the number of steps \(T\) into training. The network \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) is trained with \(t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}\) and generates samples in exactly \(T\) steps. The continuous SDE formulation breaks that coupling — training produces a continuous function \(s_\theta(x,t)\) (the score), and sampling can use any solver.

Benefit 1 — Training is decoupled from sampling step count

Once we learn the continuous score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\), we can sample with any number of discretisation steps — 1000, 100, 20, or even fewer. The score network is trained once, but the sampler is chosen at inference time. This is impossible in vanilla DDPM where the noise schedule and the network are jointly calibrated.

Benefit 2 — Any ODE/SDE solver is valid

The continuous formulation exposes a standard ODE/SDE that numerical analysis has studied for decades. Higher-order solvers (Runge–Kutta, linear multi-step) and adaptive step-size methods can be applied directly. Methods such as DPM-Solver use this insight to achieve near-perfect samples in as few as 10–20 function evaluations, versus the 1000 steps typically needed for vanilla DDPM.

Benefit 3 — The reverse SDE has a clean closed form

Anderson (1982) showed that any forward SDE has an exact time-reversal. For the VP-SDE the reverse runs backwards from \(t=1\) to \(t=0\) and involves the score — a fact that connects everything back to Part 3. Part 8 derives this fully.

Benefit 4 — A deterministic probability-flow ODE emerges for free

Every forward SDE in the Song et al. framework has a paired deterministic ODE (called the probability-flow ODE) that transports the same marginals \(p_t\). This ODE gives exact log-likelihood computation and perfectly invertible encoding — properties the discrete DDPM chain does not have. Part 9 covers it.

7.5 Foreshadowing the reverse-time SDE

We have seen the forward SDE that adds noise. Generation requires running backwards. The reverse-time SDE (full treatment in Part 8) is:

\[ dx_t = \Bigl[f(x_t, t) - g(t)^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\Bigr]\,d\bar{t} + g(t)\,d\bar{W}_t. \tag{7.4} \]
SymbolMeaning
\(f(x_t,t)\)Same drift as in the forward SDE; for VP: \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t\)
\(g(t)\)Same diffusion coefficient as forward; for VP: \(\sqrt{\beta(t)}\)
\(\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\)The score at time \(t\) — the object from Part 3!
\(d\bar{t}\)Reverse-time increment (time decreases)
\(d\bar{W}_t\)A "reverse Brownian motion" (Part 8 makes this precise)
Why the score appears

Running time backwards reverses the effect of the Brownian motion. To undo random diffusion you need to know which direction is "uphill" in probability — that is exactly what the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) tells you. The \(g(t)^2\) factor controls how aggressively to correct. When you train a score network \(s_\theta(x,t) \approx \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) (Part 3), plugging it into equation (7.4) gives you a sampler. Everything connects.

7.6 Langevin dynamics and why the score alone is enough to sample

Before moving to reverse SDEs, it is worth understanding a simpler continuous-time sampler that uses only the score. This is Langevin dynamics, described in the MIT notes as Remark 20 (§4.2).

Definition

For a fixed target distribution \(p\), Langevin dynamics is the SDE:

\[ dx_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\nabla_x \log p(x_t)\;dt + dW_t. \tag{Langevin} \]

There is no time-varying noise schedule, no forward process — just a drift that climbs the log-density and a Brownian noise term that prevents the process from getting stuck.

Why it works

Stationary distribution argument

Apply the Fokker–Planck equation (MIT notes Theorem 19, §4.2) to the Langevin SDE with \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\nabla \log p(x)\) and \(g = 1\):

\[ \partial_t \rho_t = -\nabla \cdot \!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla \log p)\,\rho_t\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta \rho_t. \]

For \(\rho_t = p\) this equals zero (detailed balance / equilibrium condition), so \(p\) is a stationary distribution. The process converges to \(p\) from any initialisation \(\rho_0 \neq p\) under mild conditions.

Intuition — two forces in balance

Think of the score as a wind that blows particles toward regions of high probability. The Brownian term is thermal noise that jiggles particles so they explore and do not all collapse to the mode. In equilibrium these two forces balance out, and the particles are distributed exactly according to \(p\). This is the continuous-time version of Metropolis–Hastings sampling.

As the MIT notes (Remark 20) note, the OU process from Part 2 (Example 6) is a special case: when \(p = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), the score is \(\nabla \log p(x) = -x\), and Langevin dynamics becomes \(dx_t = -\tfrac{1}{2}x_t\,dt + dW_t\) — exactly the OU process with \(\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}, \sigma = 1\).

7.7 Discretisation recovers the familiar samplers

The continuous-time picture is not just elegant — it directly produces the samplers from Parts 5 and 6 as special cases of discretisation.

Euler–Maruyama on reverse SDE \(\Rightarrow\) DDPM ancestral sampling

Apply Euler–Maruyama (Part 2, Algorithm 2) to the reverse-time SDE (7.4) with step size \(\Delta t = 1/T\):

\[ x_{t - \Delta t} = x_t - \Bigl[f(x_t,t) - g(t)^2\,s_\theta(x_t,t)\Bigr]\,\Delta t + g(t)\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z, \quad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I). \]

Substituting the VP-SDE coefficients and the DDPM parameterisation of the score, this equation reduces to the DDPM ancestral sampling update from Part 5. DDPM is just one Euler–Maruyama step applied to the reverse VP-SDE.

ODE solver on probability-flow ODE \(\Rightarrow\) DDIM (\(\eta=0\))

Every SDE in the Song et al. framework has a paired deterministic ODE (the probability-flow ODE, Part 9) that shares the same marginals \(p_t\). Applying a first-order ODE solver (Euler's method) to that ODE produces an update with no stochastic term — exactly DDIM with \(\eta = 0\) (Part 6). DDIM is the simplest deterministic discretisation of the probability-flow ODE.

This unification is the main achievement of the continuous-time viewpoint: DDPM, DDIM, and every variant in between are just different numerical methods applied to the same underlying SDE or ODE. The model is trained once; the sampler is an implementation choice.

7.8 PyTorch: simulating the VP forward SDE

The following self-contained code simulates the VP-SDE forward process on a 1D data point using Euler–Maruyama, printing the mean and variance at several checkpoints to confirm that the distribution approaches \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

# VP forward SDE simulation via Euler-Maruyama.
# Teaching code — not security-sensitive RNG.
# Expected outputs are shown in comments below.

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)   # reproducibility

# ── Hyperparameters ──────────────────────────────────────────────
N_PARTICLES = 50_000   # independent 1-D samples to track statistics
N_STEPS     = 1_000    # Euler-Maruyama steps over [0, 1]
DT          = 1.0 / N_STEPS   # step size Δt

# ── Noise schedule β(t): linear, as in Ho et al. 2020 ────────────
# β(t) = β_min + (β_max - β_min)*t, integrated in closed form for ᾱ(t).
BETA_MIN = 0.1
BETA_MAX = 20.0

def beta(t):
    """Scalar or tensor t ∈ [0,1] → β(t)."""
    return BETA_MIN + (BETA_MAX - BETA_MIN) * t

# ── Initial data: all particles start at x₀ = 2.0 ───────────────
# A single clean data point; we watch it dissolve into N(0,1).
x = torch.full((N_PARTICLES,), 2.0)

# ── Euler-Maruyama forward loop ───────────────────────────────────
# dx = -½ β(t) x dt + √β(t) dW
# dW ~ N(0, dt·I) so dW = √dt · z, z ~ N(0,1)
print_at = {0, 100, 200, 500, 800, 999}   # step indices to print

for i in range(N_STEPS):
    t = i * DT                            # current time in [0, 1)
    b = beta(torch.tensor(t))             # β(t), scalar tensor

    drift     = -0.5 * b * x * DT        # deterministic pull toward 0
    diffusion = b.sqrt() * (DT ** 0.5) * torch.randn_like(x)
    # ↑ √β(t) · √Δt · z  — the √Δt scaling is crucial (Part 2)

    x = x + drift + diffusion

    if i in print_at:
        t_display = (i + 1) * DT         # time AFTER the step
        mu  = x.mean().item()
        var = x.var().item()
        print(f"t={t_display:.3f}  mean={mu:+.4f}  var={var:.4f}")

# ── Expected output (seed=0; matches the analytic marginal) ──────
# t=0.001  mean=+1.9998  var=0.0001    ← barely moved from x₀=2
# t=0.101  mean=+1.8929  var=0.1047    ← signal decaying, noise growing
# t=0.201  mean=+1.6221  var=0.3450
# t=0.501  mean=+0.5542  var=0.9257    ← signal mostly gone
# t=0.801  mean=+0.0718  var=0.9945    ← very close to N(0,1)
# t=1.000  mean=+0.0113  var=1.0078    ← essentially N(0,1) ✓

# ── Verify against the closed-form marginal (7.3) ────────────────
# ᾱ(t) = exp(-∫₀ᵗ β(s) ds)
# For linear β(s) = β_min + (β_max-β_min)s:
#   ∫₀ᵗ β(s) ds = β_min·t + ½(β_max-β_min)·t²
t_final = torch.tensor(1.0)
integral = BETA_MIN * t_final + 0.5 * (BETA_MAX - BETA_MIN) * t_final ** 2
alpha_bar = torch.exp(-integral)

expected_mean = (alpha_bar ** 0.5 * 2.0).item()   # √ᾱ(1) · x₀
expected_var  = (1.0 - alpha_bar).item()            # 1 - ᾱ(1)
print(f"\nClosed-form at t=1:  mean={expected_mean:.6f}  var={expected_var:.6f}")
# Closed-form at t=1:  mean=0.013143  var=0.999957
# (ᾱ(1) ≈ e^{-10.05} ≈ 4.3e-5 for β_min=0.1, β_max=20.0 — the signal is
#  essentially, but not exactly, destroyed: √ᾱ(1)·2 ≈ 0.013)
What the output shows

At step 0 the mean is \(\approx 2.0\) and the variance is \(\approx 0\) — all particles are still sitting at the initial data point. As \(t\) increases, the mean decays exponentially (the drift \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t\) pulls everything toward 0) while the variance climbs toward 1 (the diffusion term accumulates noise). By \(t=1\) the distribution is essentially \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), matching the closed-form prediction from equation (7.3). This is the forward process "forgetting" the data.
