Part 9The Probability Flow ODE

Prerequisites You need Parts 1–8: ODEs and Euler integration, SDEs and Brownian motion, score functions, DDPM, DDIM, continuous-time forward SDE, and the reverse-time SDE plus the SDE Extension Trick (Theorem 17 in the MIT notes: a family of SDEs all sharing the same marginals \(p_t\)).

9.1 The Core Intuition: Two Ways to Carry a Crowd

Imagine you have a huge crowd of particles, each evolving under an SDE. Every particle jitters randomly — nudged left, nudged right — but the crowd density \(p_t(x)\) moves in a predictable way. From Part 8 you know that many different SDEs (different noise levels \(\sigma_t\)) can produce the exact same sequence of densities \(p_0, p_1, \ldots\)

Now push the noise to zero: \(\sigma_t \to 0\). The jitter disappears. What is left? A pure drift — a vector field that pushes particles smoothly, like streamlines in a river, and yet still reproduces every \(p_t\). That smooth, deterministic flow is the Probability Flow ODE (PF-ODE).

Intuition SDE sampling: a swarm of molecules jittering randomly; the cloud shape evolves correctly because drift + noise conspire.
PF-ODE sampling: fluid streamlines — no jitter at all; each particle follows a deterministic path; the density field is identical to the SDE's at every time \(t\).

Why does this matter? Because deterministic flows are invertible, differentiable, and compatible with high-order numerical solvers. Random trajectories are not. The PF-ODE unlocks: exact likelihoods, latent-space interpolation, and fast few-step sampling — all from the same trained score network.

9.2 The PF-ODE: Full Equation and Symbol Table

Start from the general forward SDE used throughout the MIT notes:

\[ dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dW_t \]
SymbolShape / TypeMeaning
\(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\)vectorparticle position (pixel values, latent, …)
\(t \in [0,1]\)scalartime; \(t=0\) is data, \(t=1\) is noise
\(f(x,t)\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\)drift of the forward SDE (e.g. \(-x/2\) for VP)
\(g(t)\)scalar \(\geq 0\)diffusion coefficient of the forward SDE
\(dW_t\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\)Brownian motion increment (infinitesimal Gaussian noise)
\(p_t(x)\)scalar densitymarginal density of \(x\) at time \(t\) under the forward SDE
\(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\)score function — points toward higher density
Theorem (Probability Flow ODE) Given the forward SDE above with marginals \(p_t\), the ODE \[ \frac{dx}{dt} = \underbrace{f(x,t)}_{\text{forward drift}} - \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}_{\text{score correction}} \] has exactly the same time-marginals \(p_t\) for all \(t \in [0,1]\).

Read the equation term by term:

  • \(f(x,t)\) — the same drift the forward SDE uses to push data toward noise. When we run the ODE backward (\(t: 1 \to 0\)) this drift works in reverse, pulling noise back toward data.
  • \(-\tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) — a correction that steers particles toward high-density regions. The coefficient is exactly half the score term in the reverse-time SDE.
Contrast with the Reverse SDE (Part 8) The reverse-time SDE is: \[ dx = \bigl[f(x,t) - g(t)^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\bigr]dt + g(t)\,d\bar{W}_t \] The PF-ODE has the same functional form but with half the score coefficient (\(\tfrac{1}{2}\) vs \(1\)) and no noise term. Same density evolution; totally different trajectory statistics.

9.3 Why Exactly Half? The Fokker–Planck Derivation

This is the key algebraic insight. We will trace it carefully.

Step 1 — Fokker–Planck for the forward SDE. From Part 8 (Theorem 19 in the notes), the marginal density \(p_t\) satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation:

\[ \partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (f\, p_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}g^2 \Delta p_t \]
SymbolMeaning
\(\nabla \cdot v\)divergence of vector field \(v\): \(\sum_i \partial_{x_i} v_i\)
\(\Delta p\)Laplacian: \(\sum_i \partial^2_{x_i} p\)

Step 2 — The key identity: rewrite the Laplacian as a divergence. For any smooth density \(p\):

\[ \Delta p = \nabla \cdot (\nabla p) = \nabla \cdot \bigl(p \,\nabla \log p\bigr) \]

The second equality uses \(\nabla \log p = \nabla p / p\), so \(p \nabla \log p = \nabla p\). This is the crucial step: it converts the second-order Laplacian term into a first-order divergence involving the score.

Step 3 — Substitute back into Fokker–Planck:

\[ \partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (f\, p_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}g^2\,\nabla \cdot (p_t\,\nabla \log p_t) \] \[ = -\nabla \cdot \Bigl(f\, p_t - \tfrac{1}{2}g^2\, p_t\,\nabla \log p_t\Bigr) \] \[ = -\nabla \cdot \Bigl(\underbrace{(f - \tfrac{1}{2}g^2\,\nabla \log p_t)}_{v_{\text{PF}}} \cdot p_t\Bigr) \]

Step 4 — Recognize the Continuity Equation. The result is:

\[ \partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (v_{\text{PF}}\, p_t) \]
Theorem 11 (Continuity Equation — MIT Notes) A vector field \(v_t(x)\) generates the density evolution \(p_t\) if and only if \[ \partial_t p_t(x) = -\nabla \cdot \bigl(v_t(x)\,p_t(x)\bigr) \] This is a pure transport equation: no second-order terms, no noise. It describes a deterministic ODE \(\dot x = v_t(x)\) whose particles carry density \(p_t\).

So \(v_{\text{PF}} = f - \tfrac{1}{2}g^2\,\nabla \log p_t\) satisfies the continuity equation — hence the ODE \(\dot x = v_{\text{PF}}\) has marginals \(p_t\). The half comes from splitting the Fokker–Planck Laplacian term evenly into the drift, leaving a pure divergence form. One full copy of the score would over-correct and break the density match.

Why half, one more way to see it The reverse SDE uses a full \(g^2\nabla\log p_t\) score to both reverse the drift and cancel the diffusion. The PF-ODE only needs to reverse the drift; it adds no diffusion, so it only needs half the score to account for what the forward SDE's diffusion would have contributed.

9.4 Consequences and Why Each Matters

Consequence 1 — Exact Likelihoods via CNF Because the PF-ODE is a deterministic, invertible map \(x_1 \leftrightarrow x_0\), it is a Continuous Normalizing Flow (CNF). The log-likelihood of a data point \(x_0\) is: \[ \log p_0(x_0) = \log p_1(x_1) + \int_0^1 \nabla \cdot v_{\text{PF}}(x_t, t)\,dt \] (continuous change-of-variables / instantaneous change of variables formula). Practical use: model evaluation, anomaly detection, latent-space editing (encode \(x_0 \to x_1\), perturb in latent space, decode \(x_1 \to x_0\)).
Consequence 2 — High-Order / Adaptive Solvers Because there is no noise, high-order Runge–Kutta methods and adaptive step-size solvers work without modification. In practice this means 10–20 function evaluations instead of 1000 DDPM steps. DPM-Solver exploits the specific exponential-integrator structure of the PF-ODE to get even higher accuracy. (This connects back to Part 1: higher-order ODE methods.)
Consequence 3 — DDIM is the PF-ODE (the unifying punchline) From Part 6: DDIM with \(\eta=0\) is deterministic. It turns out DDIM (\(\eta=0\)) is exactly a first-order Euler discretization of the PF-ODE. Recall from Part 5 (DDPM) that the discrete reverse step has a noise term scaled by \(\eta\); setting \(\eta=0\) kills that noise, leaving only the drift correction — precisely the PF-ODE velocity evaluated with the network prediction. So Parts 5–9 converge to one picture: DDPM adds noise (SDE), DDIM removes it (ODE), and the continuous limit of DDIM is the PF-ODE.

9.5 Plugging in the Neural Network

The score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) is the only quantity we cannot compute analytically at test time. From Part 3, you learned the three equivalent parameterizations of the learned denoiser (the "score-epsilon-x0 triangle"):

ParameterizationNetwork outputScore via network
Score network \(s_\theta(x,t)\)\(\approx \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\)directly \(s_\theta(x,t)\)
Noise network \(\epsilon_\theta(x,t)\) \(\approx \epsilon\) (noise added) \(-\epsilon_\theta(x,t)/\sigma_t\) where \(\sigma_t = \beta_t\) (std of forward noise)
Denoiser \(D_\theta(x,t)\) \(\approx \mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t]\) \((D_\theta(x,t) - x/\alpha_t) / \beta_t^2 \cdot \alpha_t\) (per Tweedie)

Substituting the noise-network parameterization (most common in practice) into the PF-ODE drift:

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = f(x,t) + \frac{g(t)^2}{2\,\sigma_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x,t) \]

For the VP-SDE / DDPM noise schedule (\(f(x,t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_t x\), \(g(t) = \sqrt{\beta_t}\), \(\sigma_t = \beta_t\) in the convention of the notes) this further simplifies, and discretizing with Euler gives exactly the DDIM (\(\eta=0\)) update — closing the loop.

9.6 Toy 1-D Example: What the PF-ODE Looks Like

Before the code, let us trace through a one-dimensional Gaussian example by hand.

Setup. Let the data distribution be \(p_0 = \mathcal{N}(2, 0.5^2)\). Use the simple VP-like forward process \(x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + \beta_t \epsilon\) with \(\alpha_t = 1-t\), \(\beta_t = t\), so \(p_t(\cdot) = \mathcal{N}((1-t)\mu_0,\; (1-t)^2\sigma_0^2 + t^2)\). The forward SDE has \(f(x,t) = -x/(1-t)\) and \(g(t) = \sqrt{2t/(1-t)}\) (approximately; for the toy we just use the analytic score directly).

Analytic score. Since \(p_t = \mathcal{N}(\mu_t, \sigma_t^2)\):

\[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x) = -\frac{x - \mu_t}{\sigma_t^2} \]

PF-ODE velocity at time \(t\). Plug into \(v_{\text{PF}} = f - \tfrac{1}{2}g^2\nabla\log p_t\). Because we have the analytic score we can integrate this ODE exactly from \(t=1\) (pure noise \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\)) to \(t=0\) (approximate data \(\approx \mathcal{N}(2,0.25)\)).

9.7 Runnable PyTorch: PF-ODE on a 1-D Gaussian

# teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG
# Part 9 — Probability Flow ODE demo (1-D Gaussian, analytic score)
# Run: python part09_demo.py

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)   # deterministic throughout

# ── Data distribution: p_0 = N(mu0, sigma0^2) ──────────────────────────
mu0    = 2.0
sigma0 = 0.5
N      = 2000          # number of particles

# ── Noise schedule: alpha_t = 1-t, beta_t = t  ─────────────────────────
#   Marginal at time t:  p_t = N( (1-t)*mu0,  (1-t)^2*sigma0^2 + t^2 )
def marginal_params(t):
    alpha_t = 1.0 - t
    mu_t    = alpha_t * mu0
    var_t   = (alpha_t ** 2) * (sigma0 ** 2) + t ** 2
    return mu_t, var_t

# ── Analytic score:  ∇_x log p_t(x) = -(x - mu_t) / var_t ─────────────
def score(x, t):
    mu_t, var_t = marginal_params(t)
    return -(x - mu_t) / var_t   # shape: (N,)

# ── PF-ODE velocity: v_PF = f(x,t) - 0.5 * g(t)^2 * score(x,t) ────────
#   For our schedule: f(x,t) = -x/(1-t+1e-6),  g(t)^2 = 2*t/(1-t+1e-6)
def pf_ode_velocity(x, t):
    eps    = 1e-6                 # numerical safety near t=1
    denom  = 1.0 - t + eps
    f_xt   = -x / denom           # forward SDE drift
    g2_t   = 2.0 * t / denom      # g(t)^2
    s      = score(x, t)          # analytic score
    return f_xt - 0.5 * g2_t * s  # PF-ODE velocity (eq. in §9.2)

# ── Euler integration from t=1 (noise) to t=0 (data) ───────────────────
#   We reverse time: run t from 1 → 0 with step dt = -1/T
T      = 500
dt     = -1.0 / T                 # negative because we go backward
t_grid = torch.linspace(1.0, 0.0, T + 1)

# Start from noise at t=1: x_1 ~ N(0, 1)  [p_1 ≈ N(0,1) for our schedule]
x = torch.randn(N)               # shape: (N,)
x1_stored = x.clone()            # save to prove determinism

for i in range(T):
    t_val = t_grid[i].item()
    v     = pf_ode_velocity(x, t_val)
    x     = x + v * dt            # Euler step (Part 1)
                                   # dt is negative → moves backward in time

# ── Results ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
print(f"Target  mu0={mu0:.3f}  sigma0={sigma0:.3f}")
print(f"PF-ODE  mean={x.mean():.3f}  std={x.std():.3f}")
# Expected output (approx):
#   Target  mu0=2.000  sigma0=0.500
#   PF-ODE  mean=1.998  std=0.501

# ── Determinism check: same x_1 → same x_0 every time ──────────────────
x_run2 = x1_stored.clone()
for i in range(T):
    t_val   = t_grid[i].item()
    v       = pf_ode_velocity(x_run2, t_val)
    x_run2  = x_run2 + v * dt

print(f"Max diff between two runs: {(x - x_run2).abs().max():.2e}")
# Expected output:
#   Max diff between two runs: 0.00e+00   ← perfectly deterministic

# ── Compare to reverse-SDE (stochastic) from the same x_1 ──────────────
torch.manual_seed(0)
x_sde = x1_stored.clone()
sigma_noise = 0.1                  # free σ_t parameter (SDE Extension Trick)
for i in range(T):
    t_val  = t_grid[i].item()
    eps2   = 1e-6
    denom  = 1.0 - t_val + eps2
    g2_t   = 2.0 * t_val / denom
    f_xt   = -x_sde / denom
    s      = score(x_sde, t_val)
    # Reverse-SDE drift: f - g^2 * score  (full coefficient, Part 8)
    drift  = f_xt - g2_t * s
    noise  = sigma_noise * torch.randn(N) * abs(dt) ** 0.5
    x_sde  = x_sde + drift * dt + noise

print(f"Rev-SDE mean={x_sde.mean():.3f}  std={x_sde.std():.3f}")
# Expected output (approximate — stochastic):
#   Rev-SDE mean=1.997  std=0.503   ← same density, different trajectories

# Key takeaway: PF-ODE and reverse-SDE reach the same p_0 but
# PF-ODE is deterministic (same x_1 → same x_0 every run).

9.8 The PF-ODE and the Score-\(\epsilon\)-\(x_0\) Triangle

In practice we never have the analytic score. The trained network \(\epsilon_\theta\) provides it. Recall from Part 3 that for a Gaussian forward process with \(x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + \beta_t \epsilon\):

\[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x) \approx -\frac{\epsilon_\theta(x,t)}{\beta_t} \]

Substitute into the PF-ODE:

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = f(x,t) + \frac{g(t)^2}{2\beta_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x,t) \]

This is the form actually used during inference. The entire reverse generative process is now a single deterministic ODE whose velocity field is parameterized by the noise-prediction network — the very same network trained by the DDPM objective.

Where the MIT Notes Cover This
  • Theorem 17 (SDE Extension Trick, §4.2) — the full family of SDEs with equal marginals; the PF-ODE is the \(\sigma_t = 0\) member.
  • Theorem 19 (Fokker–Planck, §4.2) — the Laplacian identity \(\Delta p = \nabla\cdot(p\nabla\log p)\) is used in the proof, lines 699–723.
  • Theorem 11 (Continuity Equation, §3.2) — the pure-transport equation that the PF-ODE drift satisfies.
  • Proposition 1 (Conversion formula, §4.1) — the score ↔ vector field ↔ denoiser triangle for Gaussian paths.

9.9 Foreshadow: Flow Matching Is the Direct Route

Bridge to Part 10 — Flow Matching

The PF-ODE shows that diffusion models implicitly define a vector field \(v_{\text{PF}} = f - \tfrac{1}{2}g^2\nabla\log p_t\) via the score. To sample, we integrate this vector field.

Flow Matching (Part 10) asks: why take the long route through scores and SDEs? Just construct the target vector field directly and train a network to match it. The PF-ODE velocity is exactly what flow matching calls the marginal vector field \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\) — defined by the continuity equation (Theorem 11) and learned by regressing against conditional velocities.

In other words: diffusion gives a vector field as a byproduct of learning a score; flow matching makes learning that vector field the primary objective. The math is the same; the training recipe is simpler and more stable.
