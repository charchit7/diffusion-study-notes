Part 10Flow Matching

Prerequisites

You need Parts 1 (ODEs and Euler), 3 (score functions and the marginalization identity), and 9 (probability-flow ODE). The central idea here — learning a time-dependent velocity field whose ODE flow pushes noise to data — is exactly what Part 9 set up. Flow matching is the clean, direct training recipe for that velocity field.

10.0 The Goal, Restated as a Flow

Intuition

Every generative model we have studied so far ultimately samples by integrating some ODE (or SDE) that transports a simple noise distribution into the data distribution. In diffusion, we learned the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) and then derived a velocity from it. Flow matching asks: why not learn the velocity directly? Skip the score entirely. Learn the vector field; Euler-integrate at test time; done.

Formally, we want a neural network \(u_t^\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) such that simulating the ODE

\[ \frac{dX_t}{dt} = u_t^\theta(X_t), \qquad X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}} = \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) \]

produces \(X_1 \sim p_{\text{data}}\). Sampling at test time is a single Euler (or Runge–Kutta) integration from \(t=0\) to \(t=1\), exactly as in Part 1.

Connection to Part 9

In Part 9 we showed that every diffusion model has a probability-flow ODE whose marginal distributions match the forward process. That ODE's velocity field is a linear combination of the score and the drift. Flow matching learns the same kind of object — a time-dependent velocity field that transports densities — but specifies it from first principles without any SDE. No score needed, no reverse SDE, no Tweedie's formula: just a regression target we can write down in closed form.

Why not just regress against \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\) directly?

The marginal field \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\) is an integral over all data points:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x) = \int u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\, p_t(z|x)\, dz \]

This integral is intractable — you cannot evaluate it for a given \(x\). Flow matching sidesteps this by constructing a conditional target \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\) that is analytically available, and then proving (Theorem 12) that regressing against the conditional target is equivalent to regressing against the marginal one.

10.1 Conditional and Marginal Probability Paths

MIT notes §3.1 — Equation (11), (12), (13)

Intuition

Think of a probability path as a movie: at \(t=0\) the frame is pure Gaussian noise; at \(t=1\) the frame is the data distribution. The frames in between are a smooth interpolation. We get to choose what the intermediate frames look like — this is our design freedom, and it determines both the training target and the quality of the learned flow.

Conditional probability path — for a single data point \(z\)

SymbolShape / typeMeaning
\(z\)\(\mathbb{R}^d\)A fixed data point (e.g. one image)
\(\delta_z\)distribution on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)Dirac delta at \(z\); sampling always returns \(z\)
\(p_t(\cdot|z)\)distribution on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), indexed by \(t\in[0,1]\)Conditional probability path given \(z\)
\(p_{\text{init}}\)distribution on \(\mathbb{R}^d\)\(\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\) — the noise distribution at \(t=0\)

A conditional probability path is a family of distributions \(\{p_t(\cdot|z)\}_{t\in[0,1]}\) satisfying the boundary conditions:

\[ p_0(\cdot|z) = p_{\text{init}} = \mathcal{N}(0,I_d), \qquad p_1(\cdot|z) = \delta_z. \]

At \(t=0\): the distribution is standard Gaussian — no information about \(z\). At \(t=1\): the distribution is the point mass at \(z\) — total certainty. In between: a smooth transition parameterized by our choice of path.

Marginal probability path — averaged over all data

SymbolMeaning
\(p_{\text{data}}(z)\)The true data distribution
\(p_t(x)\)Marginal probability path: the distribution obtained by averaging the conditional path over the data

Given a conditional path, the marginal probability path is:

\[ p_t(x) = \int p_t(x|z)\, p_{\text{data}}(z)\, dz \]

How to read this: To sample \(X_t \sim p_t\), first draw \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\), then draw \(X_t \sim p_t(\cdot|z)\). We can sample from \(p_t\) easily — but we cannot evaluate \(p_t(x)\) for a given \(x\), because the integral is intractable. This asymmetry drives the whole design of flow matching.

Boundary conditions of the marginal path

Because the conditional path satisfies \(p_0(\cdot|z)=p_{\text{init}}\) and \(p_1(\cdot|z)=\delta_z\), the marginal path satisfies:

\[ p_0(x) = \int p_{\text{init}}(x)\, p_{\text{data}}(z)\, dz = p_{\text{init}}(x) \] \[ p_1(x) = \int \delta_z(x)\, p_{\text{data}}(z)\, dz = p_{\text{data}}(x) \]

So the marginal path automatically interpolates from noise to data — exactly what we need.

The Gaussian CondOT Path (Example 13 / Algorithm 3)

Mental picture

Imagine a Gaussian blob centered at the origin at \(t=0\). As \(t\) increases, the blob slides toward \(z\) while simultaneously shrinking. At \(t=1\) it collapses onto the point \(z\). The path traces a straight-line interpolation in mean-space, with the variance shrinking to zero.

The most important choice — used by Stable Diffusion 3, Movie Gen, and virtually all state-of-the-art models — is the Gaussian CondOT path with \(\alpha_t = t\), \(\beta_t = 1-t\):

\[ p_t(\cdot|z) = \mathcal{N}(tz,\; (1-t)^2 I_d) \]

Let us break down every part:

PartMeaning
\(\alpha_t = t\)Mean schedule: the mean slides from \(0\) (at \(t=0\)) to \(z\) (at \(t=1\))
\(\beta_t = 1-t\)Std-dev schedule: the spread shrinks from \(1\) (at \(t=0\)) to \(0\) (at \(t=1\))
\((1-t)^2 I_d\)Variance: a scalar multiple of the identity (isotropic Gaussian at every \(t\))
\(\mathcal{N}(tz, (1-t)^2 I_d)\)Full conditional distribution at time \(t\)

Boundary check:

  • At \(t=0\): \(\mathcal{N}(0\cdot z, 1^2 I_d) = \mathcal{N}(0,I_d) = p_{\text{init}}\). ✓
  • At \(t=1\): \(\mathcal{N}(1\cdot z, 0^2 I_d) = \delta_z\). ✓

How to sample: Given \(z\) and \(t\), draw \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\) and set:

\[ x_t = t\,z + (1-t)\,\varepsilon \]

This is a deterministic linear interpolation (in the sample, not just in distribution): the sample at time \(t\) is literally a convex combination of the noise \(\varepsilon\) and the data point \(z\).

10.2 Conditional and Marginal Vector Fields

MIT notes §3.2 — Equations (17), (18), (20)

Intuition

A probability path specifies what distributions we want at each time \(t\). A vector field specifies how particles move to achieve those distributions. For every fixed data point \(z\), we can work out — analytically, on paper — the exact velocity that a particle needs to have to follow the conditional path \(p_t(\cdot|z)\). This is the conditional vector field, and its closed form is the key that makes flow matching tractable.

Conditional vector field \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\)

SymbolMeaning
\(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\)The velocity at point \(x\) and time \(t\), given that we are targeting data point \(z\)
\(\psi_t^{\text{target}}(x_0|z)\)The flow map: where does the initial point \(x_0\) land at time \(t\) when targeting \(z\)?

The conditional vector field is any vector field whose ODE flow generates the conditional probability path. That is:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} X_t = u_t^{\text{target}}(X_t|z), \quad X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}} \implies X_t \sim p_t(\cdot|z) \text{ for all } t. \]

Deriving the conditional vector field for the CondOT path (Example 10)

The CondOT path samples as \(x_t = \alpha_t z + \beta_t \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\). We can think of this as a flow map:

\[ \psi_t^{\text{target}}(x_0|z) = \alpha_t z + \beta_t x_0 \]

where \(x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) plays the role of \(\varepsilon\). Let's verify: if \(X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\), then \(X_t = \alpha_t z + \beta_t X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\alpha_t z, \beta_t^2 I)\). This matches the conditional path exactly.

Now extract the velocity from the flow. By definition of a flow (Part 1), the vector field is the time-derivative of the flow map evaluated at the current point:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) = \frac{d}{dt}\psi_t^{\text{target}}(x_0|z) \Bigg|_{x_0 = (\psi_t^{\text{target}})^{-1}(x|z)} \]

Step 1: differentiate the flow map with respect to \(t\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\psi_t(x_0|z) = \dot\alpha_t z + \dot\beta_t x_0 \]

Step 2: invert the flow map — solve \(x = \alpha_t z + \beta_t x_0\) for \(x_0\):

\[ x_0 = \frac{x - \alpha_t z}{\beta_t} \]

Step 3: substitute back:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) = \dot\alpha_t z + \dot\beta_t \cdot \frac{x - \alpha_t z}{\beta_t} = \frac{\dot\beta_t}{\beta_t} x + \left(\dot\alpha_t - \frac{\dot\beta_t \alpha_t}{\beta_t}\right) z \]

For the CondOT path \(\alpha_t = t,\; \beta_t = 1-t\), we have \(\dot\alpha_t = 1,\; \dot\beta_t = -1\), so:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) = \frac{-1}{1-t} x + \left(1 - \frac{-t}{1-t}\right) z = \frac{-1}{1-t} x + \frac{1}{1-t} z = \frac{z - x}{1-t} \]
CondOT conditional velocity (closed form) \[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) = \frac{z - x}{1 - t} \]

Reading this: The velocity always points from the current position \(x\) toward the target \(z\), scaled by \(1/(1-t)\). As \(t \to 1\), the scaling diverges — the particle accelerates to reach \(z\) exactly at \(t=1\). This is the "straight-line" interpolation made precise.

Equivalently, using the reparameterization \(x_t = tz + (1-t)\varepsilon\):

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x_t|z) = z - \varepsilon \]

This is a constant velocity along the straight line from \(\varepsilon\) to \(z\). Beautifully simple.

Marginal vector field \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\)

The conditional field is great for a known \(z\), but at test time we don't know \(z\). What we need is the marginal field — the one that generates the marginal probability path \(p_t(x)\). By the marginalization trick (Theorem 9, below), it is:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x) = \int u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\, p_t(z|x)\, dz = \mathbb{E}\!\left[u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) \,\big|\, X_t = x\right] \]

This is a posterior-weighted average of conditional velocities: for a particle currently at \(x\), the marginal field says "average over all data points \(z\), weighting each by how likely it is that the particle at \(x\) came from \(z\)." The weight is the posterior \(p_t(z|x)\).

10.3 Theorem 9 — The Marginalization Trick

MIT notes Theorem 9, §3.2

Why this theorem is the conceptual centerpiece

We have two things: (a) conditional vector fields \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\) that are easy to compute analytically; (b) the marginal vector field \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\) that we actually need for generation, but can't compute directly. This theorem says they are connected by a simple expectation. If we train a model to match the conditional field in expectation, it automatically learns the marginal field. This is the flow-matching analogue of the score-matching identity from Part 3.

Theorem 9 (Marginalization Trick)

Let \(u_t^{\text{target}}(\cdot|z)\) be a conditional vector field that generates the conditional probability path \(p_t(\cdot|z)\). Define the marginal vector field by

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x) \;=\; \int u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\, \frac{p_t(x|z)\, p_{\text{data}}(z)}{p_t(x)}\, dz \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) \,\big|\, X_t = x\right]. \]

Then the marginal vector field generates the marginal probability path: the ODE \(\dot X_t = u_t^{\text{target}}(X_t)\) with \(X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}}\) satisfies \(X_t \sim p_t\) for all \(t\). In particular, \(X_1 \sim p_{\text{data}}\).

Breaking down the formula, symbol by symbol

SymbolMeaning
\(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\)The conditional velocity at point \(x\), time \(t\), targeting data point \(z\). This is analytically known.
\(p_t(x|z)\)The conditional path density at \(x\) given \(z\). This is analytically known (e.g., Gaussian).
\(p_{\text{data}}(z)\)The prior over data points. This is the data distribution.
\(p_t(x) = \int p_t(x|z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz\)The marginal density at \(x\). This is intractable to evaluate.
\(\frac{p_t(x|z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)}{p_t(x)} = p_t(z|x)\)The posterior over \(z\) given the observation \(x\) at time \(t\). This is Bayes' theorem.

The posterior weight \(p_t(z|x)\) tells us: "given that a particle is at position \(x\) at time \(t\), how likely is it that it originated from the data point \(z\)?" We take a weighted average of the conditional velocities (each pointing toward some \(z\)) and the weights encode our belief about where the particle is going.

Why this makes training tractable

The marginal field is a posterior expectation — intractable because we'd need to integrate over all data points. But we can sample from that expectation: pick a random \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\), sample \(x \sim p_t(\cdot|z)\), and compute \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\). This is an unbiased estimate of the posterior average. Minimizing a squared loss against this estimate is equivalent to minimizing against the true marginal target — exactly what Theorem 12 will prove.

Mirror: Score Matching Identity (Part 3)

In Part 3, the score marginalization identity said: \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_x \log p_t(x|z) \mid X_t = x]\). The structure is identical to Theorem 9. The conditional score is analytically known; the marginal score is the posterior average; training against the conditional in expectation gives you the marginal. Flow matching and score matching are the same structural idea, applied to velocity fields and score functions respectively.

10.4 Theorem 11 — The Continuity Equation

MIT notes Theorem 11, §3.2 — Equation (23)

Intuition from physics

Imagine a crowd of particles, each following the ODE \(\dot X_t = u_t(X_t)\). The density \(p_t(x)\) tells you how crowded each location \(x\) is at time \(t\). How does that density change? If the vector field is pushing particles into a region, the density there should increase. If particles are flowing out, density decreases. The divergence \(\nabla \cdot (p_t u_t)\) measures the net outflow of probability mass at \(x\). The continuity equation says: the time-rate-of-change of density equals the negative net outflow.

SymbolMeaning
\(\partial_t p_t(x)\)Time derivative of the density at the point \(x\): how fast is the probability mass at \(x\) changing?
\(\nabla \cdot v\) or \(\text{div}(v)\)Divergence of a vector field \(v\): \(\sum_{i=1}^d \partial_{x_i} v_i\). Measures net outflow rate.
\(\nabla \cdot (p_t u_t)\)Divergence of the probability flux \(p_t(x) u_t(x)\): net rate at which probability mass leaves \(x\).
Theorem 11 (Continuity Equation)

A vector field \(u_t\) generates the probability path \(p_t\) (i.e., the ODE \(\dot X_t = u_t(X_t)\) with \(X_0 \sim p_0\) gives \(X_t \sim p_t\)) if and only if the continuity equation holds:

\[ \partial_t p_t(x) = -\nabla \cdot (p_t(x)\, u_t(x)) \]

This is the certification criterion for a vector field. If you have a candidate \(u_t\) and want to verify it generates a given density evolution \(p_t\), you just plug into this PDE and check. This is how Example 10 was verified (footnote in the notes), and it is the tool used to prove Theorem 9.

Connection to Part 9

The probability-flow ODE from Part 9 was derived precisely by finding a deterministic vector field satisfying the continuity equation for the same density evolution as the SDE. The key insight there was that many vector fields can generate the same density path. Flow matching exploits this freedom: we design a convenient conditional path, find its conditional vector field analytically, and build the marginal via Theorem 9 — all while satisfying the continuity equation by construction.

10.5 Training: The Conditional Flow Matching Loss

MIT notes §3.3 — Theorem 12, Algorithm 3

The core tension

We want \(u_t^\theta \approx u_t^{\text{target}}\) (marginal). The loss that measures this directly is the flow matching (FM) loss. But evaluating \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\) requires an intractable integral. So we replace the target with \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\) (conditional), which we can compute analytically. Theorem 12 proves this substitution is exactly correct: the two losses have the same gradients with respect to \(\theta\).

Flow Matching (FM) loss — the ideal but intractable version

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \text{Unif}[0,1],\; x \sim p_t} \left\| u_t^\theta(x) - u_t^{\text{target}}(x) \right\|^2 \]

This says: at a random time \(t\), sample a point from the marginal path, and penalize the squared difference between the model output and the true marginal velocity. Unattainable in practice because \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\) and even \(p_t(x)\) are intractable.

Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) loss — the tractable version

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \text{Unif}[0,1],\; z \sim p_{\text{data}},\; x \sim p_t(\cdot|z)} \left\| u_t^\theta(x) - u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) \right\|^2 \]
SymbolMeaning
\(t \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]\)Random time step, drawn uniformly
\(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\)A data sample (from the training set)
\(x \sim p_t(\cdot|z)\)A noisy version of \(z\) at time \(t\), i.e. \(x = tz + (1-t)\varepsilon\) for \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\)
\(u_t^\theta(x)\)Neural network output: predicted velocity at \((x, t)\)
\(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\)Analytic conditional velocity: for CondOT, this is \((z-x)/(1-t)\) or equivalently \(z - \varepsilon\)
\(\|\cdot\|^2\)Squared Euclidean norm (summed over all \(d\) dimensions)

Every quantity in \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}\) is either sampleable or analytically computable. This is what makes flow matching "simulation-free": we never run the ODE during training.

Theorem 12 — Gradients of FM and CFM are identical

The marginal and conditional flow matching losses differ by a constant \(C\) that does not depend on \(\theta\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) + C \]

Therefore their gradients are equal:

\[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) \]

In particular, the minimizer \(\theta^*\) of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}\) satisfies \(u_t^{\theta^*} = u_t^{\text{target}}\) (assuming a sufficiently expressive neural network).

Proof sketch — why the constant appears

Expand \(\|a - b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 - 2a^\top b + \|b\|^2\). Applying this to the FM loss with \(a = u_t^\theta(x)\) and \(b = u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\|u_t^\theta\|^2\right] - 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[{u_t^\theta}^\top u_t^{\text{target}}\right] + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\|u_t^{\text{target}}\|^2\right]}_{=C_1,\;\text{const. in }\theta} \]

The crucial step is the cross-term. By the marginalization trick (Theorem 9), \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x) = \mathbb{E}[u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) \mid X_t = x]\), so:

\[ \mathbb{E}_x\!\left[{u_t^\theta(x)}^\top u_t^{\text{target}}(x)\right] = \mathbb{E}_{x,z}\!\left[{u_t^\theta(x)}^\top u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\right] \]

Substituting this back and completing the square with \(\|u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z)\|^2\) gives exactly \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) + C\). The constant \(C\) absorbs the \(\|u_t^{\text{target}}\|^2\) and \(\|u_t^{\text{target}}(\cdot|z)\|^2\) terms, neither of which depends on \(\theta\).

Algorithm 3 — Flow Matching Training (CondOT path)

Algorithm 3 (MIT notes, §3.3)

Input: Dataset of samples \(z \sim p_{\text{data}}\), neural network \(u_t^\theta\).

  1. For each mini-batch of data:
  2. Sample a data example \(z\) from the dataset.
  3. Sample a random time \(t \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]\).
  4. Sample noise \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\).
  5. Form the noisy sample: \(x_t = t\,z + (1-t)\,\varepsilon\).
  6. Compute the loss: \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \|u_t^\theta(x_t) - (z - \varepsilon)\|^2\).
  7. Update \(\theta \leftarrow \text{grad\_update}(\mathcal{L}(\theta))\).
  8. End for.

Note: the target velocity \((z - \varepsilon)\) for the CondOT path follows from \(u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) = (z-x)/(1-t)\) with \(x = tz + (1-t)\varepsilon\):

\[ \frac{z - (tz + (1-t)\varepsilon)}{1-t} = \frac{(1-t)z - (1-t)\varepsilon}{1-t} = z - \varepsilon. \]
Why the target simplifies to \(z - \varepsilon\)

This simplification only holds for the CondOT path (\(\alpha_t = t,\, \beta_t = 1-t\)). For general noise schedulers, the target is \(\dot\alpha_t z + \dot\beta_t \varepsilon\). The CondOT case is special because both \(\dot\alpha_t = 1\) and \(\dot\beta_t = -1\) are constant, making the target independent of \(t\). This is one reason CondOT is preferred: the regression target doesn't change with time.

10.6 Connection to Diffusion: The Triangle Extended

MIT notes §3, Summary 14 — relationships between FM, score, and diffusion

How Flow Matching Generalizes Diffusion

In Part 3, we saw a triangle: the noise prediction \(\hat\varepsilon\), the score \(\nabla_x \log p_t\), and the denoised prediction \(\hat x_0\) are linear reparameterizations of each other. Flow matching adds a fourth vertex to this triangle: the FM velocity \(u_t\).

For a Gaussian diffusion forward process with \(\alpha_t z + \sigma_t \varepsilon\), the FM velocity and the probability-flow ODE velocity (Part 9) are related by:

\[ u_t^{\text{FM}}(x) = \dot\alpha_t \hat{z}_\theta(x,t) + \dot\sigma_t \hat\varepsilon_\theta(x,t) \]

where \(\hat z_\theta\) is the denoised data prediction and \(\hat\varepsilon_\theta\) is the noise prediction. For the CondOT path this becomes \(u_t^{\text{FM}} = \hat z_\theta - \hat\varepsilon_\theta = \hat z_\theta - \hat\varepsilon_\theta\), a constant-velocity straight line.

The key insight: The PF-ODE from Part 9 and the FM ODE are the same mathematical object when trained correctly. Flow matching is simply the cleanest, most direct way to train a PF-ODE velocity field — no SDE, no Tweedie's formula, no reverse process derivation needed. The CFM loss is a direct regression, the target is analytically known, and the training is simulation-free.

Why FM is preferred in practice:

  • Straighter trajectories: CondOT paths are straight lines in sample space, so fewer Euler steps are needed at inference.
  • Flexibility: Any probability path works — FM is not tied to Gaussian diffusion.
  • Simplicity: Algorithm 3 is arguably the simplest possible generative model training loop.

10.7 Runnable PyTorch: 2D Toy Flow Matching

Teaching code

This code is for illustration only. It shows the exact operations of Algorithm 3 on a tiny 2D problem. Do not interpret it as production code.

# Flow Matching: 2D toy example
# teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG
# All tensor shapes are annotated inline.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(42)

# ---- Data distribution: a simple 2D mixture of two Gaussians ----
def sample_data(n):
    # Returns shape: (n, 2)
    centers = torch.tensor([[2.0, 2.0], [-2.0, -2.0]])  # shape: (2, 2)
    idx = torch.randint(0, 2, (n,))                      # shape: (n,)
    z = centers[idx] + 0.3 * torch.randn(n, 2)          # shape: (n, 2)
    return z

# ---- A tiny MLP that takes (x, t) and outputs velocity ----
class VelocityMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        # Input: x (2-dim) concatenated with t (1-dim) = 3-dim
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(3, 64),  # (3,) -> (64,)
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 64),
            nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 2),  # (64,) -> (2,)  = predicted velocity
        )

    def forward(self, x, t):
        # x: (batch, 2), t: (batch, 1)
        xt_input = torch.cat([x, t], dim=-1)  # (batch, 3)
        return self.net(xt_input)             # (batch, 2)

model = VelocityMLP()
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

# ---- compute_loss: implements Algorithm 3, lines 2-6 ----
def compute_loss(model, z):
    # z: (batch, 2)  — one mini-batch of data points
    batch = z.shape[0]

    # Step 3: sample t ~ Unif[0, 1]
    t = torch.rand(batch, 1)              # shape: (batch, 1)

    # Step 4: sample noise epsilon ~ N(0, I)
    eps = torch.randn_like(z)             # shape: (batch, 2)

    # Step 5: form x_t = t*z + (1-t)*eps  (CondOT interpolation)
    x_t = t * z + (1 - t) * eps          # shape: (batch, 2)

    # Analytic target velocity for CondOT: u_t(x|z) = z - eps
    # (constant along each straight-line trajectory)
    v_target = z - eps                    # shape: (batch, 2)

    # Step 6: compute CFM loss = ||model(x_t, t) - v_target||^2
    v_pred = model(x_t, t)               # shape: (batch, 2)
    loss = ((v_pred - v_target) ** 2).mean()  # scalar
    return loss

# ---- Training loop (abbreviated; run ~2000 steps for convergence) ----
for step in range(500):
    z_batch = sample_data(256)           # shape: (256, 2)
    loss = compute_loss(model, z_batch)
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    if step % 100 == 0:
        print(f"step {step:4d}  loss {loss.item():.4f}")
# Expected output (approx): step    0  loss 5.0xxx
#                           step  100  loss 0.9xxx
#                           step  400  loss 0.25xx

# ---- Sampling: Euler-integrate from p_init to p_data ----
def sample(model, n=512, steps=50):
    # No gradients needed at inference time
    model.eval()
    dt = 1.0 / steps                     # scalar: Euler step size

    # Start at t=0: X_0 ~ N(0, I_2)
    x = torch.randn(n, 2)               # shape: (n, 2)

    with torch.no_grad():
        for i in range(steps):
            t_val = i * dt               # current time in [0, 1)
            t_tensor = torch.full((n, 1), t_val)  # shape: (n, 1)

            # Euler update: x_{t+dt} = x_t + dt * u_theta(x_t, t)
            v = model(x, t_tensor)       # shape: (n, 2)
            x = x + dt * v              # shape: (n, 2)

    # x now approximates samples from p_data
    return x                             # shape: (n, 2)

samples = sample(model, n=512, steps=50)
print("samples shape:", samples.shape)  # Expected: torch.Size([512, 2])
print("samples mean:", samples.mean(0)) # Expected: near [~0, ~0] (mixture center)
print("samples std: ", samples.std(0))  # Expected: near [~2.x, ~2.x] (spread of mixture)

10.8 Summary 14 — Flow Matching at a Glance

MIT notes Summary 14, §3

Summary 14 (MIT notes)

Goal: Learn the marginal vector field \(u_t^{\text{target}}\) such that \(\dot X_t = u_t^{\text{target}}(X_t)\), \(X_0 \sim p_{\text{init}}\) gives \(X_1 \sim p_{\text{data}}\).

Step 1 — Choose a conditional probability path:

\[ p_t(\cdot|z) \text{ with } p_0(\cdot|z) = p_{\text{init}},\quad p_1(\cdot|z) = \delta_z \]

Step 2 — Derive the conditional vector field analytically:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) \text{ such that its flow satisfies } \psi_t(\cdot|z)_\sharp p_{\text{init}} = p_t(\cdot|z) \]

Step 3 — Build the marginal field via the marginalization trick:

\[ u_t^{\text{target}}(x) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[u_t^{\text{target}}(x|z) \mid X_t = x\right] \]

Step 4 — Train by minimizing the CFM loss:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,z,\varepsilon}\!\left[\left\|u_t^\theta(tz + (1-t)\varepsilon) - (z - \varepsilon)\right\|^2\right] \]

Gaussian CondOT specialization (\(\alpha_t = t,\, \beta_t = 1-t\)):

\[ p_t(\cdot|z) = \mathcal{N}(tz,\; (1-t)^2 I_d), \quad x_t = tz + (1-t)\varepsilon, \quad u_t^{\text{target}}(x_t|z) = z - \varepsilon \]
