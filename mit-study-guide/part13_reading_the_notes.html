Reading §1–2: Generative Modeling and the ODE/SDE Machinery

§1 Introduction — Key Ideas 1–4 and Summary 2

Unlocked by Guide Part 4 (big picture of diffusion) and Part 0 (math notation).

What it says. Section 1 sets up the problem in four key ideas. Key Idea 1: data objects (images, videos, proteins) are vectors \(z\in\mathbb{R}^d\). Key Idea 2: generating means sampling from \(p_\text{data}\). Key Idea 3: a dataset is a finite set of i.i.d. samples from \(p_\text{data}\). Key Idea 4: guided generation means sampling from the conditional \(p_\text{data}(\cdot|y)\). Summary 2 restates these four points. §1.1 and §1.2 are the overview and roadmap — read them as a preview, not a source of new mathematics.

Symbol to watch. The notes use \(z\) (not \(x\)) for a clean data point throughout §1–3. This is the standard variable for a sample from the data set, while \(x\) will later denote an intermediate noisy point on a probability path.

§2.1 Flow Models — Theorem 3, Example 4, Algorithm 1

Unlocked by Guide Part 1 (differential equations and flows).

What it says. A vector field \(u_t:\mathbb{R}^d\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^d\) defines an ODE \(\dot{X}_t = u_t(X_t)\). The solution is the flow \(\psi_t\), which maps initial conditions forward in time. Theorem 3 guarantees existence and uniqueness of \(\psi_t\) when \(u_t\) is \(C^1\) with bounded derivative — always satisfied for neural networks. Example 4 is the linear case \(u_t(x)=-\theta x\), whose exact flow is \(\psi_t(x_0)=e^{-\theta t}x_0\). Algorithm 1 is the Euler method: \(X_{t+h}=X_t+h\,u_t^\theta(X_t)\).

Orientation You already built intuition for flows in Part 1. The notes' Definition/Theorem/Algorithm sequence is a compact formal restatement of exactly that content. Read Example 4 to check your algebra, then move on.

§2.2 Diffusion Models — Theorem 5, Example 6 (OU), Algorithm 2, Summary 7

Unlocked by Guide Parts 2 (Brownian motion, Itô calculus) and 7 (continuous-time diffusion).

What it says. The SDE \(dX_t = u_t(X_t)\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t\) extends the ODE by injecting Brownian noise at each step. Theorem 5 gives existence/uniqueness (analogous to Theorem 3 but for SDEs). Example 6 is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process: \(dX_t = -\theta X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\), which has a Gaussian stationary distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2/(2\theta))\). Algorithm 2 is Euler–Maruyama: add a \(\sigma_t\sqrt{h}\,\varepsilon\) noise term to the Euler step. Summary 7 frames the full diffusion model: a neural network \(u_t^\theta\) paired with a fixed \(\sigma_t\), initialized from \(p_\text{init}\), run to \(t=1\) to produce \(p_\text{data}\). A flow model is the special case \(\sigma_t=0\).

Watch out The notes never actually train the model in §2 — they only define how to sample once the network is trained. Training comes in §§3–4. Keep this separation clear.

Reading §3: Flow Matching

§3.1 Conditional and Marginal Probability Paths — Example 8 (Gaussian path)

Unlocked by Guide Part 10 (flow matching, conditional/marginal paths).

What it says. A conditional probability path \(p_t(x|z)\) is a family of distributions that start at \(p_\text{init}\) and end at \(\delta_z\) (a point mass at the data point). Marginalising over \(z\sim p_\text{data}\) gives the marginal path \(p_t(x)=\int p_t(x|z)\,p_\text{data}(z)\,dz\), which starts at \(p_\text{init}\) and ends at \(p_\text{data}\). Example 8 is the Gaussian path \(p_t(\cdot|z)=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_t z,\beta_t^2 I)\) with noise schedulers \(\alpha_t,\beta_t\).

Symbol to watch. The integral in the marginal \(p_t(x)=\int\cdots\) is tractable for sampling (draw \(z\sim p_\text{data}\), then sample \(x\sim p_t(\cdot|z)\)) but intractable for density evaluation. This asymmetry drives the entire flow-matching design.

§3.2 Conditional and Marginal Vector Fields — Theorem 9, Example 10, Theorem 11

Unlocked by Guide Part 10 (marginalization trick, continuity equation).

What it says. A conditional vector field \(u_t^\text{target}(x|z)\) is any field whose ODE follows the conditional path \(p_t(\cdot|z)\). Theorem 9 is the marginalization trick: the weighted average \(u_t^\text{target}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_\text{data}}[u_t^\text{target}(x|z)\,|\,x]\) is the marginal vector field and its ODE follows the marginal path, giving \(X_1\sim p_\text{data}\). Example 10 derives the explicit conditional Gaussian field \(u_t^\text{target}(x|z)=\dot\alpha_t z + \dot\beta_t\,(x-\alpha_t z)/\beta_t\). Theorem 11 is the continuity equation \(\partial_t p_t = -\text{div}(p_t u_t)\), the ODE analogue of conservation of probability mass.

Core algebra The proof of Theorem 9 is a single chain of equalities: show that the marginal field satisfies the continuity equation by substituting the definition of \(p_t(x)\) and swapping the integral with the divergence operator. Guide Part 10 walked through this step-by-step. Re-read it there if the notes' proof feels rushed.

§3.3 Learning the Marginal Vector Field — Theorem 12, Algorithm 3, Example 13, Summary 14

Unlocked by Guide Part 10 (CFM loss) and Part 11 (CondOT / rectified flow as the \(\alpha_t=t\) special case).

What it says. Theorem 12 is the central training result: the marginal FM loss \(L_\text{FM}(\theta)\) and the conditional FM loss \(L_\text{CFM}(\theta)\) differ only by a constant in \(\theta\), so minimising \(L_\text{CFM}\) is equivalent to minimising \(L_\text{FM}\). The proof expands the MSE and uses the same integral-swap trick as Theorem 9. Algorithm 3 summarises the training loop for the CondOT Gaussian path (\(\alpha_t=t,\beta_t=1-t\)). Example 13 instantiates the CFM loss for general Gaussian paths, giving \(L_\text{CFM}=\mathbb{E}[\|\,u_t^\theta(\alpha_t z+\beta_t\varepsilon)-(\dot\alpha_t z+\dot\beta_t\varepsilon)\,\|^2]\). Summary 14 is the reference card for all of §3.

Key insight Algorithm 3 is simulation-free: you never unroll an ODE during training. You only sample \(t\), \(z\), \(\varepsilon\), form \(x=\alpha_t z+\beta_t\varepsilon\), and regress the network against the closed-form target. This is the practical reason flow matching dominates large-scale training.

Reading §4: Score Functions and Score Matching

§4.1 Conditional and Marginal Score Functions — Proposition 1, Remark 16

Unlocked by Guide Parts 3 (score functions) and 8 (SDE formulation).

What it says. The score is \(\nabla\log p_t(x)\). For Gaussian paths the conditional score is \(\nabla\log p_t(x|z)=({\alpha_t z - x})/{\beta_t^2}\), linear in both \(x\) and \(z\). Proposition 1 (the conversion formula) shows that for Gaussian paths the conditional vector field and the conditional score satisfy \(u_t^\text{target}(x|z) = a_t x + b_t s_t^\theta(x)\) (and equivalently for marginals), so learning one gives the other for free. Remark 16 generalises: any parameterisation that recovers the posterior mean \(\mathbb{E}[z|x]\) — the vector field, the score, or the denoiser \(D_t(x)\) — is equivalent. DDPM originally learned the noise \(\varepsilon_t^\theta\), which is a rescaled version of the score.

§4.2 Sampling with SDEs — Theorem 17, Example 18, Theorem 19, Remark 20

Unlocked by Guide Parts 8 (reverse SDE, Fokker–Planck) and 9 (probability-flow ODE).

What it says. Theorem 17 is the SDE extension trick: given a trained flow model (ODE with drift \(u_t^\text{target}\)), you can add any diffusion coefficient \(\sigma_t\ge 0\) by augmenting the drift with the score — \(u_t^\text{SDE}=u_t^\text{target}+\tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t\) — without retraining. The resulting SDE still follows the marginal path \(p_t\). Example 18 instantiates this for Gaussian paths using Proposition 1. Theorem 19 is the Fokker–Planck equation \(\partial_t p_t=-\text{div}(p_t u_t)+\tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\Delta p_t\), the SDE analogue of the continuity equation; it is the technical workhorse of Theorem 17's proof. Remark 20 is Langevin dynamics: the special case \(u_t=0\), which drives any initial distribution towards the stationary distribution \(p\).

Important nuance Theorem 17 says you can pick \(\sigma_t\) after training. In theory any \(\sigma_t\ge 0\) preserves the marginals. In practice, large \(\sigma_t\) requires smaller step sizes (Algorithm 2), so there is an empirical optimum. The probability-flow ODE (\(\sigma_t=0\)) has fewer NFEs; stochastic sampling (\(\sigma_t>0\)) can escape local errors but is slower.

§4.3 Score Matching — Theorem 22, Algorithm 4

Unlocked by Guide Parts 3 (score matching) and 7 (DDPM's noise-prediction loss).

What it says. Theorem 22 is the score-matching analogue of Theorem 12: the score matching loss equals the denoising score matching loss up to a constant. The proof is word-for-word the same — replace \(u_t^\text{target}\) with \(\nabla\log p_t\). Algorithm 4 summarises DDPM training: for Gaussian paths the score network predicts \(-\varepsilon/\beta_t\) (or equivalently the noise \(\varepsilon\) after DDPM's reparameterisation).

Unification moment After reading §3.3 and §4.3 together you see that flow matching and score matching solve the same optimisation problem: both regress a neural network against a tractable conditional target. They differ only in what that target represents (velocity vs. score), but Proposition 1 shows they are the same for Gaussian paths.

Reading §5: Guidance

§5 Guidance — Remark 25, §5.1 Vanilla, §5.2 Classifier-Free, Remark 28

Unlocked by Guide Part 12 (classifier-free guidance, CFG-CFM loss).

What it says. Remark 25 clarifies terminology: "conditional" in the notes always means conditioning on a data point \(z\), while "guided" means conditioning on a prompt \(y\). This avoids overloading the word "conditional". §5.1 Vanilla guidance feeds \(y\) into the network alongside \(x\) and trains with the guided CFM objective Eq. (58); straightforward but empirically under-conditions on \(y\). §5.2 Classifier-free guidance derives CFG from Bayes' rule: the guided score decomposes into the unguided score plus \(\nabla\log p_t(y|x)\) (a classifier gradient). Scaling that classifier gradient by \(w>1\) amplifies adherence to \(y\). Rather than training a separate classifier, a single network is trained with both \(u_t^\theta(x|y)\) and \(u_t^\theta(x|\emptyset)\) using label-dropout probability \(\eta\). At inference, \(\tilde{u}_t=(1+w)u_t^\theta(x|y)-w\,u_t^\theta(x|\emptyset)\). The CFG-CFM objective (Summary 27) is the production-ready training loss. Remark 28 notes that everything extends trivially to diffusion models by replacing \(u\) with \(\tilde{u}\) in Algorithm 2.

Watch out For \(w>1\) the generated distribution is no longer \(p_\text{data}(\cdot|y)\) — it is a biased distribution that over-emphasises \(y\). CFG is an empirical heuristic, not a probabilistically grounded operation. The notes are careful to say this.

Reading §6: Building Large-Scale Generators

§6.1 Neural Network Architectures — Embeddings, Remark 29 (DiT block), §6.1.3 U-Net

Unlocked by Guide Part 12 (DiT, U-Net, embeddings).

What it says. The neural network \(u_t^\theta(x|y)\) must accept three inputs: an image-shaped \(x\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}\), a conditioning variable \(y\), and a scalar time \(t\in[0,1]\). Time is embedded via Fourier features (Eq. 65); class labels via a learned look-up; text via frozen CLIP or T5 models. A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) patchifies the image, runs multi-head self-attention over patch tokens and cross-attention to text tokens, and uses adaptive LayerNorm (AdaLN) for time conditioning. Remark 29 spells out the DiT block math: scaled dot-product attention, multi-head projection, AdaLN modulation. A U-Net uses an encoder–midcoder–decoder stack with residual skip connections; it is the backbone of early DDPM/DDIM papers. For modern large-scale models the DiT dominates; the U-Net is still used in some labs and ablation studies.

§6.2 Working in Latent Space — Remark 30 (KL), Algorithm 6 (\(\beta\)-VAE), Remark 32

Unlocked by Guide Part 12 (VAE, latent diffusion).

What it says. Raw pixel space at 1024×1024 is \(d\approx 3\times10^6\); training a flow model there is prohibitive. A VAE compresses to a tractable latent \(z\in\mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k\ll d\). The encoder \(q_\phi(z|x)\) and decoder \(p_\theta(x|z)\) are Gaussian; training minimises the reconstruction loss plus a KL penalty that keeps the latent distribution close to \(\mathcal{N}(0,I_k)\). Remark 30 is a self-contained KL-divergence primer. Algorithm 6 is the \(\beta\)-VAE training loop with the reparameterisation trick. Remark 32 states the latent-diffusion recipe: train the VAE first, then train the flow/diffusion model on the latent distribution; at inference, decode with \(\mu_\theta(z)\) (not a stochastic sample) to avoid noise artifacts.

§6.3 Case Study: Stable Diffusion 3 and Meta Movie Gen

Unlocked by Guide Parts 11 (rectified flow / CondOT) and 12 (full system architecture).

What it says. Stable Diffusion 3 uses the CondOT path (\(\alpha_t=t,\beta_t=1-t\) — i.e. rectified flow), a pre-trained VAE latent space, three text embeddings (two CLIP variants + T5-XXL), and an MM-DiT that attends jointly to image patches and text tokens. Their largest model has 8B parameters; sampling uses 50 Euler steps with CFG weight 2–5. Meta Movie Gen extends the same recipe to video: a temporal VAE compresses \(T\times H\times W\) video frames; the DiT attends along both spatial and temporal patch dimensions; three text encoders (UL2 + ByT5 + MetaCLIP). Largest model: 30B parameters.

Big picture SD3 and Movie Gen are not new algorithms — they are careful engineering combinations of everything in §§3–5: CondOT flow matching + CFG + latent VAE + DiT backbone. The main novel contributions are architectural (MM-DiT) and scale.

Reading §7: Discrete Diffusion Models

New territory This is the one chapter not covered in depth in the guide. Read the short primer here first, then return to the notes.

§7 Primer — What is a CTMC and why does it look familiar?

So far every model lived in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Language is a sequence of discrete tokens \(x\in\mathcal{V}^d\) where \(\mathcal{V}\) is a vocabulary. SDEs do not exist on discrete spaces — there is no "infinitesimal step in a direction". Instead, we use a Continuous-Time Markov Chain (CTMC), the discrete analogue of an SDE.

The analogy table:

Continuous (SDE)Discrete (CTMC)
State space \(\mathbb{R}^d\)State space \(\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}^d\) (finite/countable)
Drift \(u_t(x)\in\mathbb{R}^d\)Rate matrix \(Q_t(y|x)\ge 0\) for \(y\ne x\)
ODE continuity equationKolmogorov Forward Equation (KFE, Prop 2)
Flow existence (Thm 3)CTMC existence (Thm 33; proved via KFE as a linear ODE)
Conditional vector field \(u_t^\text{target}(x|z)\)Conditional rate matrix \(Q_t^z(y|x)\)
Marginalization trick (Thm 9)Discrete marginalization trick (Thm 36)
CFM loss: MSE against \(u_t^\text{target}(x|z)\)DFM loss: cross-entropy against \(p_{1|t}(z_j|x)\) (Alg 8)
Euler method (Alg 1)Euler-like categorical sampler (Alg 7)

The key intuition: instead of asking "in which direction should I move \(x\)?", the CTMC asks "at which rate should I jump from token \(x\) to token \(y\)?". The rate matrix \(Q_t(y|x)\) is non-negative for \(y\ne x\) and has diagonal entries chosen so each column sums to zero (a consistency condition analogous to divergence-free probability flux).

§7.1 CTMC Models — Theorem 33, Example 34

What it says. Theorem 33 is the discrete analogue of Theorem 3: given any valid rate matrix \(Q_t\), there exists a unique CTMC. The proof uses the fact that the KFE (Kolmogorov Forward Equation) is a linear ODE over the transition matrix, so uniqueness of ODEs (Theorem 3 again!) applies directly. Example 34 is a two-state chain \(\mathcal{S}=\{a,b\}\) with constant jump rate \(\lambda\): the transition probability decays as \(e^{-2\lambda h}\) and the chain forgets its initial state as \(h\to\infty\). Think of this as the discrete analogue of the OU process (Example 6) converging to a Gaussian.

In practice, the CTMC is factorized: the rate \(Q_t(y|x)=0\) unless \(x\) and \(y\) differ by exactly one token. This sparsity constraint (Figure 18 in the notes) makes the output shape \(d\times|\mathcal{V}|\) — linear in sequence length — instead of exponential.

§7.2 Training CTMC Models — Theorem 36, Proposition 2 (KFE), Theorem 38, Example 39 (MDLM), Algorithm 8

What it says. The training recipe mirrors §3 exactly in three steps.

  1. Conditional & marginal probability paths (§7.2.1): The factorized mixture path (Example 35) independently masks or noises each token with probability \(1-\kappa_t\), where \(\kappa_t\) is a scheduler with \(\kappa_0=0,\kappa_1=1\). This is the discrete analogue of the Gaussian path — not a smooth interpolation in space, but a smooth mixture of "data token" and "noise token".
  2. Conditional & marginal rate matrices (§7.2.2): Theorem 36 (discrete marginalization trick) mirrors Theorem 9: the marginal rate matrix \(Q_t(y|x)=\mathbb{E}[Q_t^z(y|x)|x]\) generates a CTMC that follows the marginal path. Proposition 2 (Kolmogorov Forward Equation) is the discrete analogue of the continuity equation and is used to prove Theorem 36 exactly as Theorem 11 was used to prove Theorem 9.
  3. Learning the marginal rate matrix (§7.2.3): Theorem 38 shows that for the factorized mixture path the marginal rate reduces to predicting \(p_{1|t}(z_j=v_i|x)\) — the probability that token \(j\) of the clean sequence is \(v_i\), given the noisy sequence \(x\). This is just a per-position classifier. Algorithm 8 trains a transformer on a cross-entropy loss against the clean tokens. Example 39 is the masked diffusion language model (MDLM): add a \([\text{mask}]\) token to \(\mathcal{V}\), start from a fully masked sequence, and iteratively unmask tokens during inference — the discrete analogue of denoising.
The surprising simplicity Just as continuous flow matching reduces to simple velocity regression (Algorithm 3), discrete flow matching reduces to simple per-token classification (Algorithm 8). The mathematical machinery is heavier but the training objective is no more exotic than a standard language model cross-entropy loss. Current state-of-the-art discrete diffusion models (e.g. LLaDA) follow exactly this recipe.

Appendix B — Proof of the Fokker–Planck Equation

Unlocked by Guide Part 8 (SDE formulation and Fokker–Planck).

A self-contained, test-function proof using integration by parts. The key steps: (1) write the SDE update as a 2nd-order Taylor expansion of a test function; (2) take expectations; (3) identify divergence and Laplacian terms via integration by parts. Sufficiency follows from the uniqueness of parabolic PDEs. This is mathematically the most demanding appendix; treat it as optional unless you want the derivation rigour.

Appendix C — Existence and Uniqueness of CTMCs

Unlocked by §7 primer above.

Proves Theorem 33 by casting the KFE as a linear ODE over the space of transition matrices, then invoking the ODE uniqueness theorem (Theorem 3). Existence follows from the fact that linear ODEs always have solutions. Read this if you want to understand why CTMCs are well-defined.

Appendix D — Additional Perspectives on VAEs

Unlocked by Guide Part 12 (VAEs, latent diffusion).

Three supplementary viewpoints: (1) Proposition 3 (chain rule for KL divergences) underpins the data-processing inequality and shows why the VAE loss is an upper bound on the data-distribution KL. (2) Remarks 42–44 analyse what happens at perfect training and connect to the ELBO (evidence lower bound). (3) The division-of-labour discussion (Intuition 44) explains the rate–distortion tradeoff: the VAE must balance compression (easy latents for the generative model) against reconstruction fidelity. This tradeoff is why modern latent diffusion models use a small KL weight \(\beta\ll 1\).

An invaluable Rosetta Stone for the broader literature. Summary 45 catalogues the most common notational differences: (1) discrete-time DDPM vs. continuous-time SDE; (2) inverted time convention (\(t=0\) means data, \(t=1\) means noise — the reverse of the notes' convention); (3) "forward process" as a concrete way to construct a Gaussian probability path; (4) time-reversal SDEs (Anderson 1982) versus the Fokker–Planck approach used in this class. After this appendix you should be able to map any paper from the DDPM/DDIM/Song et al./Lipman et al./Liu et al. family back to the unified notation of the notes.

Where to Go Next

Original papers (read in this order)
  1. DDPM — Ho, Jain, Abbeel (NeurIPS 2020). The original denoising diffusion paper; uses discrete time and noise-prediction parameterisation.
  2. DDIM — Song, Meng, Ermon (ICLR 2021). Derives deterministic sampling from the DDPM objective; connects to the probability-flow ODE.
  3. Score SDE — Song et al. (ICLR 2021, arXiv 2011.13456). The continuous-time unification; introduces the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE as a single framework.
  4. Flow Matching — Lipman et al. (ICLR 2023, arXiv 2210.02747). The first flow-matching paper; introduces conditional probability paths and the CFM loss.
  5. Rectified Flow — Liu, Gong, Liu (arXiv 2022.03003). Straight-line paths, reflow, and fast one-step sampling; backbone of SD3.
  6. Stable Diffusion 3 — Esser et al. (arXiv 2403.03206). MM-DiT + CondOT flow + latent space; 8B parameter image model.
  7. Flow Matching Guide and Code — Lipman et al. (arXiv 2412.06264). The comprehensive companion; covers geometry, discrete spaces, and code examples.
