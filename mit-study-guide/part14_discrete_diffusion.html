Part 14Discrete Diffusion — Diffusion Language Models

Big picture — why this part exists

Everything in Parts 1–13 assumed the data lives in a continuous vector space \(\mathbb{R}^d\): pixels, latents, audio waveforms. You added Gaussian noise, reversed it with a score function or vector field, and called it done.

Text is different. A sentence is a sequence of discrete tokens drawn from a vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\). There is no notion of "add a little Gaussian noise to the word cat." You cannot take a derivative with respect to a token index. SDEs and ODEs simply do not exist in a finite set.

The fix: replace the SDE/ODE with a Continuous-Time Markov Chain (CTMC) — a stochastic process that jumps between discrete states rather than drifting continuously. Every concept you learned has a discrete-state twin. That is the thesis of §7 of the MIT notes and the thesis of this page.

Orienting analogy — one-liner dictionary

Continuous world (Parts 1–13): state \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\), moves smoothly, governed by a vector field or drift.
Discrete world (Part 14): state \(X_t \in \mathcal{S}\) (a finite set), jumps instantaneously, governed by a rate matrix.

Everything else — probability path, marginalization trick, continuity / Fokker–Planck equation, simulation algorithm, training loss — has an exact counterpart. We will point to each one as we go.

14.1 The Discrete State Space

Prerequisite — discrete-state stochastic process

In Parts 1–2 you saw \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\): at every time \(t\) the state is a real-valued vector. Now the state lives in a finite set.

Vocabulary: \(\mathcal{V} = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_V\}\), where \(V = |\mathcal{V}|\). For English text, \(\mathcal{V}\) might be 50 000 BPE tokens; for DNA, \(\mathcal{V} = \{A, T, G, C\}\).

State space: \(\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{V}^d\), all sequences of length \(d\). A single element \(x \in \mathcal{S}\) is a tuple \(x = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)\) with each \(x_j \in \mathcal{V}\). Note \(|\mathcal{S}| = V^d\) — exponentially large.

Distribution over \(\mathcal{S}\): a probability mass function \(p_t : \mathcal{S} \to [0,1]\) with \(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} p_t(x) = 1\). This is the discrete analogue of the density \(p_t(x)\) from Part 8.

14.2 Continuous-Time Markov Chains (§7.1)

A Continuous-Time Markov Chain (CTMC) is a random trajectory \(X : [0,1] \to \mathcal{S}\) that is piecewise constant — it stays at a state for a random holding time, then jumps to a new state. The "continuous-time" part means the jumps can happen at any real-valued \(t\), not just at integer steps. The "Markov" part means the future depends only on the present state, not the entire history (exactly like SDEs/ODEs — they are also Markov).

The Rate Matrix \(Q_t\) — the discrete vector field

Definition — rate matrix (generator)

Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be our finite state space. A rate matrix \(Q_t\) is a function

\[Q_t : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}, \quad (x, y) \mapsto Q_t(y \mid x)\]

satisfying two conditions:

  1. Non-negative off-diagonal: \(Q_t(y \mid x) \geq 0\) for all \(y \neq x\). (You can't have a negative rate of jumping somewhere.)
  2. Rows sum to zero: \(\sum_{y \in \mathcal{S}} Q_t(y \mid x) = 0\) for all \(x\). This forces \(Q_t(x \mid x) = -\sum_{y \neq x} Q_t(y \mid x) \leq 0\). (Total probability is conserved.)

Think of \(Q_t\) as a \(|\mathcal{S}| \times |\mathcal{S}|\) matrix where columns index "current state \(x\)" and rows index "destination state \(y\)".

Intuition: \(Q_t\) is an instantaneous jump rate

Intuition — tiny-time interpretation

In a tiny interval \([t, t+h]\) with \(h \to 0\):

\[P(X_{t+h} = y \mid X_t = x) \approx Q_t(y \mid x) \cdot h \quad \text{for } y \neq x\]

So \(Q_t(y \mid x)\) is the rate (probability per unit time) of jumping from \(x\) to \(y\). A higher value means the chain moves between those states more rapidly.

Compare to a continuous SDE: \(dx_t = f_t(x_t)\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t\). The drift \(f_t\) tells you the direction of movement; \(Q_t\) tells you the rates of jumping. Both are the "engine" that drives the process.

The discrete Euler update

Recall the Euler step for an ODE (Part 1): \(x_{t+h} \approx x_t + h \cdot f_t(x_t)\). In the CTMC world, the analogous update acts on the distribution \(p_t\) (a row vector of length \(|\mathcal{S}|\)):

\[p_{t+h} \approx p_t \,(I + h\, Q_t)\]

This is exactly a first-order Euler step applied to the distribution. The matrix \((I + h Q_t)\) is a valid (approximate) transition matrix for small \(h\): diagonal entries are close to 1 (stay put with high prob), off-diagonal entries are \(h \cdot Q_t(y \mid x) \geq 0\) (small probability of jumping). This is called the tau-leaping approximation in the literature.

Theorem 33 — Existence and Uniqueness (MIT notes §7.1)

Theorem 33 (CTMC existence & uniqueness)

For any rate matrix \(Q_t\) that is bounded and continuous in time, there exists a unique CTMC \(X_t\) (i.e., a unique set of transition probabilities \(p_{t+h|t}(y \mid x)\)) consistent with \(Q_t\).

Connection to Parts 1–2

This is the exact discrete analogue of the ODE/SDE existence and uniqueness theorems (Theorem 3 / Theorem 5 in the MIT notes). The message is the same: specifying the "engine" (vector field or rate matrix) is enough to uniquely determine the process. For machine learning, this means: train a neural network to output a rate matrix \(Q_t^\theta\), and you have implicitly defined a generative model.

Example 34 — Two-State CTMC (the discrete OU process)

Let \(\mathcal{S} = \{a, b\}\) and let the chain jump between the two states at a constant rate \(\lambda > 0\). The rate matrix is:

\[Q = \begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & \lambda \\ \lambda & -\lambda \end{pmatrix}\]

Reading the columns: column \(a\) says "if you are at \(a\), you jump to \(b\) at rate \(\lambda\) and the diagonal \(-\lambda\) ensures the row sums to zero." Column \(b\) is symmetric.

The resulting transition probabilities over a time increment \(h\) are:

\[p_{t+h|t}(a \mid a) = p_{t+h|t}(b \mid b) = \frac{1 + e^{-2\lambda h}}{2}\] \[p_{t+h|t}(b \mid a) = p_{t+h|t}(a \mid b) = \frac{1 - e^{-2\lambda h}}{2}\]

Let's trace what happens to the distribution. Start with \(p_0 = (1, 0)\) (all mass on \(a\)):

\[p_t(a) = \frac{1 + e^{-2\lambda t}}{2}, \quad p_t(b) = \frac{1 - e^{-2\lambda t}}{2}\]

As \(t \to \infty\): \(e^{-2\lambda t} \to 0\), so \(p_\infty = (1/2, 1/2)\) — the uniform (stationary) distribution. The chain forgets where it started.

Connection — discrete OU process

Compare to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process from Part 2/8: it starts at some \(x_0 \in \mathbb{R}\) and drifts toward \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) exponentially fast. The two-state CTMC does the same thing in a two-element set, drifting toward the uniform distribution. Higher \(\lambda\) = faster forgetting = more noise at any given \(t\). Same role as \(\sigma\) in the SDE.

14.3 Factorized CTMCs and Algorithm 7

Why factorization is necessary

The state space \(\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{V}^d\) has size \(V^d\). For GPT-scale language (\(V \approx 50000\), \(d \approx 1024\)), storing even a single column of \(Q_t\) — which has \(V^d\) entries — is physically impossible. We need a sparsity constraint.

Intuition — factorization = one token jumps at a time

The key insight: at most one token changes per jump. If the sequence is \(x = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)\), only transitions to "neighbors" \(\mathcal{N}(x)\) — sequences that differ in exactly one position — have nonzero rate. This is analogous to treating each coordinate's noise independently in a Gaussian diffusion model. The total number of nonzero rates per state is \(d \times (V-1)\) — linear in \(d\) and \(V\), not exponential.

Factorized rate matrix (MIT notes §7.1)

A CTMC model is factorized if for all \(x, y \in \mathcal{S}\):

\[Q_t(y \mid x) = 0 \quad \text{if } y \notin \mathcal{N}(x)\]

where \(\mathcal{N}(x)\) = sequences differing from \(x\) in at most one position. For neighbors \(y\) that differ from \(x\) only at position \(j\) (i.e., \(y_j = v_i \neq x_j\), all other positions equal), we write:

\[Q_t(y \mid x) = Q_t^{(j)}(v_i \mid x)\]

The per-position rate matrix satisfies \(\sum_{v_i \in \mathcal{V}} Q_t^{(j)}(v_i \mid x_j) = 0\) for each \(j\). A transformer with output shape \([d, V]\) can directly parameterize this — one row per position, one column per vocabulary token.

Algorithm 7 — Sampling from a Factorized CTMC

Algorithm 7 (MIT notes §7.1) — tau-leaping sampler

Require: trained rate network \(Q_t^\theta\) (factorized), initial distribution \(p_\text{init}\), number of steps \(n\).
Set \(t \leftarrow 0\), step size \(h \leftarrow 1/n\).

  1. Draw \(X_0 \sim p_\text{init}\), so \(X_0 = (X_0^{(1)}, \ldots, X_0^{(d)}) \in \mathcal{V}^d\).
  2. For \(i = 1, \ldots, n\):
  3.    Query the network: compute per-position jump rates \(\{q_j(v)\}_{j, v} \leftarrow Q_t^\theta(\cdot \mid X_t)\).
  4.    For each position \(j = 1, \ldots, d\) in parallel:
  5.       Let \(x = X_t^{(j)}\) (current token at position \(j\)).
  6.       Build per-position Euler transition probabilities:
    \[\tilde{p}_{j,t}(v \mid x) = \begin{cases} h \cdot Q_t^{(j)}(v \mid x) & v \neq x \\ 1 + h \cdot Q_t^{(j)}(x \mid x) & v = x \end{cases}\]
  7.       Sample \(X_{t+h}^{(j)} \sim \text{Categorical}\bigl(\{\tilde{p}_{j,t}(v \mid x)\}_{v \in \mathcal{V}}\bigr)\).
  8.    Set \(t \leftarrow t + h\).
  9. Return \(X_1\).

This is the discrete analogue of the Euler–Maruyama step. Each token is updated independently (the factorization), with a small (\(O(h^2)\)) probability that two tokens jump simultaneously — acceptable for small \(h\).

PyTorch: Two-state CTMC converging to uniform

# teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG
# Simulate the two-state CTMC from Example 34.
# We evolve the distribution p_t using the discrete Euler step:
#   p_{t+h} = p_t @ (I + h * Q)

import torch

lambda_ = 2.0            # jump rate
# Rate matrix Q, shape [2, 2]
# Q[y, x] = rate of jumping from state x to state y
# (column = source state, row = destination state)
Q = torch.tensor([
    [-lambda_,  lambda_],   # row a: jump INTO a from each source
    [ lambda_, -lambda_],   # row b: jump INTO b from each source
])   # shape [2, 2]

# p is a ROW vector: p[i] = P(X_t = state_i)
p = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.0]])   # start at state a, shape [1, 2]

h   = 0.001   # small step size
n   = 1000    # total steps  (simulates t = 0 -> 1.0)
I   = torch.eye(2)

print_times = {0, 100, 300, 600, 1000}

for step in range(n + 1):
    if step in print_times:
        t = step * h
        print(f"t={t:.3f}  p(a)={p[0,0]:.4f}  p(b)={p[0,1]:.4f}")
    if step < n:
        # Euler step on the distribution (tau-leaping in distribution space)
        p = p @ (I + h * Q)   # shape [1, 2] @ [2, 2] = [1, 2]

# Expected output (approximate):
# t=0.000  p(a)=1.0000  p(b)=0.0000
# t=0.100  p(a)=0.8187  p(b)=0.1813   (still remembers it started at a)
# t=0.300  p(a)=0.6703  p(b)=0.3297
# t=0.600  p(a)=0.5878  p(b)=0.4122
# t=1.000  p(a)=0.5084  p(b)=0.4916   (nearly uniform — converged)
# At t->inf: p = [0.5, 0.5]  (stationary / uniform distribution)

14.4 Training CTMC Models (§7.2)

The MIT notes describe the same three-step recipe used in flow matching (Part 10), now applied to discrete state space:

  1. Construct a probability path from noise to data.
  2. Derive a conditional rate matrix and the marginal rate matrix via the marginalization trick.
  3. Learn the marginal rate matrix in a simulation-free way (cross-entropy loss).

14.4.1 Conditional and Marginal Probability Path (§7.2.1)

Let \(z \in \mathcal{S}\) be a fixed clean data point (a "conditioning" anchor, analogous to \(z\) in the continuous conditional flow matching of Part 10). A conditional probability path is a family of distributions \(p_t(\cdot \mid z)\) over \(\mathcal{S}\) satisfying:

\[p_0(\cdot \mid z) = p_\text{init} \quad \text{(pure noise, independent of }z\text{)}\] \[p_1(\cdot \mid z) = \delta_z \quad \text{(all mass on the clean data point }z\text{)}\]

where \(\delta_z(x) = 1\) if \(x = z\), else \(0\). The marginal probability path averages over all clean data:

\[p_t(x) = \sum_{z \in \mathcal{S}} p_t(x \mid z)\, p_\text{data}(z)\]

At the endpoints: \(p_0 = p_\text{init}\) (pure noise) and \(p_1 = p_\text{data}\) (the data distribution). This is identical in structure to the continuous case (Part 10), just with an integral replaced by a sum.

Example 35 — Factorized mixture path

Fix a monotone schedule \(\kappa_t \in [0,1]\) with \(\kappa_0 = 0\), \(\kappa_1 = 1\). The factorized mixture path corrupts each token independently:

\[p_t(x \mid z) = \prod_{j=1}^d \bigl[\kappa_t\,\delta_{z_j}(x_j) + (1-\kappa_t)\,p_\text{init}^{(j)}(x_j)\bigr]\]

Equivalently, to sample \(x \sim p_t(\cdot \mid z)\):

  • For each position \(j\), flip a coin: with prob \(\kappa_t\) keep the clean token (\(x_j = z_j\)); with prob \(1-\kappa_t\) draw a noise token \(x_j \sim p_\text{init}^{(j)}\).

At \(t=0\): \(\kappa_0=0\) so all tokens are noise. At \(t=1\): \(\kappa_1=1\) so \(x = z\). The information is progressively revealed as \(t\) increases from 0 to 1.

Key difference from Gaussian paths: there is no notion of "direction" here. Probability mass does not flow continuously — it teleports. Token \(j\) either has the clean value or a noise value, with no interpolation in between.

14.4.2 Conditional and Marginal Rate Matrix (§7.2.2)

A conditional rate matrix \(Q_t^z(y \mid x)\) is one whose CTMC "follows" the conditional path \(p_t(\cdot \mid z)\). Formally, the CTMC with rate \(Q_t^z\) and initial distribution \(p_0(\cdot \mid z)\) has marginal distribution \(p_t(\cdot \mid z)\) at every time \(t\).

Given the conditional rate matrices for each \(z\), we want to build the marginal rate matrix that drives the marginal path \(p_t\). This is exactly the marginalization trick.

Theorem 36 — Discrete Marginalization Trick (MIT notes §7.2.2)

The marginal rate matrix defined by

\[Q_t(y \mid x) = \sum_{z \in \mathcal{S}} Q_t^z(y \mid x)\, \frac{p_t(x \mid z)\, p_\text{data}(z)}{p_t(x)}\]

is a valid rate matrix, and its CTMC follows the marginal probability path \(p_t\). In particular, \(X_1 \sim p_\text{data}\) — the model generates real data.

The fraction \(\frac{p_t(x \mid z)\, p_\text{data}(z)}{p_t(x)} = p_{1|t}(z \mid x)\) is the posterior: "given that the current noisy sequence is \(x\), what is the probability the original clean sequence was \(z\)?"

So the marginal rate matrix is a posterior-weighted average of conditional rate matrices:

\[\boxed{Q_t(y \mid x) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[Q_t^z(y \mid x) \;\big|\; X_t = x\bigr]}\]
Connection — this IS the flow-matching marginalization trick

In continuous flow matching (Part 10), the marginal vector field is:

\[u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[u_t^z(x) \mid X_t = x\bigr]\]

The structure is identical: marginal object = conditional expectation of the conditional object, conditioned on the current state. The only difference is that \(u_t\) is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(Q_t\) is a rate matrix on \(\mathcal{S}\). Same recipe, different state space.

Proposition 2 — Kolmogorov Forward Equation

Proposition 2 (KFE, MIT notes §7.2.2)

Let \(Q_t\) be the rate matrix of a CTMC. The distribution \(p_t\) is the marginal of this CTMC if and only if it satisfies the Kolmogorov Forward Equation (KFE):

\[\frac{d}{dt} p_t(x) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{S}} p_t(y)\, Q_t(x \mid y)\]

In matrix form (with \(p_t\) as a row vector):

\[\frac{d}{dt} p_t = p_t\, Q_t\]

This is a linear ODE over \(\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\), and its solution is unique by the ODE existence-uniqueness theorem (Theorem 3 in the notes) — which is precisely why Theorem 36's proof works.

Connection — KFE is the discrete Fokker–Planck / continuity equation

You have seen two continuous analogues of the KFE in this guide:

  • Part 8 (Fokker–Planck): \(\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (f_t p_t) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta p_t\) — governs the density of an SDE.
  • Part 10 (Continuity equation): \(\partial_t p_t + \nabla \cdot (u_t p_t) = 0\) — governs the density of an ODE flow.

The KFE \(\frac{d}{dt} p_t = p_t Q_t\) is the same equation in discrete space. All three say: "the rate matrix / vector field / drift determines how the distribution evolves." The key structural insight is that in all cases, the governing equation is linear in \(p_t\), which makes the marginalization trick possible.

14.4.3 Learning the Marginal Rate Matrix (§7.2.3)

Theorem 38 — Marginalization for factorized mixture path (MIT notes §7.2.3)

For the factorized mixture path (Example 35), the marginal rate matrix is also factorized and has the form: for position \(j\), token \(v_i \neq x_j\),

\[Q_t^{(j)}(v_i \mid x) = \dot{\kappa}_t\, \frac{p_{1|t}(z_j = v_i \mid x)}{1 - \kappa_t}\]

where \(\dot{\kappa}_t = \frac{d}{dt}\kappa_t\) is the schedule's derivative and \(p_{1|t}(z_j = v_i \mid x)\) is the posterior probability that the \(j\)-th clean token is \(v_i\), given the current noisy sequence \(x\).

Intuition — the rate matrix is just a posterior classifier

Look at the formula: the marginal rate matrix is entirely determined by \(p_{1|t}(z_j = v_i \mid x)\) — the per-token probability of the clean token. This is a classifier: "given the noisy sequence \(x\) at time \(t\), what is the clean token at position \(j\)?"

Training amounts to training a denoiser / classifier per position. No special CTMC machinery needed at training time — just cross-entropy. The CTMC structure only shows up during sampling (Algorithm 7).

Compare to score matching (Part 3): the score \(\nabla \log p_t(x)\) was the training target there; here the token posterior \(p_{1|t}(z_j \mid x)\) is the training target. Both are learned by regression to a conditional signal.

Algorithm 8 — Training (MIT notes §7.2.3)

Algorithm 8 — Training a factorized CTMC (Discrete Flow Matching)

Require: dataset \(\{z\} \sim p_\text{data}\), noise marginals \(p_\text{init}^{(j)}\), schedule \(\kappa_t\), posterior network \(f^\theta\) (e.g. a transformer), optimizer.

  1. Repeat for each training iteration:
  2.   Sample a clean sequence \(z \sim p_\text{data}\).
  3.   Sample time \(t \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]\), compute \(\kappa \leftarrow \kappa_t\).
  4.   For each position \(j = 1, \ldots, d\) in parallel: sample mask \(m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\kappa)\); sample noise \(\xi_j \sim p_\text{init}^{(j)}\); set \(x_j = m_j z_j + (1-m_j)\xi_j\).
  5.   Assemble noisy sequence \(x = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)\).
  6.   Run network: \(\hat{p}_{1|t}(\cdot \mid x) \leftarrow \text{softmax}(f^\theta(x, t))\), output shape \([d, V]\).
  7.   Compute Discrete Flow Matching loss (token-wise cross-entropy):
\[\mathcal{L}_\text{DFM}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^d \log \hat{p}_{1|t}(z_j \mid x)\]
  1.   Update: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DFM}(\theta)\).

Post-training: at inference, use Algorithm 7 with \(Q_t^\theta\) recovered from \(\hat{p}_{1|t}\) via Theorem 38.

Connection — Discrete Flow Matching = Continuous Flow Matching

In Part 10, the Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) loss was an MSE regression to the conditional vector field. Here, the Discrete Flow Matching loss is a cross-entropy regression to the conditional posterior. Both are simulation-free: you never need to run the full forward or reverse process during training — just sample \((z, t, x)\) and compute a local loss. Same framework, same elegance.

14.5 Example 39 — Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDLMs)

Intuition — BERT meets diffusion

The most practical and important special case: set \(p_\text{init} = \delta_{[\text{mask}]}\). That is, the "noise" distribution puts all mass on a special [MASK] token added to the vocabulary.

  • Forward process (corruption): each token is independently masked with probability \(1 - \kappa_t\). At \(t=0\), all tokens are masked. At \(t=1\), all tokens are clean.
  • Reverse process (generation): start from all-[MASK], run Algorithm 7, and the network progressively unmasked tokens over \(n\) steps.
  • Training target: given a partially masked sequence \(x\) and time \(t\), predict the clean token \(z_j\) at each masked position — standard cross-entropy, exactly like BERT's masked language modeling (MLM) objective.

The key upgrade over BERT: BERT is a discriminative model trained once with random masking and never generates sequences. MDLM is a generative model with a principled sampling schedule — it can generate full sequences by iterative unmasking, in parallel across all positions, in a fixed number of steps.

MDLM setup (MIT notes Example 39)

Extend vocabulary: \(\mathcal{V}' = \mathcal{V} \cup \{[\text{mask}]\}\). Set \(p_\text{init} = \delta_{[\text{mask}]^d}\) (sequence of all mask tokens). The factorized mixture path with this noise distribution gives:

\[x_j = \begin{cases} z_j & \text{with prob } \kappa_t \\ [\text{mask}] & \text{with prob } 1 - \kappa_t \end{cases}\]

The network \(f^\theta\) takes a partially masked sequence \(x\) and time \(t\) and outputs logits of shape \([d, V]\) — a predicted clean token distribution per position. Loss = cross-entropy over masked positions.

During generation: start with \(x = [\text{mask}]^d\), run Algorithm 7 for \(n\) steps. The rate matrix (from Theorem 38) ensures that each step unmasking a token is done according to the learned posterior — not greedily, but stochastically with the correct marginal.

Connection — MDLM vs autoregressive LMs

Autoregressive (GPT-style): generates left-to-right, one token at a time, \(O(d)\) sequential steps. Highly expressive but inherently sequential.

MDLM: generates all positions in parallel, with \(n\) refinement steps (typically \(n \ll d\)). Potentially much faster at inference. Each step can update many tokens simultaneously (factorized rate matrix). State-of-the-art MDLMs (e.g. SEDD, MDLM — Masked Diffusion Language Models by Sahoo et al. 2024, and related work) approach GPT-class perplexity on language benchmarks, making this a serious competitor for language generation.

The tradeoff: autoregressive models have an exact likelihood; MDLMs require ELBO-style bounds. But the parallel generation and controllable refinement make them attractive for many applications (protein design, code generation, constrained text generation).

PyTorch: Masked diffusion training pair

# teaching code, not security-sensitive RNG
# Illustrate one training step of a Masked Diffusion Language Model.
# We build a (clean, masked) pair (z, x) that the model would learn to invert.

import torch

# Vocabulary: 5 real tokens + 1 [MASK] token (index 5)
V    = 5
MASK = 5          # special mask token index
d    = 8          # sequence length

torch.manual_seed(42)   # reproducibility for teaching

# Clean sequence z  shape [d]  — a "sentence" from the dataset
z = torch.randint(0, V, (d,))
print(f"Clean sequence z:   {z.tolist()}  shape {list(z.shape)}")
# e.g. [0, 3, 4, 1, 2, 0, 3, 2]

# Sample a random time t in [0, 1]
t = torch.rand(1).item()
kappa_t = t                  # linear schedule: kappa_t = t
print(f"Sampled time t={t:.3f},  kappa_t={kappa_t:.3f}")
# kappa_t = fraction of tokens KEPT clean

# Sample noisy sequence x via factorized mixture path
# Each token j is kept (x_j = z_j) with prob kappa_t,
# else replaced with [MASK] (x_j = MASK_INDEX)
mask_coin = torch.bernoulli(torch.full((d,), kappa_t))   # 1=keep, 0=mask  shape [d]
x = torch.where(mask_coin.bool(), z, torch.full((d,), MASK, dtype=torch.long))
print(f"Noisy  sequence x:  {x.tolist()}  shape {list(x.shape)}")
# e.g. [MASK, 3, MASK, 1, 2, MASK, 3, 2]  (some tokens replaced by 5)

# This (z, x) pair is the training example:
#   Input  to network f^theta: x (partially masked sequence), t
#   Target for network:        z (clean sequence)
# The loss is cross-entropy:  -sum_j log p(z_j | x, t)

# Simulate a toy "perfect predictor" returning one-hot logits over V tokens
# (in practice, this is the transformer output, shape [d, V])
perfect_logits = torch.zeros(d, V)
for j in range(d):
    perfect_logits[j, z[j]] = 10.0   # very confident about the true token
loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(perfect_logits, z)
print(f"Training loss (perfect predictor): {loss.item():.4f}")
# Expected output: loss near 0.0  (perfect predictor)

#   z:              [d]       = [8]     clean sequence
#   x:              [d]       = [8]     noisy (partially masked) sequence
#   mask_coin:      [d]       = [8]     Bernoulli mask (1=keep, 0=mask)
#   perfect_logits: [d, V]    = [8, 5]  network output logits per token position

14.6 Remark 40 — Generator Matching

Remark 40 (MIT notes) — Everything is one family

Why did the CTMC recipe look so familiar? Because flow matching, diffusion, and CTMC training are all instances of a single unifying framework called Generator Matching (Holderrieth et al., 2024 [19]).

A generator is the mathematical object that drives any Markov process: vector fields drive ODEs, drifts/diffusion coefficients drive SDEs, rate matrices drive CTMCs. The generator matching loss — "regress the marginal generator to the conditional generator, using the posterior as weights" — is the one principle that produces CFM, score matching, and Discrete Flow Matching as special cases.

The framework extends further: smooth manifolds (protein backbone angles), mixed continuous-discrete spaces (joint text and image generation), and general jump processes all fit the same recipe. Everything in this guide is one family.

Common confusion — time direction convention

The MIT notes (§7) use the convention \(t=0\) = noise and \(t=1\) = clean data, which is the opposite of DDPM (where \(t=0\) is clean). This matches the flow-matching convention used in Parts 10–11. Keep track: when you read \(p_\text{init}\), that is the \(t=0\) noise distribution, and \(p_\text{data}\) is the \(t=1\) target. Algorithm 7 runs from \(t=0\) to \(t=1\) — it starts from noise and generates data.
